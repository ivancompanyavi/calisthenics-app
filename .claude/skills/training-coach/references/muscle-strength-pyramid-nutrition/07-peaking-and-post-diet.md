# MSP Nutrition — Peaking for Competition & Post-Diet Recovery

> **Source:** *The Muscle & Strength Pyramid: Nutrition* 3e (Helms/Valdez/Morgan), book pp.203-258. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/msp-nutrition.txt` or PDF pp.203-258.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] research-established · [CONVENTION] the authors' specific evidence-based recommendation · [DISPUTED] contested / authors flag uncertainty.
> **COMPETITION-ONLY FLAG:** Most of this unit (physique peak week, carb-loading protocols, powerlifting/weightlifting making-weight) applies only to competitors preparing for a specific event/weigh-in. It has no direct application to a non-competing lifter's general training. Condensed heavily per scope; only the transferable mechanics (reverse-dieting/recovery-diet logic ending a diet, and the caution around aggressive short-term weight manipulation) are covered in more depth.

## Introduction & scope (p.203)
Two peaking contexts, both COMPETITION-ONLY:
1. **Peak week for physique competitors** — timing/manipulating training & nutrition for maximum stage appearance.
2. **Making weight for strength athletes** — whether to cut weight for a weight class at all, and how to do it without sacrificing performance.
[CONVENTION] The book explicitly frames strength-sport tapering (training side) as covered in the companion Training Pyramid book, not here.

## Peaking for physique competition — condensed (p.204-221) — COMPETITION-ONLY

**Foundational truth (p.205):** [CONVENTION] ≥90% of final stage appearance comes from years of training/dieting to get lean, not peak-week manipulation. A competitor who peaks perfectly but isn't lean loses to a shredded competitor who peaks moderately. Peak week can help modestly if done well, but risks making things worse if done poorly — many traditional peak-week practices (restricting sodium/water to "dry out," raising potassium) lack physiological basis in natural (non-PED) athletes and more often backfire than help [DISPUTED as folk practice, refuted by authors].

**Carbohydrate loading mechanics (p.205-209):**
- [SCIENCE] Carb-loading after a period of lower-carb intake/glycogen-depleting training can push muscle glycogen above baseline ("supercompensation"); glycogen is stored with intramuscular water contributing to visual fullness.
- [SCIENCE] Endurance-athlete loading protocols use up to 12 g/kg (5-6 g/lb) — inappropriate for physique athletes, who risk "spilling over": excess carb/water spilling into subcutaneous space, blurring definition (mechanism plausible, not fully confirmed) (p.205-206). Distinguish true spillover from temporary "soft" look during glycogen storage that resolves in 12-24h.
- [CONVENTION] Physique competitors typically eat only 1-3 g/kg carbs on non-refeed days late in prep and have reduced glycogen storage capacity (chronic restriction + depletion training downregulates it) — so load size should be based on **recent dietary intake / refeed-day amounts**, not bodyweight formulas like 10-12 g/kg (p.206).
- 2024 review (Homer, Cross, Helms) [SCIENCE] key findings (p.207-208): carb loading acutely increases muscle size; dehydration acutely decreases it; loading while dehydrated stores much less intramuscular fluid than loading while hydrated; muscle damage impairs glycogen storage; targeted depletion training can cause muscle-specific supercompensation but isn't required; most competitors are already partially glycogen-depleted by end of prep; supercompensated glycogen is stable for days once exercise stops; high carb intake relative to habitual level + stress can cause GI distress; low-carb/low-sodium diets downregulate gut carb transporters and can drop blood pressure (already lower during prep).
- **Evidence-based peaking guidelines** (p.208-209), [CONVENTION]: load 3-12 g/kg over 1-several days (individual/division-specific); rehearse loads 1-2 months out once lean to learn individual response; avoid drastic changes to carbs/water/electrolytes or new foods; don't go extremely low-carb early in peak week; avoid training that causes muscle damage (high volume, near-failure, long-muscle-length/eccentric work) during peak week; avoid extreme water/sodium restriction; manage stress (travel early, minimize changing variables); prioritize high-GI low-fiber carbs; get lean early (3-4 weeks out) and slowly raise carbs; a small pre-stage sodium bump + pump-up may help some divisions.

**Eating up into the show (p.209-211):** [CONVENTION] Rather than sprinting the deficit into the final weeks (physiologically/psychologically the worst time to intensify), reach stage leanness 3-4 weeks out, then raise carbs (and sometimes fat) ~10%/week while tapering cardio, provided appearance holds or improves; pull back if appearance backslides. Improves energy availability, digestion/glycogen capacity, metabolic/hormonal recovery, and can regain some lost muscle. Requires tighter macro tracking (±10 g of target) in this phase versus the flexible "calories + macro minimums" approach from Level 2.

**Front-load vs. back-load (p.211-215) — figure/template heavy, condensed:** Back-loading = carb increase in the final 1-2 days; used either (a) when not yet lean enough and still dieting to the wire, timing refeeds to align with the day the competitor visually looks best, or (b) as a simple "top-off" after already eating up. Front-loading = a two-phase carb increase over several days ("stretching the tank" then "filling it") exploiting the fact that stored glycogen is stable once training stops; gives more days to correct if load is off. **Hierarchy of approaches** (p.213-214), [CONVENTION]: (1) best — early readiness + eating up + a modest back load (low stress, predictable); (2) second-best — on-time readiness + a front load (time to correct errors); (3) least ideal — late readiness + skipping the prior week's refeeds then back-loading (high risk of unrecoverable flatness or spillover; usually backfires; needs an experienced coach). Peak-week templates, sodium dosing (~1 g/1000 kcal before stage, individually trialed), and meal-timing tables exist in the source (Tables 7.1-7.2, p.217-221) — competition-specific execution detail, not reproduced here; see PDF if needed.

**Training adjustments for peak week (p.215-217):** [CONVENTION] maintain or reduce volume ~10-20%; keep 1-2 RIR, no failure/depletion work; shift toward higher reps (8-20) if normally training heavier; stick to familiar exercises, avoid long-muscle-length/eccentric-heavy movements (e.g., RDLs, good mornings, deep DB flyes); include a light "pump-up" session (15-20 reps, RPE 5-6/4-5 RIR, 2-3 circuit rounds, ≤30 min) ~2 days out, and a brief bodyweight/band version on show day (skip legs on show day itself — posing already overloads them).

## Making weight for strength sports (p.221-233) — COMPETITION-ONLY, condensed

**Choosing a weight class (p.222-224):**
- [CONVENTION] Target the class where strength-to-weight ratio (coefficient: Dots/Wilks/Good Lift Points) is highest — not automatically the lowest reachable class or automatically a higher class because of height/frame assumptions.
- [SCIENCE] Muscle mass is one of the strongest predictors of powerlifting performance (multiple citations, p.223) — chronic under-eating to stay light limits strength potential.
- [CONVENTION] Novices/still-growing teenagers should **not** cut weight — cutting early impairs strength/size development and maturation; they should be allowed to grow into heavier classes naturally.
- [CONVENTION] Even mature lifters should be cautious about cutting purely to chase a better coefficient at a lower level of competition ("I'll be elite if I just drop a class" is usually false — the gap from good to elite is longer than expected). Despite this, survey data show >80% of powerlifters/weightlifters rapid-weight-cut, and 30-80% also gradually diet beforehand (p.223, refs 13-17) — described as widespread but often not strategically wise for non-elite competitors.
- [CONVENTION] If within 1-2% of the lower class, a minor 2-4 day water cut/low-residue diet is low-risk; avoid large or prolonged cuts while still developing as a lifter.
- [CONVENTION] Moving up a class: follow Level 1 weight-gain pace (**0.5-1.5% bodyweight/month**); e.g., 57→63 kg or 83→93 kg (~10% BW increase) should take close to a year, not be rushed. While gaining, track **relative** strength (coefficient), not just absolute — if coefficient isn't rising with bodyweight, the gain rate is too fast, training needs adjustment, or the heavier class may not suit the lifter's frame.

**Acute making-weight methods (p.225-230), 2-hour-weigh-in context (IPF/IWF-style; 24h weigh-ins tolerate far more aggressive cuts):**
- General rule [CONVENTION]: acute methods suit lifters **1-3% over** class (up to 4-5% for experienced/tolerant lifters) one week out. Losing >5% acutely is dangerous and very hard to recover from within a 2h window. In this final week the goal is *not* fat loss — it's food weight, gut content, and body water, followed by rehydration/refueling.
- **Gut cut** (lowest risk): <10 g fiber/day for 2-4 days pre-weigh-in, switch to low-residue/energy-dense foods (protein powder, chocolate, refined carbs) instead of fibrous produce/whole grains/lean meats → ~1-2% BW loss without dehydration (p.225-226) [SCIENCE, cited].
- **Body water reduction**: additional ~1-3% BW safely losable via mild dehydration. [SCIENCE] 2% body-water loss impairs aerobic/skill performance but strength/power are more resilient to that magnitude (p.226-227, ref 19); with proper rehydration most lifters recover within a 2h window. Methods, least-to-most extreme: modest fluid/sodium restriction in final 12-24h; spitting (saliva loss via gum/sour candy); **water loading** (days of high fluid intake before restriction — produces slightly greater losses because urinary output stays elevated during the restriction phase, ref 20); 12-16h fast timed for a **morning** weigh-in (harder for afternoon/evening weigh-ins). Active/passive sweating (cardio in a hoodie, heat exposure) explicitly **not recommended** — unnecessary added stress.
- **Carb restriction**: carbs are 4 kcal/g vs. fat 9 kcal/g, so shifting toward higher-fat/lower-carb reduces food weight; also triggers mild diuresis via glycogen-bound water loss and lower insulin altering renal sodium handling (p.227, refs 18/21) [SCIENCE mechanism]. Caution: severe glycogen depletion can impair muscle contraction and cause fatigue/strength decrements (refs 22-24) [SCIENCE]. [CONVENTION] Taper gradually with isocaloric swaps — e.g., -45 g carb (-180 kcal) for +20 g fat (+180 kcal) per day, starting the Sunday before a Saturday meet; never drop below **1 g/kg** if habitual intake is 3-4 g/kg, or below **2 g/kg** if habitual intake exceeds 4 g/kg.
- **Combining methods** for a 3-5% cut [CONVENTION]: low-fiber/high-energy-density + carb restriction + water loading + fluid/sodium restriction + 12-16h fast, ordered from lowest to highest performance risk: (1) low-fiber/high-energy-density diet, (2) 12-16h fast + morning weigh-in, (3) water loading then fluid/sodium restriction, (4) carb restriction with isocaloric fat increase, (5) 12-16h fast for an afternoon/evening weigh-in, (6) heat/exercise-induced dehydration — **not recommended**. Trial the full combination in advance of competition.

**Rehydration after weigh-in (p.228-230):**
- Fluids/electrolytes take priority over solid food immediately post-weigh-in (food slows gastric emptying, delaying rehydration).
- [SCIENCE] Target ~**15 ml/kg** of a carb-electrolyte sports drink; don't exceed 1000 ml in the first 30 min; single-sitting fluid absorption threshold ~600-1000 ml before bloating risk (ref 26). Max single-source carb absorption ≈**60 g/hr**; dual-source (glucose+fructose) ≈**90 g/hr** (refs 30-31) — usually unnecessary to push this hard.
- 1 kg lost fluid ≈ 1 L to replace, but gastric-emptying rate paces how fast this can happen.
- If BW loss from dehydration exceeded ~1.5%, 15 ml/kg may not fully restore hydration before the opener — watch warm-up feel and consider a lighter opener if sluggish.
- Note (p.229): waking weight is typically ~1% lighter than bedtime weight (respiration/urination) — if only ~1% over class the night before a morning weigh-in, no further cut steps are usually needed.

## The Post-Diet — general population (non-competitors) (p.236-238)

[SCIENCE] 2017-2018 NHANES DXA data (n=3,658, ages 8-59): body-fat range in a representative US sample was **12.1%-56.1%** — nobody in the sample was at or below 12% BF, despite 8-12% (men) / 16-20% (women) often being portrayed on social media as an easily attainable, sustainable target. [CONVENTION] Social-media physiques reflect genetic outliers, selection bias, professional-level lifestyle investment, and frequently PED use — not a realistic benchmark.

**Signal that the diet target itself is the problem, not the method** [CONVENTION] (p.237): constant food preoccupation, repeated unintentional off-plan eating, or repeated past failure to reach/sustain the same target suggests the goal — not the approach — needs revising. Even a well-executed evidence-based diet cannot fix an inappropriate goal.

**Post-diet transition for non-competitors** [CONVENTION] (p.238):
- Brief diet / small weight loss → return to **95-100%** of previous maintenance intake.
- Substantial weight loss / long diet (but to a sustainable end-point) → use a conservative maintenance estimate; keep activity/step level similar (drop extra cardio); **increase intake by 200-400 kcal/day** to start, then taper up over several weeks until weight shows a slight, steady rise; hold there for maintenance goals or continue rising for a gaining phase.
- Numbers matter less than getting post-diet behavior/structure/habits right (deferred to the Behavior & Lifestyle chapter, out of scope here).

## The Post-Diet for competitors / reverse dieting vs. the Recovery Diet (p.238-244) — TRANSFERABLE MECHANICS, most detailed section

**Three post-show paths** [CONVENTION] (p.237-238): (1) unstructured eating → binge-driven rapid fat gain; (2) reverse dieting — slow, tapered calorie increase (sometimes staying in a slight deficit) to "build the metabolism" and minimize fat gain; (3) Recovery Diet — controlled surplus to regain a portion of lost fat quickly, then slow to normal offseason gain pace.

**Why reverse dieting underperforms its promise** [SCIENCE/CONVENTION mix] (p.239-241):
- Mechanism: normal downregulation from dieting (metabolic adaptation, low energy availability) reverses only by exiting the deficit and gaining weight/fat; leptin stays chronically low if leanness is maintained, blocking full recovery. "You can't stay shredded and fully reverse metabolic adaptation at the same time."
- Case-series evidence, [SCIENCE], all small-N observational:
  - Longstrom et al.: reverse-dieting participants showed only 2-5% BW increase at 8-10 weeks post-show, small fat-free-mass losses (-0.5 to -1 kg), negligible RMR change (-6% to +4%); across the full sample, fat-mass change correlated strongly with RMR change and BF% change correlated strongly with leptin change — i.e., **more fat regained → more leptin/RMR recovery**.
  - Pardue et al.: male bodybuilder adding only 10-30 g carb and/or 4-10 g fat + 5-10 min less cardio weekly took **5 months** to return to pre-diet body fat (13.8% DXA); anaerobic power, RMR, testosterone, thyroid hormone remained suppressed even then.
  - Halliday et al.: cautious figure competitor also took **5 months** to reach baseline BF (~15%, itself very low); energy availability stayed near/below the ~30 kcal/kg-lean-mass threshold for the first 10 weeks of recovery; **menstrual function did not return until 71 weeks post-contest**, only once BF was maintained at ~20% for months.
  - Tinsley et al.: faster, still-graded approach — ~1% BW/week gain (with ~8-9% weekly fat-mass increase) for 9 weeks after a 25-week, two-show prep — returned to baseline BW by week 9, **fully restored and exceeded** pre-last-show RMR and fat-free mass; menstrual cycle returned within 3 months; grip strength/anaerobic power still suppressed at week 9.
  - **Conclusion drawn by the authors**: the fastest-regaining approach [6] showed the most complete recovery in the shortest time; slower reverse-dieting approaches left athletes in a compromised state longer without delivering the promised leanness-preservation benefit.
- [SCIENCE] Reported consequences of physique-competition dieting (ref 7): sleep disruption, mood disturbance, lowered testosterone (men)/amenorrhea (women), reduced RMR and thyroid output, strength/lean-mass loss, food focus, hunger, irritability, elevated disordered-eating risk.

**The Recovery Diet protocol** (3D Muscle Journey; used with "hundreds of athletes") [CONVENTION] (p.241-248):
- Two defining principles vs. reverse dieting: (1) **end the deficit immediately** — no extended taper; (2) target an **appropriate rate of weight gain**, not "maximum food without gaining."
- **Scenario 1 — more shows upcoming:**
  - Next show ≤1 week away: continue dieting after peak week; a modest celebratory dinner post-show is fine, not a feast.
  - Next show 2-4 weeks away: one celebratory dinner OR breakfast (not both), kept modest (e.g., meat + steamed vegetables) given peak-week's already-elevated carbs/calories.
  - Next show 1-4 months away: both a celebratory dinner (add starchy carbs to the protein/veg base) and breakfast next morning; don't formally track these two meals but eat "like a health-conscious adult" (no multiple courses/dessert/appetizer stacking); resume macros right after.
- **Scenario 2 — season is over (next show ≥4 months out):** celebratory meal, then a full day of three normal-size, complete, untracked meals (breakfast/lunch/dinner, dessert allowed) the following day. If a binge happens, don't dwell on it — described as a normal occurrence after months of semi-starvation — resume the plan the next day.
- **Recovery duration & target** [CONVENTION] (p.244): recovery diet lasts **4-8 weeks**; target **5-10% above end-of-prep average weight** (not the lowest, depleted weigh-in number) by the end of that window. Example: 165 lb (75 kg) end-of-prep average → target ~173-181 lb (78.5-82.5 kg).
- **Mechanics** (p.244): eliminate formal cardio, return to habitual step count (≥~7000 steps/day guideline); run a **400-1,000 kcal/day surplus** (often meaning +600-1,500 kcal added to end-of-prep intake, since that intake was already a deficit) — use the lower end if a large post-show celebratory binge already added some fat. Continue until 5-10% above stage weight, ideally within 4-8 weeks; **target weight-gain rate ~1-2 lb (0.5-1 kg)/week** during this phase, faster than a normal muscle-gaining phase because intentional fat regain (not just muscle) is required for physiological normalization.
- After hitting the target: shift to the standard Level 1 weight-gain pace (**0.5-1.5% BW/month**).

**Fear of weight gain (p.246):** [CONVENTION] normal psychological reaction; extreme leanness itself degrades energy, muscle fullness, immune resilience, injury risk, and training motivation — regained body fat and calories restore energy/mood/libido/hormonal function/sleep. Plan the recovery diet a couple of weeks before the season ends to prepare mentally for the prep→recovery→offseason transition.

**Transitioning tracking rigor (p.246-248):** [CONVENTION] Precision that's necessary in prep (small calorie/output changes have big effects when margins are thin) becomes unnecessary and even counterproductive once fat, glycogen, hormones, and recuperative capacity are restored. Progressively loosen tracking: estimate portions instead of weighing, use post-meal appetite ratings (see Level 1), relearn hunger/satiety cues (distinguish craving from true hunger — a trainable skill), then let bodyweight trend become the primary/only tracked variable once macros are reliably above minimums; audit specific weak variables (e.g., protein, produce) periodically rather than tracking everything. Advanced/experienced athletes with a slow target gain rate may only need to weigh in 1-2x/week, viewed over 1-2 month trends.

**In summary — recovery diet checklist** (p.248-249): celebratory meal → (if season over) one untracked full day of 3 meals → surplus targeting **~1-2 lb (0.5-1 kg)/week** gain until 5-10% above stage weight within 4-8 weeks → step down to normal-lifter gain pace (0.5-1.5% BW/month) → progressively shift tracking toward internally-cued/qualitative habits. [CONVENTION] These are guidelines, not hard rules — competition schedule, career constraints (e.g., fitness-model work), and individual variance can require adjusting the specific numbers; the underlying principles (exit the deficit fully, regain fat deliberately, don't over-extend leanness) are what matter.

## Nutrition periodization for physique competitors (p.249-255) — COMPETITION-ONLY, condensed

Two-year cycle example (figure, p.251 — see PDF for chart): ~6-month prep, ~3-month recovery window (though the Recovery Diet itself is only 4-8 weeks; the extended window reflects full psychological/physiological normalization time), ~15-month offseason containing two 1-month mini-cuts and a 2-month pre-prep phase.

[CONVENTION] Recommendation: compete **no more often than every other season** (except highly experienced competitors) — full recovery to baseline muscle typically leaves only ~3 months to gain before prepping again otherwise, too little for productive muscle gain or full food-relationship recovery.

**Phase characteristics** (p.252-255):
- **Prep**: falling body fat/calories, rising training stress, rising cardio, increasing reliance on external cues (tracking, weigh-ins) as hunger/satiety signals become unreliable.
  - *Diet breaks*: every **4-8 weeks** during prep (or autoregulated to plateaus), **1-2 weeks at ~maintenance** — mitigates hormonal/metabolic adaptation, improves training quality, aids muscle regain, provides mental relief; experienced competitors may run these untracked, eating to internal maintenance cues.
  - *Eating up*: ideally reaching stage condition 2-4 weeks out, then small incremental carb/fat increases + cardio decreases for glycogen storage, muscle regain, and a more predictable, lower-stress peak.
  - *Peak week(s)*: front/back/mid load per athlete history (see peaking section above).
- **Recovery**: training stress ~unchanged; energy intake and body fat rise while cardio drops; muscle regained, hormonal balance (incl. menses) restored, performance improves, food preoccupation fades; tracking shifts from strict toward internally cued.
- **Offseason**: once ≥5% over stage weight and fully recovered (hunger/satiety, mood, energy, libido, menses, performance, fullness, sleep all at baseline), reduce intake to the Level 1 muscle-gain rate; minimal cardio; increasingly internally-cued eating.
  - *Mini cuts*: to cap offseason fat gain, run brief mini cuts at up to a **4:1 ratio of surplus-time to cut-time** (e.g., 4 months surplus : 1 month cut); more aggressive than normal fat loss, up to **~1% BW/week**; light tracking (weight, protein, calories) for early-career competitors; slightly reduced training stress, some cardio added.
- **Pre-prep**: final ~4-12 weeks before prep, after the last mini cut — maintenance calories, pushing intake as high as possible without regaining fat, and re-habituating to tracking discipline ahead of prep.

[CONVENTION] Long-term goal for veteran competitors: make prep and offseason lifestyles *qualitatively* similar (consistent meal rhythm, training schedule, year-round activity) so that only *quantitative* variables (calories, cardio volume, step count) shift between phases — this is what enables experienced athletes to autoregulate via internal cues year-round rather than needing an externally-guided/internally-guided phase switch each cycle. This skill takes years and only develops through intentional practice with internally-cued regulation.
