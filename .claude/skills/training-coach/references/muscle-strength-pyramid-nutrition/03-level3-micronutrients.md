# MSP Nutrition — Level 3 — Micronutrients

> **Source:** *The Muscle & Strength Pyramid: Nutrition* 3e (Helms/Valdez/Morgan), book pp.95-118. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/msp-nutrition.txt` or PDF pp.95-118.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] research-established · [CONVENTION] the authors' specific evidence-based recommendation · [DISPUTED] contested / authors flag uncertainty.

## Micronutrient classes (p.96)
- [SCIENCE] Micronutrients = minerals (inorganic, from soil/water) + vitamins (organic, from plants/animals). Vitamins: convert food to energy, protect cellular integrity, maintain organ function, support immunity, enable tissue repair and blood clotting (p.96).
- [SCIENCE] Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) absorbed with dietary fat, stored in adipose tissue/liver → no need for consistent daily intake, but can accumulate to harmful levels in excess (p.96).
- [SCIENCE] Water-soluble vitamins (8 B-vitamin forms + C) are not stored, excreted via sweat/urine → lower excess-toxicity risk but require more regular consumption (p.96).
- [SCIENCE] Minerals: macrominerals needed in higher amounts (calcium, phosphorus, potassium, sulfur, sodium, chlorine, magnesium); trace minerals in smaller amounts but equally essential (iron, zinc, selenium, iodine, copper, cobalt, molybdenum, etc.) (p.96). Roles: fluid regulation, energy production, blood pressure/blood sugar regulation, immune function, bone health, neuromuscular function.
- [SCIENCE] Electrolytes = the subset of minerals that carry electrical charge in bodily fluids (not all minerals are electrolytes). Roles: nerve function, heart/muscle contraction, cellular fluid balance, nutrient delivery, waste removal (p.96).

## Ensuring adequate micronutrient intake (p.97-99)
- [SCIENCE] Micronutrient guidance is less certain/specific than macronutrient guidance because: many micronutrients vs. 3 macros; consumed in small quantities; food micronutrient content varies with farming practices, processing, and environment, whereas macronutrient content is relatively consistent (p.97). This makes micronutrients harder to study than macronutrients for performance/body-composition outcomes.
- [CONVENTION] Because dose-response data for individual micronutrients is often lacking, the practical strategy is to follow broad healthy-eating guidelines (which embed micronutrient adequacy) rather than target specific mg/mcg amounts per nutrient (p.97).
- [CONVENTION] Recommended dietary mindset: "inclusive vs. exclusive" — focus on regularly including a variety of nutrient-rich foods rather than following restrictive fad-diet rules; many fad diets that appear varied are actually micronutrient-deficient on analysis (p.97).
- [SCIENCE] US, UK, WHO, and independent researcher-derived guidelines are all evidence-based (systematic literature review, public/transparent process) and converge on similar conclusions despite independent development (p.98-99). ~80-90% of Americans fail to meet US dietary guidelines (p.99).

### US Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2020-2025 (p.99)
- [CONVENTION] Core premise: meet nutrient needs via nutrient-dense foods rich in vitamins/minerals, minimizing added sugar, saturated fat, sodium — an inclusionary, whole-diet approach rather than per-nutrient mg targets or blanket food restriction (p.99).
- [CONVENTION] Food groups to include (p.99):
  1. Vegetables of all types — dark green, red/orange, beans/peas/lentils, starchy, other.
  2. Fruits, especially whole fruits.
  3. Grains, ≥half whole grain.
  4. Dairy — fat-free/low-fat milk, yogurt, cheese; lactose-free or fortified soy alternatives.
  5. Protein foods — lean meats, poultry, eggs, seafood, beans/peas/lentils, nuts/seeds/soy.
  6. Oils — vegetable oils and oils naturally in foods (seafood, nuts).
- [CONVENTION] Limits, not full exclusion (p.99):
  1. Added sugars: <10% of daily calories.
  2. Saturated fat: <10% of daily calories.
  3. Sodium: <2300 mg/day (book flags this may be insufficient for sweating exercisers — see Sodium section below).
  4. Alcohol: abstain, or ≤2 drinks/day (men), ≤1 drink/day (women).

### UK Eatwell Guide (p.99-100)
- [CONVENTION] ≥5 portions/day of varied fruit and vegetables.
- [CONVENTION] Base meals on potatoes/bread/rice/pasta/starchy carbs, favoring whole-grain.
- [CONVENTION] Include dairy or dairy alternatives, lower-fat/lower-sugar options.
- [CONVENTION] Include beans, lentils, peas, fish, eggs, meat, other proteins — 2 portions of fish/week, one oily.
- [CONVENTION] Choose unsaturated oils/spreads, small amounts.
- [CONVENTION] Fluid: 6-8 cups/glasses per day.
- [CONVENTION] High-fat/salt/sugar foods/drinks: less often, small amounts.
- [SCIENCE] Note on precision: most food-group advice uses qualitative terms ("eat some," "choose," "base meals on") rather than numeric targets, because observational data support benefits but controlled dose-response trials are often lacking — precise numeric guidance would outrun the evidence (p.100).

### WHO 2020 Healthy Diet Indicator (p.100-102)
- [SCIENCE] For health-promoting foods lacking an established dose-response curve: recommendation is simply ">0 g/day" (some is better than none, exact amount undetermined). For foods with negative health associations (e.g., processed meat): "0 g/day" — not because trace amounts are proven harmful, but because no safe threshold has been established (p.101). Occasional small intake of any food is unlikely to matter meaningfully; the emphasis is overall pattern, not zero-tolerance avoidance (p.101).

**Table 3.1 — Healthy Diet Indicator Criteria (2020)** (p.101)

| Dietary Element | Criteria for scoring (quantitative daily intake) |
|---|---|
| Fruits, vegetables | ≥400 g |
| Beans and other legumes | >0 g |
| Nuts and seeds | >0 g |
| Whole grains | >0 g |
| Dietary fiber | >25 g |
| Total fat | <30% total energy |
| Saturated fat | <10% total energy |
| Dietary sodium | <2 g sodium |

| Unprocessed red meat | <71 g |

### The Athlete's Plate® (p.102-104)
- [SCIENCE] Visual nutrition tool developed by Meyer et al. (Univ. of Colorado Colorado Springs SWELL Sport Nutrition Program) with the US Olympic Committee, reviewed by USOC Sport Dietitians, empirically validated against international sports-nutrition recommendations, published in IJSNEM 2019 (p.102-103).
- [CONVENTION] Adapts fat allotment by training load: Easy training = 1 tsp fats; Moderate training = 1 Tbsp fats; Hard training/race day = 2 Tbsp fats (figures — see PDF pp.103-104).
- [CONVENTION] Easy-training plate is modified for weight management by shifting some energy from carbohydrate to protein, to support satiety and muscle preservation during a deficit (p.104).
- [CONVENTION] Vegan/vegetarian versions validated separately (p.105-106); these versions flag: consider Vitamin D, B12, and Zinc supplementation; consume ≥1 serving of fermented food/drink per day; consume protein-rich snacks throughout the day (figures — see PDF pp.105-106).
- [CONVENTION] All plate guidance must be individualized for energy budget/expenditure, logistical/financial constraints, personal preference, clinical needs, and cultural/ethical/ethnic/religious dietary practices (p.106).

## Fiber intake recommendations (p.107-110)
- [SCIENCE] Fiber is not classed as a micronutrient but co-occurs with micronutrient-dense foods and has independent benefits: reduces disease risk, enhances satiety (helps prevent excess fat gain), supports digestive health (p.107). Fiber = carbohydrate subcategory that is indigestible or only partially digestible.
- [SCIENCE] Traditional classification (soluble/insoluble) is outdated; modern classification is by fermentability: non-fermentable vs. microbiota-accessible carbohydrates (MACs) (p.107-108). Fermentability varies by individual gut microbiota (e.g., lactose is not normally an MAC, but becomes one in lactose-intolerant individuals lacking lactase) (p.107-108).
- [SCIENCE] Fiber fermentation categories (Mancin, Burke & Rollo 2025 review) (p.108, Figure 3.7 — see PDF p.108 for full table):
  - High fermentation (onion, garlic, artichoke, wheat, rye, peas, chickpeas, lentils): increases beneficial bacteria (e.g., bifidobacteria), maintains microbiome stability, most short-chain-fatty-acid energy yield; downside — more gas/bloating/distention/pain.
  - Moderate fermentation (cooked vegetables, potatoes, corn, rice, pasta, oats, eggplant, bean, broccoli, spinach, grapes, strawberries, blueberries, raisins, brown rice, quinoa, flaxseed): may increase bacterial diversity, some SCFA energy, less gas/bloating than high-fermentation sources.
  - Low/no fermentation (celery, rhubarb, fruit skin, almonds, peanuts, walnut, chia seeds, sesame seeds): increases bacterial mass/stool bulking, gel-forming, improves gut transit, better tolerated by those with GI issues; downside — excess may be laxative, and high satiety can risk under-eating other intake goals.

### How much fiber to consume
- [SCIENCE] US recommendation: 14 g fiber per 1000 kcal → averages to 25 g/day (women), 38 g/day (men) at typical energy intakes (p.108).
- [SCIENCE] WHO recommendation: 12.5 g per 1000 kcal, general floor of ≥25 g/day for everyone (p.108).
- [CONVENTION] Practical rule of thumb (borrowed from 3DMJ dietitian Steve Taylor): minimum 20 g/day on average (doesn't need to be hit daily), then +1 g fiber for every additional 100 kcal above 1,500 kcal (p.108-109):

| Daily kcal | Fiber target |
|---|---|
| 1,600 kcal | 21 g |
| 2,000 kcal | 25 g |
| 2,500 kcal | 30 g |

- [SCIENCE] Very high or sharply increased fiber intake can cause GI distress (bloating, cramping, altered bowel regularity) and in some cases impair nutrient absorption (p.109).
- [SCIENCE] Most people in Western countries undershoot targets — typical American consumes <20 g/day (p.109).
- [CONVENTION] If current intake is ≥10 g below target, increase gradually over 1-2 months, adding ~2-5 g/week, to allow digestive adaptation (p.109).
- [CONVENTION] General upper limit: do not exceed 40 g/day unless already habituated to higher intakes with no digestive/bowel issues (p.109).

### Counting fiber calories
- [SCIENCE] Regardless of whether a food label lists fiber within total carbohydrate or separately, the energy from fermentable fiber (MACs) is already included in the label's total calorie count — no separate tracking adjustment needed (p.109).
- [CONVENTION] Practical simplification, restated from macronutrient chapter: only the carbohydrate *minimum* needs to be hit, not a precise fiber gram count; three checks suffice — (1) hit average daily fiber goal, (2) stay within total energy target, (3) meet minimum carbohydrate intake (p.110).

## Fruit and vegetable recommendations (p.110-111)
- [SCIENCE] Guideline consensus: >400 g/day (WHO) or 5 daily servings combined (UK) (p.110). Intakes in this range associated with lower all-cause mortality across populations (p.110).
- [CONVENTION] Rationale for a simple serving-based rule: sufficient varied fruit/veg intake reliably meets fiber and micronutrient targets plus other plant-based nutrients, without needing to track micronutrients directly (p.110).
- [CONVENTION] Energy-balance-adjusted targets (p.110-111):
  - Surplus or maintenance: 1 vegetable serving + 1 fruit serving per 1000 kcal/day.
    - Vegetable serving example: ½ cup cooked vegetables, 1 cup raw vegetables, or 2 cups leafy greens.
    - Fruit serving example: 1 cup berries/diced fruit, 1 medium piece of fruit, or 2 small fruits.
  - Deficit: keep fruit intake constant (fruit is more calorie-dense) but increase vegetables to 1 serving per 500 kcal (rounded up), prioritizing lower-energy-density options (berries over bananas; cruciferous vegetables/leafy greens/eggplant/pumpkin over starchy potatoes/yams/corn/sweet potatoes) — to boost satiety without excess calories (p.111).
  - Large/active individuals eating ≥4000 kcal/day: full 4-5 servings each may be impractical (excessive fullness/bloating from volume+fiber); a minimum of 2 servings of each is acceptable, approximating the health-associated intake while preserving room for energy needs (p.111).

**Table 3.2 — Fruit and vegetable intake recommendations** (p.111)

| Food Class | Recommendation | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Fruits | 1 serving/1000 kcal. Minimum 2 servings/day. | Round up. Choose low-energy-density options in deficit. |
| Vegetables | 1 serving/1000 kcal in surplus, 1 serving/500 kcal in deficit. | Round up. Choose fibrous, low-energy-density options in deficit. |

## Fluid intake recommendations (p.111-114)
- [SCIENCE] Hydration affects health, performance, and training adaptation. A 2% body-weight loss from fluid loss can impair aerobic performance; a 3% loss negatively impacts strength (p.111). Relevant to lifters because muscle tissue is predominantly water.
- [SCIENCE] During/after resistance training, muscles temporarily swell via intramuscular water retention ("the pump"), which may enhance the hypertrophy stimulus; training dehydrated considerably reduces this intramuscular fluid increase (p.111). An estimated 37-46% of gym-goers train in a dehydrated state (p.111).
- [SCIENCE] Nearly all fluids count toward hydration — water, coffee, tea, diet soda/soda, flavored waters, sports drinks, milk, juice, even beer/lager — because they are predominantly water with a similar hydration index to water (Beverage Hydration Index study, Maughan et al.) (p.112). Exception: high-alcohol-concentration drinks (spirits, strong cocktails) have a net dehydrating effect and don't count (p.112).
- [DISPUTED/CONVENTION] Body-weight-based hydration formulas are considered insufficient on their own: two equal-weight people can have very different hydration needs due to climate, training intensity/frequency, sweat rate, and genetics (p.113). Similarly, thirst alone is an unreliable indicator for athletes, given the ~4-in-10 dehydration rate noted above (p.113).
- [CONVENTION] Recommended primary method: autoregulate via urine color chart (validated real-time hydration assessment, Armstrong et al.) (p.113):
  - Color 1-3 → well hydrated.
  - Color ≥4 → dehydrated, consume more fluid.
  - Goal: majority of urinations in the 1-3 range. First morning urination is expected to be darker and is not itself a concern, but implies a relatively large fluid volume should be consumed in the morning/with breakfast (p.113).
  - Caveat: high-dose water-soluble vitamin supplements (vitamin C, B-complex) can artificially brighten/yellow urine color — combine urine-color monitoring with general intake guidelines when in doubt (p.113).

**Table 3.3 — Fluid intake guidelines** (p.114)

| Method | Guideline |
|---|---|
| Imperial (lbs) | ~2/3 of body weight in fluid ounces/day. Example: 180 lb person → ~120 fl oz/day. |
| Metric (kg) | ~1 L fluid per 23 kg body weight/day. Example: 69 kg person → ~3 L/day. |
| Qualitative | Monitor urine color; keep most urinations in the 1-3 range. |

### Sodium intake (p.114)
- [SCIENCE] US/UK/WHO guidance: limit sodium to ~2 g/day, since lower sodium intake is associated with lower cardiovascular disease risk (p.114) (cross-ref: US limit is <2300 mg/day sodium, per Healthy Eating Guidelines above).
- [SCIENCE] For athletes/active individuals, sweat sodium losses must be factored in; sweat rate varies by person and environment (p.114).
- [CONVENTION] Rule of thumb: consume 300-600 mg sodium (1.7-2.9 g table salt) per hour of exercise (p.114).
- [CONVENTION] Accounting for training losses, a daily sodium intake of 3-5 g/day may be more appropriate — and still likely safe — for lifters/athletes training regularly in warm/humid environments or with high sweat rates (p.114).
