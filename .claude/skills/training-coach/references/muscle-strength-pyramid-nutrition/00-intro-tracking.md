## Book roadmap (p.1-4)
The book is structured as a 5-level priority pyramid, bracketed by two non-Pyramid chapters the authors consider the most important in the text: **Mindset and Materials** (opens the book) and **Behavior and Lifestyle** (closes it) (p.5).

- Level 1: Energy Balance — maintenance calories, cut/bulk decision, rates of gain/loss.
- Level 2: Macronutrients — protein/carb/fat function and intake ranges.
- Level 3: Micronutrients — healthy-eating patterns, fiber, fruit/veg, fluid.
- Level 4: Nutrient Timing — diet breaks/refeeds, meal frequency, peri-workout.
- Level 5: Supplementation — least important level; most supplements unnecessary, a few evidence-backed ones offer modest benefit (p.3).
- Follow-on chapters (outside Pyramid): Making Adjustments and Measuring Progress; Peaking for Competition; Post-Diet Recovery; Behavior and Lifestyle.

[CONVENTION] The book is explicitly "not a meal plan or a rigid set of rules" but "a flexible, evidence-based framework" (p.4). A companion calculation spreadsheet is referenced at muscleandstrengthpyramids.com/nutrition-aid (p.4).

## Mindset and Materials — framing (p.5)
[SCIENCE] Despite ~two-thirds of the global adult population having attempted dieting since the 1980s, global obesity rates continue to rise (p.5, refs [1],[2]) — evidence that knowledge/information alone is insufficient; mindset and long-term adherence strategy are the limiting factor, not lack of technique.

## To Track or Not to Track? (p.6-13)

### First, consider your goals (p.6-7)
[CONVENTION] Tracking value scales with how demanding the goal is. Recreational lifters need it less than competitive physique/strength athletes.

[SCIENCE] Physique competition prep (prolonged, highly restrictive dieting to reach very low body-fat levels) is associated with substantial physiological costs (p.6, refs [3],[4]):
- Loss of menstrual cycle (women); decline in testosterone (men).
- Disrupted sleep, elevated stress hormones, elevated hunger signals, reduced satiety hormones.
- Disproportionate drops in energy expenditure beyond what weight loss alone predicts (metabolic adaptation).
- Loss of lean body mass and strength.
- Micronutrient deficiencies, fatigue, other negative health effects.

[SCIENCE] Psychological costs of competition dieting: lower mood, increased stress/irritability, food preoccupation, reduced sense of vigor (p.6). Physique athletes carry higher risk of body-image concerns and disordered eating vs. non-competitors, from a mix of personality traits, chronic energy restriction, and possibly the tracking methods themselves (p.7, ref [5]).

[SCIENCE] Weight-class strength sports (powerlifting, weightlifting, non-superheavyweight) usually require making-weight diets at some point (p.7, refs [6]-[8]); ~two-thirds of modern powerlifters diet year-round even in off-season, and most regularly track calories/macros (ref [9]).

### What about mental health concerns? (p.7)
[SCIENCE] Survey/correlational data: people with eating disorders are more likely to use nutrition-tracking apps than those without (refs [10],[11]); many with eating disorders report tracking worsened symptoms (refs [12],[13]). But correlation ≠ causation — those already struggling may simply be more likely to track (p.7).

[SCIENCE] Experimental (causal) data is more reassuring: for adults who do **not** already have body-image or disordered-eating issues, tracking does not appear to meaningfully harm mental health (p.7, refs [14]-[19]).

### Who should be cautious? (p.7-8)
[CONVENTION] Tracking can worsen pre-existing issues in people with:
- Body-image concerns or a history of disordered eating.
- Perfectionist tendencies.
- High anxiety or obsessive thinking.

The authors note many people drawn to physique/strength sports carry these traits (p.7). Recommendation: anyone currently or previously struggling with body image, food-related anxiety, or disordered eating should consult a qualified mental-health professional (psychologist, counselor, or eating-behavior-specialized dietitian) **before** adopting regimented tracking (p.8).

### The takeaway (p.8)
[SCIENCE] Tracking/self-monitoring is used successfully by most strength athletes and physique competitors, and is associated with long-term weight-loss success in the general population (p.8, ref [20]). But it isn't right for everyone, and even when appropriate, may not be the best tool at every point in time. Choice of method should reflect goals, personality, experience level, and current life circumstances (p.8).

### Accuracy, flexibility, and consistency (p.8-9)
[CONVENTION] Consistency drives results, and consistency requires balancing accuracy against flexibility:
- Too much accuracy focus → flexibility suffers → harder to stick to the plan when life gets messy.
- Too much flexibility → misses target too often → progress slows → motivation dips → consistency threatened.

**Core principle: be as accurate as necessary for the specific goal, no more.** Different goals demand different precision, but all goals require consistency, and consistency is built via appropriate flexibility (p.9). Example gradient: bodybuilder in final show-prep weeks needs far more precision than someone with an open-ended 100 lb loss goal; even off-season bodybuilder needs less precision than one prepping for stage.

[CONVENTION] "True flexible dieting" is adaptive, not obsessive — distinct from IIFYM practiced with rigid, black-and-white rules (weighing every gram, avoiding social meals, treating any macro miss as failure) (p.9).

### The problem with black-or-white / all-or-nothing / good-or-bad mindsets (p.10-13)
[SCIENCE] Processed/hyperpalatable foods are eaten faster, suppress appetite less, and passively raise energy intake → fat gain over time (p.10, ref [21]). So "eat this not that" heuristics have a kernel of truth, but "clean vs. dirty" food labeling is driven more by culture/trend than science (fat demonized 1950s-90s → low-carb/Atkins → Paleo → keto/carnivore/vegan all claiming the moral high ground) (p.10).

[CONVENTION] Key mindset shift: focus on **including** more nutrient-dense foods rather than **excluding** "bad" ones (p.10, p.13).

[SCIENCE] Unnecessary food restriction harms long-term adherence even when equally effective for fat loss. Bread-restriction study: two groups on the same diet, one forbidden bread — both lost equal fat, but the no-bread group had significantly more non-compliance episodes and a dropout rate >3x higher (p.10-11, ref [22]).

[SCIENCE] Flexible vs. rigid dietary restraint (Westenhoefer et al. 1999, ref [24]) (p.11):
| Type | Characteristics | Associated outcomes |
|---|---|---|
| Rigid control | Crash diets, fixed meal plans, moralizing food, all-or-nothing thinking | Higher BMI, more frequent binge eating |
| Flexible control | Moderate/adaptive, flexible meal timing, realistic timelines, no strict good/bad labeling | Lower BMI, fewer binges, reduced calorie intake, more successful long-term weight loss |

[SCIENCE] Follow-up research: the more someone shifted from rigid toward flexible control, the more likely they maintained results at 3-year follow-up (p.11, ref [25]). Black-or-white food thinking is itself a hallmark of rigid control (refs [26],[27]).

[CONVENTION] Bodybuilding's classic strict-cut/intentional-regain cycle is designed for short-term extremes, not sustainable long-term balance; non-competitors who imitate it often fall into yo-yo dieting not from lack of discipline but because the system wasn't built for their goal (p.11-12).

[SCIENCE] There is no such thing as a single "bad" food in isolation — no food, absent consideration of amount/frequency, has a measurable negative health/physique/strength impact ("the dose makes the poison") (p.12). Most "bad" foods are better described as **empty calories**: energy-dense, hyperpalatable, low in protein/fiber/micronutrients — problematic only when they dominate intake, not in small amounts (p.12, ref [21]).

[SCIENCE] Weight-loss-outcomes analysis: increasing low-energy-density foods (fruit, veg, lean meat, soups, low-fat dairy) was linked to better weight-loss outcomes; trying to restrict high-energy-density foods (the ones typically labeled "bad") was **not** linked to better outcomes (p.12-13, ref [28]). Conclusion: dietary pattern as a whole matters more than eliminating specific "bad" foods; moralizing food choices makes adherence harder and can raise disordered-eating risk (p.13).

## How to Track (p.13-16)

[CONVENTION] For beginners, best starting point is tracking the *current* diet without trying to change it (a couple weeks), to build tracking skill/awareness before setting macro targets (p.13). Tracking is not one skill but a set of "mini-games": spotting bad database entries, finding accurate label info, creating custom foods, logging correctly (raw vs. cooked), and estimating portions without a scale (p.13-14).

[CONVENTION] How much tracking is warranted depends on person/context (p.14):
- Competitive bodybuilders in prep / powerlifters making weight → high accuracy critical, especially early career.
- Non-competitors in a muscle-building phase → can often use a relaxed, habit-based approach: let appetite guide a slight surplus, hit daily minimums for fruit/veg/fiber/protein.
- Fat-loss goal → typically needs more structure (return to food scale + logging app).
- Some people track only during plateaus (as a check-in) or periodically (as an audit).

"Tracking is a tool, not a rule" (p.14).

[SCIENCE] Relying too heavily on external cues (calorie targets, daily weigh-ins) may reduce sensitivity to internal hunger/fullness signals, raising disordered-eating risk (p.14, ref [29]). Even among disciplined physique athletes, those with stronger internal-cue awareness (hunger, satiety, emotional state) self-regulate intake better during untracked "free meals" in contest prep (p.14, ref [30]).

### Nutrition tracking basics (p.15-16)
[CONVENTION] Core accuracy rules:
1. **Weigh food in grams, not volume** (cups/tablespoons) — volume measures vary by preparation, packing, and cup calibration; e.g. a labeled "½ cup oats (45 g)" may actually weigh 40-54 g depending on technique (p.15).
2. **Weigh foods before cooking, not after** — cooking changes water content but not nutrition content; high-water foods (vegetables, meats) lose weight cooking, water-absorbing foods (pasta, rice) gain weight cooking, so pre-cook weighing is the reproducible reference point (p.15).
3. Logging tool is flexible: app, spreadsheet, notebook, phone Notes app, or a combination (p.15-16).
4. App database entries are often user-submitted and inconsistent — if a food shows wildly different calorie/macro values across entries, verify via a quick web search; USDA data is a reliable reference, especially for whole/unprocessed foods (p.16).
5. For mixed dishes (pasta, pizza, curry, stir-fry, nachos) with known ingredients, log ingredients individually where possible — build once, reuse the saved meal (p.16).
6. Restaurant meals: estimate conservatively high — chefs typically use more oil/butter/sugar than home cooking (p.16).
7. Don't forget "extras" that add up untracked: cooking oil, salad dressing, butter/spreads, sauces/dips, sugar/creamers, alcoholic drinks, splash of milk in coffee (p.16-17).

## Body Weight Tracking Basics (p.17-19)

[CONVENTION] Scale reliance should match the goal: prepping bodybuilders / powerlifters cutting to make weight almost always need to track weight during (part of) their diet; someone just aiming to get leaner or reach a healthier body-fat level might not need the scale at all (p.17). Weight tracking, like food tracking, is optional — use it only when useful; scale anxiety affects even competitive athletes, and managing one's relationship with the number matters as much as the number itself (p.17).

### What the scale really measures (p.17-18)
[SCIENCE] Scale weight reflects everything in the body, not just fat. Non-fat sources of scale movement (p.17-18):
- More fruit/veg intake → more digestive-tract food mass → scale can rise even as calorie intake falls.
- More sodium (common with packaged/"diet" foods) → water retention.
- Dieting can slow bowel movements → temporarily stalls scale weight loss.
- Combined effects → plateaus of days-to-weeks even while fat loss continues.
- Normal daily fluctuation: **1-2%** of bodyweight is entirely normal (some people fluctuate more) (p.18).
- Alcohol → dehydration → can lower scale weight despite calorie overconsumption.
- High-carb meals → raise scale weight via water bound to glycogen.
- Stress → alters hormones/habits (e.g. hydration) that shift weight.
- Menstrual-cycle fluid shifts affect readings (p.18).

[CONVENTION] If scale-induced stress builds, take a break from the scale for a week or two and focus on process instead (p.18).

### Tracking for your goal — target rates (p.18)
[CONVENTION] (Detailed derivation deferred to Level 1, but stated here as the headline targets):
- Fat loss target: **0.5-1% of bodyweight per week**.
- Muscle gain target: **1-2% per month** (i.e., <1% per week).

[SCIENCE] A single weekly weigh-in cannot reliably detect changes of this magnitude, especially if timing/clothing/hydration/post-exercise state vary between weigh-ins (p.18).

### Protocol: how to weigh accurately (p.18-19)
[CONVENTION] Ordered method:
1. Weigh first thing in the morning.
2. After using the bathroom.
3. Before eating or drinking.
4. Ideally nude (or in the same light clothing each time).
5. Weigh **daily**, or at minimum **3x/week including one weekend day** if habits differ on weekends.
6. Take a **weekly average** — treat the average, not any single day's number, as the real data point.
7. Prefer a **rolling average** (e.g., each day averaged with the preceding 13-20 days) to visualize trend more clearly than a fixed weekly average (p.19).

[CONVENTION] Tools: any method that supports consistency works — nutrition-tracking apps with weight-logging built in, or standalone smoothing/averaging apps (e.g. Happy Scale, Libra) (p.19). The shortest time frame worth evaluating is a weekly average; single weigh-ins are not a valid basis for a decision (p.19).

[CONVENTION] Consistent conditions + averaging make trends far less volatile than raw daily numbers; when fluctuation occurs, the underlying trend still becomes visible over time. A flat or unclear trend on the average (not a single reading) is the signal to consider adjusting energy intake — expanded in Level 1 (p.19).
