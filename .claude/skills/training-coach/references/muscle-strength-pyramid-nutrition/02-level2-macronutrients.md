# MSP Nutrition — Level 2 — Macronutrients

> **Source:** *The Muscle & Strength Pyramid: Nutrition* 3e (Helms/Valdez/Morgan), book pp.67-94. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/msp-nutrition.txt` or PDF pp.67-94.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] research-established · [CONVENTION] the authors' specific evidence-based recommendation · [DISPUTED] contested / authors flag uncertainty.

## Macronutrient basics (p.67-69)

- **Protein**: built from amino acids (some essential, diet-required; some nonessential, body-synthesized). [SCIENCE] Primary role is building/repairing tissue, not fuel (p.67). Higher protein intake is associated with greater strength/lean-mass gains; omnivorous, plant-based, and vegan diets can produce similar muscle/strength gains provided total protein is sufficient and varied in source (p.68) [SCIENCE].
- **Carbohydrate & fat**: the body's primary fuel sources — carbs dominate at higher exercise intensity, fat at lower intensity (p.68) [SCIENCE]. Both are storable: fat as adipose tissue, carbs as glycogen (muscle + liver). Storage capacity is wildly asymmetric: a healthy young adult typically carries 10-15 kg (22-33 lb) body fat (~200 meals worth of energy) vs only ~600 g (1.3 lb) of glycogen (p.68) — a lean athlete can fuel weeks from fat stores alone, while a single hard session can deplete glycogen.
- **Fat's non-fuel roles** [SCIENCE]: supplies essential fatty acids (p.68), supports organ function (gallbladder) and sex-hormone levels, enables absorption of fat-soluble vitamins (p.68-69).
- **Carbohydrate's non-fuel roles** [SCIENCE]: carb-rich whole foods (fruit, veg, grains, legumes) supply fiber, vitamins, minerals, phytonutrients, and promote satiety even though carbs have no essential-nutrient status themselves (p.69). Too-low carbohydrate intake for training demands can cause underperformance and compounds low-energy-availability risk, raising REDs (Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport) symptom risk (p.69).

## Evidence-based practice framework (p.70-73)

- Recommendations synthesize three inputs (Venn diagram): (1) best relevant research evidence, (2) lifter's preferences/actions, (3) lifter's biopsychosocial state/circumstances — decisions sit at their intersection, mediated by the lifter/trainer (p.70).
- **Evidence pyramid** (hierarchy of evidence *quality*, top = strongest) (p.71): meta-analyses & systematic reviews > randomized controlled trials > non-randomized controlled trials > observational studies > case reports & series > expert opinion.
  - RCTs and non-randomized trials assess causation; RCTs randomize (isolating cause/effect) but often have small sport-science sample sizes (noise-prone); non-randomized trials use convenience samples (confounding risk) (p.71).
  - Systematic reviews/meta-analyses combine multiple studies statistically, increasing confidence and power to detect real effects despite individual study flaws (p.71).
  - Observational studies track groups without controls — can describe "what happened" but not isolate cause (p.72).
  - Case reports/series and expert opinion are lowest tier; useful for unique populations / real-world detail but cannot establish that an approach was optimal, only that it "didn't prevent success" (p.72).
- Top-tier evidence tells you what works, why, and how confidently to act; lower-tier evidence gives ideas that must be weighed carefully against context (p.73). The book draws heavily on the highest-quality evidence (systematic reviews/meta-analyses) where available, adapting recommendations for energy status, body-fat level, training type, and goals (p.73).

## Macronutrient Intake Guidelines Summary Table (p.74, restated p.86)

| Macronutrient | Calculation Basis | Energy | Minimum Guideline |
|---|---|---|---|
| Protein | g/kg total body mass or lean mass, adjusted for energy status and body fat | ~4 kcal/g | ≥1.6 g/kg/day total mass (≥2.2 g/kg/day lean mass) in surplus/maintenance, or in deficit for men >19% / women >27% body fat. ≥2.0 g/kg/day total mass (≥2.5 g/kg/day lean mass) in deficit if leaner. |
| Fat | Minimum absolute intake in grams/day | ~9 kcal/g | 40-60 g/day minimum. May drop lower briefly in advanced bodybuilding contest-prep phases. |
| Carbohydrate | g/kg body mass, adjusted for energy status | ~4 kcal/g | ≥2.0 g/kg/day in surplus. ≥1.0 g/kg/day in deficit. |

Three ways to prescribe macros, each with tradeoffs (p.74):
- **Relative to body weight** (g/kg or g/lb) — individualizes to body size but ignores total calorie "budget"; if expenditure is unusually low/high for body size, weight-based-only prescription across all three macros can cause unintended calorie excess/deficit.
- **% of total calories** — auto-scales macro intake to overall calorie intake (e.g., fat at 25% of calories stays proportional whether dieting at 1800 kcal or bulking at 3200 kcal).
- **Absolute gram amount** — simple minimum floor, doesn't scale with body size or calorie intake.
- Practical convention: combine methods — weight-based for protein, calorie-% for fat, carbs fill the remainder (p.74).
- Energy conversions: Protein ~4 kcal/g, Carbohydrate ~4 kcal/g, Fat ~9 kcal/g (p.74).

## Protein guidelines (p.75-82)

- [SCIENCE] Protein contributes only modestly to energy: at most ~10% of energy may come directly from amino acids depending on activity (p.75); unused dietary protein can be converted to glucose/ketones by the liver, but total energy contribution (direct+indirect) is small vs. carbs/fat.
- [SCIENCE] Protein's thermic effect of food (TEF) is >2x that of carbohydrate, despite both being ~4 kcal/g (p.75) — i.e., protein calories are more metabolically "costly" to process.
- In a deficit, the body relies more on protein for fuel for three reasons (p.75): (1) resistance training itself raises protein requirements; (2) reduced carb/fat intake shifts more energy burden onto protein; (3) shrinking glycogen/fat stores force greater protein (incl. muscle) breakdown to compensate.
- Muscle growth requires muscle protein synthesis (MPS) > muscle protein breakdown (MPB); resistance training triggers MPS, but adequate dietary protein is required to capitalize on that signal (p.75).

**Surplus/maintenance protein — Nunes et al. 2022 meta-analysis** [SCIENCE] (p.75-76): systematic review + meta-analysis, 66 studies / 2665 participants (62 resistance-trained, used for this analysis). Found a significant linear dose-response between protein intake and lean-mass gain, but statistical uncertainty widens at higher intakes; the lower-bound-of-uncertainty plateaus/flattens at ~2 g/kg/day total body mass, meaning it's unclear whether intakes above ~2 g/kg add further lean-mass benefit in surplus/maintenance. Category comparison: the benefit of 1.2-1.59 g/kg vs <1.2 g/kg was **2x** the benefit of >1.6 g/kg vs 1.2-1.59 g/kg — large gains going from low→moderate protein, diminishing returns moderate→high.
- **[CONVENTION] Recommendation (surplus/maintenance): ≥1.6 g/kg/day total body mass.** Higher intakes give marginal-at-best extra benefit but don't hurt, so overshooting slightly is fine (p.76, p.81).

**Deficit protein — Refalo, Helms & Trexler meta-regression** [SCIENCE] (p.77-78): systematic review with meta-regression, 29 studies / 729 non-obese resistance-trained participants in an energy deficit [ref 24]. Found a linear relationship between protein intake and lean-mass change: low intakes associated with lean-mass loss, high intakes with retention/slight gain.
  - Expressed per **total body mass**: point of no lean-mass loss = **1.9 g/kg/day**; increases up to the highest analyzed intake of 3.2 g/kg/day associated with slight lean-mass gains (p.77).
  - Expressed per **lean body mass**: point of no lean-mass loss = **2.5 g/kg/day**; increases up to highest analyzed intake of 4.2 g/kg/day associated with slight gains (p.78). Model certainty was slightly higher when scaled to lean mass (physiologically more direct driver of protein need) (p.78-79).
  - [SCIENCE] Protein intake explained only ~8% of variance in lean-mass change while dieting (p.79) — training quality, deficit size, body fat, sleep, genetics account for the rest.
  - **Hierarchy of muscle-mass-retention levers, most to least impactful** (Figure 2.6, p.79-80): (1) lifting weights/training, (2) rate of weight loss (deficit size), (3) macronutrient intake (protein) — protein is "a distant third." Aggressive calorie cuts paired with near-all-protein diets often degrade training performance/volume; a slower, higher-calorie, training-prioritized approach gives better results (p.79-80).
  - [SCIENCE] Body-fat-stratified sub-analysis (Refalo et al., Figure 2.7, p.80-81): studies grouped low (<12% men / <20% women), medium (~12-19% men / ~20-27% women), high (>19% men / >27% women) body fat. Leaner groups show a steeper slope (low protein → greater lean-mass loss) and the "zero-loss" protein threshold shifts higher as body fat drops — i.e., the leaner the lifter, the more protein matters during a deficit.

**[CONVENTION] Final protein recommendations** (p.81-82):
- Surplus/maintenance: ≥1.6 g/kg (0.7 g/lb) total body mass, or ≥2.2 g/kg (1 g/lb) lean body mass.
- Deficit, higher body fat (men >19%, women >27%): same as surplus (≥1.6 g/kg total / ≥2.2 g/kg lean) — higher body fat is protective against lean-mass loss.
- Deficit, leaner (men ≤19%, women ≤27%): ≥2 g/kg total body mass or ≥2.5 g/kg lean body mass.
- **Upper ceiling while dieting**: ~3 g/kg total body mass or ~4 g/kg lean body mass. Rationale: within a fixed calorie "budget," pushing protein higher forces carbs/fat down, which can reduce diet nutrient density or hurt performance if carb/fat drops below their own minimums (p.82).
- [SCIENCE] Plant-based/vegan diets: not inferior for muscle/strength gains if total protein is sufficient (≥1.6 g/kg) and spread across ≥3 meals/day, despite lower essential-amino-acid density/bioavailability of individual plant proteins (p.82).
- [SCIENCE] "Anabolic resistance" in older lifters: concern applies mainly to sedentary older adults; masters athletes are metabolically closer to younger trained counterparts and don't need meaningfully higher protein than the general ranges above (p.82).
- General note: protein should be split across ≥3 servings/day within an overall balanced diet regardless of age/diet type (detail deferred to Level 4) (p.82).

## Carbohydrate and fat guidelines (p.83-86)

- Once protein is set, calculate carb and fat intake next; these guidelines are minimum-gram floors for a balanced, performance/health-supporting diet (p.83).
- [CONVENTION] Carb minimums vary by energy status (higher in surplus for performance, lower in deficit to preserve the intended shortfall); fat minimum is constant regardless of energy status, to guarantee essential fatty acids and key physiological functions (p.83).
- [SCIENCE] Fat is denser (9 kcal/g) than carbs (4 kcal/g) and depletes more slowly; liver glycogen maintains blood glucose during fasting, muscle glycogen is preserved until high-intensity contraction (p.83).
- [SCIENCE] Unlike endurance/intermittent-power sports (soccer, basketball) that rely heavily on glycolysis and are glycogen-limited, strength training relies primarily on stored ATP and the phosphocreatine system; higher-rep hypertrophy work sits between strength and endurance, drawing more on glycolysis/glycogen as set duration increases / rest shortens (p.83). Muscle glycogen (esp. fast-twitch fibers) can be significantly depleted after a hard strength session; intramuscular triglycerides are also drawn on to a degree (p.84).

**Fat minimums** (p.84-85):
- [SCIENCE] Table 2.2 — Rough heuristics for absolute minimum fat intakes (compiled by Dr. Eric Trexler), healthy adults only, not medical advice:

| Outcome of interest | Approx. minimum intake (g/day) |
|---|---|
| Attenuate gallstone formation | 7-12 |
| Support essential fatty acid intake | 20-30 |
| Support fat-soluble vitamin absorption | 20-30 |
| Support sex hormone levels | 40-60 |

  - These are absolute floors, not targets/recommendations, and should rarely be a target; the ~10 g/day tier may avoid acute complications, but 20-30 g/day is needed to avoid short-term nutrient deficiency. Appropriate only for brief (weeks, not months) advanced physique-competition-prep phases before returning to healthier fat intake (p.84).
- [SCIENCE] The **40-60 g/day** practical day-to-day minimum is based on two meta-analyses: one on dietary fat and testosterone in men, one on dietary fat and estrogen in women (p.85) — chosen to prevent reductions in sex-hormone levels. This corresponds to roughly **20% of maintenance calories** scaled to body size/energy needs; the same analyses suggest hormone optimization is better supported nearer **≥25% of maintenance calories** (p.85). Energy availability/body-fat level has a larger effect on hormones than fine differences in fat intake, analogous to the protein-retention hierarchy (p.85).
- **[CONVENTION] 40-60 g/day is a floor, not a target** — best practice is to exceed it most days. Heavier lifters should treat the upper end (60 g) as their personal minimum, lighter lifters the lower end (40 g), others in between. In deficit, fat intake may naturally sit within this range; in surplus, most lifters should exceed the minimum, with the amount above it set by preference/carb-fat balance (p.85).

**Carbohydrate minimums** (p.85-86):
- [SCIENCE] Carbs are well-documented to affect performance especially in high-intensity endurance and field sports, where insufficient intake impairs performance — hence most sports-nutrition guidance emphasizes high carb intake. Resistance training's intermittent nature and lower total work (vs. endurance/field sports) makes it comparatively less carb-sensitive at low training volumes (p.85). However, inadequate carbs can impair *higher-volume* resistance training, exacerbate REDs symptoms, and negatively affect hormonal status, similar to insufficient fat (p.85-86).
- **[CONVENTION] Recommendation: ≥2 g/kg/day in surplus; ≥1 g/kg/day minimum in deficit** if needed to reach target rate of weight loss (p.86). Carbs below 2 g/kg aren't ideal, but dropping to 1 g/kg is sometimes the least-bad option versus cutting protein/fat below their minimums or adding excessive cardio — common in already-lean physique athletes facing metabolic adaptation (p.86). When feasible (especially at high training volume), keeping carbs higher is likely optimal for performance/muscle preservation, but isn't always achievable depending on phase/goals (p.86).

## Tracking macronutrients (p.87-88)

- [CONVENTION] Precise gram targets (sometimes ±5-10 g buffer) are common but the precision itself is largely arbitrary; the better question is what precision level actually changes results (p.87).
- [SCIENCE] When calories and protein are equated, diets differing in carb vs. fat ratio produce similar weight-loss results within reason (DIETFITS trial-type evidence) (p.87) — the "too low" threshold for any one macro is fairly forgiving as long as calorie needs and the protein/carb/fat minimums above are met.
- [CONVENTION] Approach: hit protein/carb/fat *minimums*, then distribute remaining ("discretionary") calories by preference/how the person feels or performs, rather than rigid day-to-day macro-swapping. Consistency in food choices (whole foods, fiber, fruit/veg) is recommended primarily for psychological reasons — habit formation, reduced decision fatigue, less scale-weight noise (making trend-spotting easier) — not because a specific macro ratio is physiologically superior once minimums are cleared (p.87).
- **[CONVENTION] Worked example — 75 kg (165 lb) lifter, gaining phase, target 2800 kcal/day** (p.87):
  - Protein minimum: 1.6 g/kg × 75 kg = **120 g** → 480 kcal
  - Carbohydrate minimum: 2 g/kg × 75 kg = **150 g** → 600 kcal
  - Fat minimum: mid-range body weight → **50 g** → 450 kcal
  - Total from minimums: **1530 kcal**
  - Discretionary calories remaining: 2800 − 1530 = **~1300 kcal**, distributed by preference.
- **[CONVENTION] Daily calorie-target tolerance: ±100 kcal** (p.88) — e.g., 2800 kcal target → aim for 2700-2900 kcal/day, while still clearing the 120 g protein / 150 g carb / 50 g fat minimums.
- Alcohol calories should be tracked and counted within the overall calorie range on days consumed; moderate occasional drinking is unlikely to meaningfully hurt progress if minimums and calorie range are still met (regular consumption isn't recommended) (p.88).

## Example calculations — full worked case (p.88-90)

**Setup**: 90 kg (198 lb) male, 19% body fat, intermediate, entering a gaining phase. Estimated maintenance (from prior chapter's method) ≈ 2600-4400 kcal/day range; simplified to **3000 kcal maintenance** for the example (p.88). Macronutrient minimums rounded to the nearest 5 g — "more precision isn't useful, as food labels themselves often vary by greater amounts" [ref 34] (p.88).

- [CONVENTION] **Gaining-phase calorie target**: aiming for 1% monthly weight gain ≈ +250 kcal/day above maintenance (198 lb × 0.01 × 0.25 × 500 lb→kcal conversion), targeting a conservative ~0.5 lb/week gain → **3250 kcal/day bulking target** (p.88).
- [CONVENTION] **Cutting-phase calorie target**: −500 kcal from maintenance (plus increasing step count, not detailed here, to offset expenditure drops from reduced food intake/metabolic adaptation) → **2500 kcal/day cutting target** (p.88).

**Protein** (p.88-89):
- Surplus, total body mass: 1.6 × 90 kg = 144 g → rounded **145 g** → 580 kcal.
- Surplus, lean mass basis: lean mass = 90 kg × 0.81 (100%−19% BF) ≈ 73 kg; 2.2 × 73 = **160 g** → 640 kcal.
- Deficit (not above 19% BF, so higher-protein rule applies): 2 g/kg total = 2×90 = 180 g; 2.5 g/kg lean = 2.5×73 = 182.5 g → rounded to 180 g. Both bases converge on **180 g** → 720 kcal.

**Fat** (p.89): at a "reasonably heavy" 90 kg, use the upper end of the 40-60 g range → **60 g/day** → 540 kcal.

**Carbohydrate** (p.89): 2 g/kg surplus → 180 g → 720 kcal. 1 g/kg deficit → 90 g → 360 kcal.

**Intake summary** (p.89-90):

| | Surplus | Deficit |
|---|---|---|
| Daily calorie target (±100 kcal) | 3250 → 3150-3350 kcal | 2500 → 2400-2600 kcal |
| Min protein (total/lean basis) | 145 g / 160 g (580/640 kcal) | 180 g (720 kcal, both bases converge) |
| Min fat | 60 g (540 kcal) | 60 g (540 kcal) |
| Min carbs | 180 g (720 kcal) | 90 g (360 kcal) |
| Calories from minimums (total/lean) | 1840 kcal / 1900 kcal | 1620 kcal |
| Discretionary calories (total/lean) | 1310-1510 kcal / 1250-1450 kcal | 780-980 kcal |

- Surplus phases leave more discretionary calories (more flexibility for social situations/customization); deficit phases leave less, but still far more flexibility than rigid to-the-gram macro systems (p.90).
