# MSP Nutrition — Level 1 — Energy Balance

> **Source:** *The Muscle & Strength Pyramid: Nutrition* 3e (Helms/Valdez/Morgan), book pp.23-66. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/msp-nutrition.txt` or PDF pp.23-66.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] research-established · [CONVENTION] the authors' specific evidence-based recommendation · [DISPUTED] contested / authors flag uncertainty.

## Overview (p.23)
- [SCIENCE] Long-term body-weight change is dictated by the cumulative difference between energy intake and energy expenditure ("calories in, calories out") — true but oversimplified, since intake/expenditure are themselves regulated subconsciously by the brain, food environment, and activity level (p.23).
- [SCIENCE] Rising obesity rates are largely driven by food-environment changes: ubiquitous, cheap, energy-dense, hyperpalatable foods (high fat/sugar, low fiber/protein, large portions) that are easy to overeat (p.23).
- [SCIENCE] Inactivity reduces energy expenditure AND disrupts appetite regulation, increasing overeating risk; regular physical activity restores/strengthens appetite-control mechanisms (p.23).

## Determining Maintenance Energy (p.23-28)
Two methods: **empirical tracking** (more accurate) vs **predictive equations** (a rough starting estimate) (p.24).

### Method 1: Tracking weight + food intake (p.24-27) [CONVENTION]
Protocol:
1. Prerequisite: basic tracking skills already developed (weighing/logging accurately) before attempting this (p.24).
2. Collect **14 consecutive days** of daily weigh-ins + logged calorie intake (3-4 days/week logging can work if representative) (p.24).
3. **Do not change diet** during data collection — changes introduce water/glycogen/gut-content noise that obscures results. If diet was already changed, discard the first week and extend tracking by another week (p.25).
4. Split data into two weekly averages; compare Week 1 vs Week 2 average body weight to determine if stable/increasing/decreasing (p.25).
5. Convert weight-change to an estimated surplus/deficit using the **3,500 kcal ≈ 1 lb (~0.5 kg) of adipose tissue** rule of thumb (p.26) [SCIENCE-derived convention, refined later]:
   - Required daily deficit/surplus estimate = (weekly weight change in lb × 500) kcal/day.
   - Worked example: +0.2 lb/week gain → 700 kcal/week surplus → ~100 kcal/day surplus; if average intake was 2,100 kcal/day, estimated maintenance ≈ 2,000 kcal/day (p.26).
6. Caveats on the 3,500-kcal rule (p.26): not all mass gained/lost is fat (water/glycogen/lean mass shift too); adipose tissue is ~85-90% lipid, not 100%; energy expenditure adapts to changes in intake/activity/body composition [SCIENCE] (metabolic adaptation, detailed below).
7. Despite the caveats, empirical tracking beats equations for three reasons (p.26): (a) individual variability in energy expenditure is large — [SCIENCE] even at identical body mass (~60 kg / 132 lb lean mass), individuals can vary up to **fourfold** (~5,000 kcal/day difference at extremes; ~500-1,500 kcal/day differences common) (p.26, ref [5]); (b) a consistent diet stabilizes weight, removing big water-shift noise; (c) muscle gain is too slow (except in novices) to meaningfully confound a 2-week measurement.

### Method 2: Predictive equations (p.27-28) [CONVENTION — explicitly discouraged by authors]
> "We don't recommend this" — equation estimates are just as likely to be inaccurate as accurate, and initial tracking-skill inexperience compounds the error (p.27).

- Most equations estimate **BMR/REE** (energy burned at rest), then apply an activity multiplier.
- **Cunningham (1980) equation** [SCIENCE, one of the better-performing formulas in both general population and muscular physique athletes, refs 6,7]: `BMR (kcal/day) = 500 + 22 × lean body mass (kg)`. Limitation: most people don't know their LBM accurately enough (p.27).
- **Muscle & Strength Pyramids 2025 simplified equation** [CONVENTION, authors' own]: `BMR (kcal/day) = 10 × total body mass (lb)` (or `22 × total body mass in kg`) (p.27).

Activity multipliers (Table 1.2, p.28) — apply to the BMR above:

| Lifestyle & Training Frequency (≥3 days/wk lifting) | Activity Multiplier |
|---|---|
| Sedentary | 1.3 – 1.6 |
| Lightly active | 1.5 – 1.8 |
| Active | 1.7 – 2.0 |
| Very active | 1.9 – 2.2 |

Worked example (p.28): 200 lb (91 kg) lifter → BMR = 200 × 10 = 2000 kcal → range 2000 × 1.3-2.2 = **2,600-4,400 kcal/day**. Authors note this range is wide, hence real-world tracking wins; equation is only for "a starting point."

## Is Tracking Caloric Intake Necessary? (p.28-31)
- [CONVENTION] Tracking is not mandatory for everyone. If current/past mental-health concerns exist or logging causes stress, tracking may be inappropriate. Some goals are achievable via body-weight tracking (3-4×/week for a running average) plus **post-meal appetite ratings** instead of calorie/macro tracking (p.28).

### Post-meal appetite ratings (Table 1.3, p.29), scale 1-7
| Rating | Description |
|---|---|
| 7 | Stuffed to point of discomfort, possibly nauseous |
| 6 | Uncomfortably full, need 1-2h to digest before training |
| 5 | Full, but not uncomfortably so |
| 4 | Satisfied, but could eat a little more |
| 3 | Hungry, but not uncomfortably so |
| 2 | Uncomfortably hungry, distracted, irritable |
| 1 | Extremely hungry, low energy, very food-focused, weak |

- [CONVENTION] 3-5 range after meals ≈ near maintenance; 4-6 ≈ modest surplus; 2-4 ≈ modest deficit (p.29).
- Preconditions for appetite ratings to work well (p.29-30) [SCIENCE-backed factors]:
  - **Activity level**: sedentary lifestyle disrupts appetite regulation and contributes to weight gain (p.29); hitting **≥7,000 steps/day** (per physical-activity guidelines, ref [9]) improves appetite control (p.29).
  - **Food quality**: diets high in energy density with low micronutrients/fiber/protein/thermic-effect-per-calorie are associated with obesity (refs 10-13); ultra-processed diets are eaten faster, cause higher energy intake at similar satiety, and drive weight gain vs. unprocessed diets in controlled trials (ref 14) (p.29).
  - **Appetite awareness varies** individually (ref 15); mindful eating practice (ref 16) and reflection improve it; even elite physique athletes benefit from trusting hunger cues during "free meals" (ref 17) (p.29).
  - **Sleep**: inconsistent/insufficient sleep undermines appetite regulation, increases high-calorie food cravings, raises overeating risk (refs 18-20) (p.30).
  - **Body fat**: everyone has a natural body-fat range where appetite regulates best; the body is better at ramping hunger UP when body fat drops below this range than dialing it back when body fat rises above it (ref 21) (p.30). Low body fat → hungry at maintenance; high body fat → feel stuffed at maintenance.

### When appetite ratings work / don't (p.30-31)
- Best suited to those who: are physically active, sleep consistently, eat mostly whole/unprocessed foods, can stay present while eating, and understand appetite signals shift with body-fat level (p.30).
- [CONVENTION] Regulating by appetite is NOT the same as eating freely — it will generally only get someone to the low end of their natural body-fat range; going lower (photoshoot/competition/weigh-in) requires accepting more hunger, tracked or not (p.30).
- For weight gain with low appetite/high expenditure: do the OPPOSITE of the above — eat more processed/energy-dense food, reduce physical activity (if lifestyle allows), use less-filling liquid calories (shakes, juice) (ref 22) (p.30).

## Should I Cut or Bulk? (p.31-35)
- [CONVENTION] No universal "ideal" physique; year-round ultra-lean "stage-ready" leanness is not sustainable and can be physically/psychologically unhealthy (p.31).

### New/untrained lifters (p.31-32)
- [CONVENTION] Terminology: authors prefer "gaining" over "bulking" (bulking implies aggressive, uncontrolled surplus) (p.31).
- New lifter with obesity: often no need for an intentional deficit — increased activity alone improves appetite regulation (ref 8), and gaining muscle while fat stays flat still drops body-fat % (p.31).
- Untrained/moderately-fat-but-not-obese lifter: structured resistance training alone (see MSP Training) often drives desired body-composition change without deliberate surplus/deficit, provided adequate protein — "newbie gains" happen even at maintenance (p.31-32). Reassess once beginner gains slow (criteria given later in book).

### Will high body fat blunt muscle gain? (p.32) [SCIENCE — myth-busting]
- No support in cross-sectional/longitudinal evidence for high body fat impairing hormonal/metabolic health enough to blunt hypertrophy. Opposite pattern: athletes with most muscle mass relative to height tend to carry HIGHER body fat (ref 23) (p.32).
- Division 1 football linemen: most >25% body fat, majority meet metabolic-syndrome/insulin-resistance criteria (ref 24), yet gain similar or greater lean mass than leaner teammates under the same program, often while simultaneously losing fat (refs 25-27) (p.32).
- Takeaway: high body fat does NOT prevent muscle gain. Getting very lean before a gaining phase can backfire — extreme leanness heightens hunger, harder to control intake, risks overeating/fat gain (p.32).

### Experienced lifters (p.33-35)
- [CONVENTION] Decision driven by goals/logistics/sustainability, not social-media rules.
- **Weight-class strength athletes**: avoid drifting far above class; those with 2-hour weigh-ins should keep body mass within **~5%** of weight class — allows dropping half via fat loss, the rest via short-term water/food-volume manipulation (p.33).
- **Physique competitors**: back-to-back seasons (not generally recommended for natural athletes) → cap body mass at **~10% over stage weight**; longer offseason (preferred for natural athletes) → allow up to **~15% over stage weight**, with mini-cuts bringing it back to ~10% before prep (p.33).
- **Non-competitors**: balance of personal preference, performance, sustainability; avoid starting a gaining phase already uncomfortable in one's skin.
- [DISPUTED/CONVENTION] Arbitrary body-fat targets (15/20/25% women, 8/10/12% men) common in fitness media are called out as unrealistic; sustainable body fat should not require restrictive diet/excessive exercise or cause disrupted sleep, hormonal imbalance (low libido, irregular cycles/amenorrhea), lethargy, constant hunger/food focus (p.33-34).
- [SCIENCE] Body fat ≠ health directly — can have low body fat + poor metabolic health, or high body fat + metabolically healthy, though odds of metabolic health decline as fat rises: only **7.5%** of BMI>30 obese individuals are metabolically healthy, just **1%** above BMI 36 (ref 28) (p.34).
- Translating "overweight" BMI (≥25, where health risk increases per ref 29) to body-fat % via BMI conversion (ref 30) gives much higher "healthy" limits than fitness media suggests (p.34):
  - **<20-25% body fat for men**
  - **<32-38% body fat for women**
- [CONVENTION] Recommend cutting to the low end of a sustainable body-fat range, then gaining as long as desired provided personal/health limits on total body fat aren't exceeded.
- **4:1 ratio rule**: recommend at least a **4:1 ratio of time spent gaining/maintaining : time spent cutting** — e.g., bulk 4 months → earns at most a 1-month mini-cut (p.34). Rationale: avoids staying too lean too long and "spinning wheels."
- Figure 1.1 (p.35, figure — see PDF p.35): illustrates an "optimal gaining zone" — gaining-phase body-fat cap and mini-cut body-fat target (both ~8 percentage points higher for women), oscillating over time as mass/muscle increase.

## Appropriate Rates of Weight Loss (p.36-37)
- [SCIENCE/CONVENTION] Recommended weekly weight-loss rate: **0.5-1.0% of body weight**, described as "generally ideal for lifters" because it minimizes lean-mass loss (ref 31) (p.36).
  - Example: 200 lb (91 kg) person → 1-2 lb (~0.5-0.9 kg)/week.
- Using 3,500 kcal ≈ 1 lb rule: daily deficit of **500-1,000 kcal** → weekly deficit of 3,500-7,000 kcal → matches the 1-2 lb/week target (p.36).
- **Formula**: `Required daily calorie deficit = Target weekly weight loss (lb) × 500`. Example: 1.5 lb/week target → 750 kcal/day deficit (p.36).
- [SCIENCE] Lower body fat → higher risk of lean-mass loss during a diet (ref 32) → leaner individuals should use slower loss rates (p.36-37).
  - Natural competitive physique athletes: weekly loss ≤0.5% more consistently associated with lean-mass retention than faster rates (ref 33) (p.37).
  - Murphy & Koehler (2022) meta-regression, 1,213 participants / 52 studies of resistance-trained dieters (ref 34): lean-mass losses increase **linearly** with energy deficits >500 kcal/day; smaller deficits allow lean-mass INCREASES even while dieting (p.37, Figure 1.2 — see PDF p.37).
  - High individual variability in lean-mass outcome at a given deficit, driven by training status (novices often gain muscle even under suboptimal diet conditions), training quality/quantity, and initial body-fat level (p.37).
  - Individuals with obesity have maintained lean mass on extremely low-calorie diets (e.g., 1,000 kcal/day) provided exercise was included (ref 35) — higher body fat offers a protective buffer against lean-mass loss during aggressive dieting (p.37-38).

**Table 1.4 — Recommended rates of weight loss and deficit sizes (p.38):**

| Body Fat % | Weekly Weight Loss | Max Daily Deficit |
|---|---|---|
| <12% (men) or <20% (women) | ≤0.5% of body mass | 500 kcal |
| 12-19% (men) or 20-27% (women) | 0.5-1% of body mass | 1000 kcal |
| >19% (men) or >27% (women) | 0.5-1.5% of body mass | 1500 kcal |

## Metabolic Adaptation During Weight Loss (p.38-42)
- [SCIENCE] A planned 500 kcal/day cut does NOT reliably yield 1 lb/week fat loss, because reduced intake also reduces expenditure.
  - **Thermic effect of food (TEF)** ≈ 10% of calories consumed (ref 36). Example: maintenance 3,000 kcal/day → TEF ≈300 kcal; cutting to 2,000 kcal/day → TEF ≈200 kcal, shrinking a planned 1,000 kcal deficit to ~900 kcal (p.38).
  - **Metabolic adaptation (adaptive thermogenesis)** = reduction in energy expenditure BEYOND what's predicted from mass loss alone — distinct from the expected reduction from simply being smaller (p.38-39).
  - [SCIENCE] On average, losing 10% of body weight → **20-25% reduction in total energy expenditure**, but only **10-15%** of that reduction is explained by smaller body size; the remaining **10-15%** is metabolic adaptation (ref 37) (p.39). Example: dieting 155→140 lb, predicted maintenance ~2,200-2,300 kcal, actual could be only ~2,000 kcal (a 200-300 kcal gap) (p.39).
  - Physiological drivers: decreased sympathetic nervous system activity, reduced metabolic hormone production (leptin), shifts in sex hormones (ref 38) (p.39). Majority of the reduction, however, is from **non-resting energy expenditure**: reduced fidgeting/postural movement, increased sedentary behavior from fatigue, improved muscular work efficiency at low-intensity activity (ref 40) (p.39, Figure 1.3 — see PDF p.39-40).
- **Consequence**: as weight loss slows, calorie intake likely needs to be cut MORE than initially expected, or expenditure increased via cardio — both viable but come with trade-offs (low energy availability risk on one hand, exercise energy compensation on the other) (p.40).

### Energy Availability (EA) & REDs (p.40-45)
- **Definition**: energy left over for basic physiological function after accounting for exercise expenditure, relative to lean body mass (p.40).
- **Formula**: `EA = (Total energy intake − Exercise expenditure) / LBM (kg)`, expressed as kcal/kg LBM/day (p.40).
  - Worked example: 100 kg athlete, 10% BF → 90 kg LBM; eats 3,000 kcal, burns 400 kcal exercise → (3000−400)/90 = **~28.9 kcal/kg LBM/day** (p.40).
- [SCIENCE] **Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs)**: syndrome from too-low EA (severe or prolonged), impairing metabolic rate, reproductive/menstrual health, bone integrity, immune function, protein synthesis, cardiovascular health (ref 41) (p.40).
- **Threshold**: symptoms of REDs tend to emerge when EA drops below **~30 kcal/kg LBM/day (13.6 kcal/lb LBM/day)**, regardless of whether the deficit is from more exercise, less intake, or both (refs 42,43) (p.40-41). [DISPUTED] Explicitly flagged as a helpful rule of thumb, NOT a universal threshold — most physique athletes dip below it during contest prep; even those staying above it may still show REDs symptoms depending on individual variability (baseline NEAT, LBM composition, sex) (ref 44) (p.41).
- [DISPUTED] Possible sex-specific thresholds: recommend staying above **~30 kcal/kg LBM (13.6 kcal/lb LBM) for women** and possibly above **~25 kcal/kg LBM (11.4 kcal/lb LBM) for men**, since some research suggests men tolerate lower thresholds (ref 53) (p.44). Authors stress: "your best guide is not the numbers, but your body" — watch for signs/symptoms of REDs.
- Distinguish metabolic adaptation (expenditure reduction mechanism) from REDs (the health/performance consequence of low EA) — EA can be low even AT energy balance if metabolic adaptation persists (p.42).
- [SCIENCE] Rosenbaum et al. (ref 45): matched trios (≥10% weight loss maintained 5-8 weeks vs. >1 year vs. never lost) — weight-reduced groups had significantly lower TDEE than never-lost group regardless of how long ago the loss occurred; persistent downregulation averaging **~15% below predicted levels**, a consistent literature finding (ref 37) (p.42).
  - Worked hypothetical (p.42-43): 70 kg, 25F, 12% BF, moderate exercise 4-5×/week → predicted TDEE ~2,491 kcal/day (via ref 46 equation); sedentary version ~2,041 kcal/day → exercise expenditure ~450 kcal/day. If she previously weighed 77 kg (10% loss) → apply 15% adaptation → actual expenditure ~2,117 kcal/day. LBM = 70×0.88 = 61.6 kg. EA at maintenance (2,117 kcal) = (2117−450)/61.6 = **~27.1 kcal/kg LBM/day** — below the 30 threshold even while eating at (adapted) maintenance.
- **Leptin** is the primary metabolic hormone triggering the metabolic-adaptation cascade; drops quickly at diet onset even before measurable fat loss (ref 47); mainly from adipose tissue (ref 48) but also stomach (short-term signal, ref 49) (p.43). Resting leptin most strongly predicted by body-fat % across individuals (ref 51) (p.43) — implying degree of adaptation/REDs severity depends on magnitude/duration of restriction, fat lost, and how far below one's normal body-fat range one goes.
- Post-diet: bodybuilders intentionally regain body fat post-competition to restore hormonal function (p.43). Case series (ref 52): among physique athletes eating above maintenance for 8-10 weeks post-contest, it was the **amount of body fat regained**, not calorie intake per se, that determined how quickly metabolic-adaptation/REDs symptoms reversed — those staying very lean with minimal fat regain kept low leptin/suppressed metabolic rate similar to pre-show levels (p.44).
- [CONVENTION] Non-physique athletes can likely avoid REDs entirely by not exceeding recommended weight-loss rates and avoiding unsustainably low body fat; if symptoms occur, diet more conservatively or cut back cardio, or use non-linear dieting strategies (diet breaks/refeeds — detailed in Level 4) (p.44-45).

## Cardio for Fat Loss (p.45-49)
- [CONVENTION] Cardio should NOT be the primary fat-loss tool.
- **Estimating cardio calorie burn via RPE 1-10** (distinct from lifting RPE/RIR scale) (p.45), rough kcal per 10 min per lb of body weight, ABOVE baseline daily movement (ref 54):

| Intensity | kcal/10min per lb |
|---|---|
| Light (RPE 2-4) | ~0.2 |
| Moderate (RPE 5-7) | ~0.45 |
| Vigorous (RPE 8-10) | ~0.7 |

RPE descriptors (Table 1.5, p.46): 1-2 very light/talk with ease … 10 maximal/talking impossible.

**Table 1.6 — Energy expended per 10 min cardio, NEAT-subtracted (p.46):**

| Intensity | 120 lb (54 kg) | 160 lb (73 kg) | 200 lb (90 kg) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Light (RPE 2-4) | 24 kcal | 32 kcal | 40 kcal |
| Moderate (RPE 5-7) | 54 kcal | 74 kcal | 90 kcal |
| Vigorous (RPE 8-10) | 84 kcal | 112 kcal | 140 kcal |

- [SCIENCE] Only the calories burned ABOVE normal baseline activity count toward the deficit — machine/wearable calorie counts overstate net contribution because resting/NEAT burn during that time isn't subtracted (p.46).
- [SCIENCE] Huiberts et al. 2024 meta-analysis (59 studies, 1,346 participants, ref 55): small but consistent **negative effect on lower-body strength** from combining cardio + lifting (interference effect) — from both physiological (competing adaptations) and practical (fatigue/recovery) factors (p.47). Mitigated by separating cardio sessions from lifting sessions (ref 56); low-impact cardio (cycling, incline walking) anecdotally may help. Effect mainly applies to true endurance-training-intensity cardio, not low-intensity/short-duration/low-RPE fat-loss cardio (p.47).
- [CONVENTION] Recommend increasing **step count** over relying on cardio-session kcal estimates:
  - **~7,000 steps/day** minimum goal for health and appetite regulation (p.47).
  - Above 7,000 steps, each additional **~2,500 steps burns ~100 kcal** (ref 57) — can substitute for a calorie cut when fat loss stalls (p.47).
  - Cap daily step increases at **no more than 50% above baseline** for fat-loss purposes, unless already very active (>15,000 steps/day) (p.48).
- [SCIENCE] HIIT was hypothesized to avoid interference (similar bioenergetic adaptations to lifting) but a 2023 meta-analysis found the same interference effect with HIIT (ref 58), likely from fatigue rather than conflicting adaptations (p.48). If using moderate/high-intensity cardio: limit to **1-3 sessions/week, ≤30 minutes each** (p.48).

### Exercise Energy Compensation (p.48-49)
- [SCIENCE] Constrained model of total energy expenditure: at higher activity levels, other components of daily expenditure adjust downward (compensation), unlike the naive additive model (ref 59) (p.48, Figure 1.5 — see PDF p.48).
- Context matters: at low activity or short durations (e.g., a marathon), activity increases expenditure mostly additively; chronic high activity prompts compensation (ref 60) (p.49). Compensation doesn't erase activity's benefits, just makes increases less impactful, especially during a deficit (ref 61).
- [SCIENCE] On average people burn **~30% fewer calories than predicted** when increasing activity (ref 62) (p.49), with wide individual variation:
  - Lean + aggressively dieting + lots of cardio → up to **~50% reduction**.
  - Person with obesity, eating at maintenance/surplus, moderate cardio → only **~10% compensation**.
- **Bottom-line cardio recommendation** (p.49): cap daily steps at ~50% above baseline (unless already >15,000/day); limit moderate/high-intensity cardio to 1-3 sessions/week, <30 min each, ideally separate from lifting.

## Appropriate Rates of Weight Gain (p.49-53)
- [CONVENTION] Goal post-novice-phase: minimize (not eliminate) fat gain while maximizing muscle growth; more advanced lifters progress slower and benefit less from a large surplus because muscle can't be built past physiological limits that fast (refs 63-65) (p.49).

### Supporting studies (p.49-51)
- **Rozenek et al. [66]**: untrained men, +2,000 kcal/day weight-gainer shakes (carb-only vs. mixed protein+carb) × 8 weeks → both groups gained ~3 kg (~7 lb), almost all lean mass — **~2% body mass/month as pure lean mass** (p.49-50).
- **Smith et al. [67]**: ≥6 months training experience, 6-week protocol + high-calorie carb/protein shake, target ~1 lb/week gain → on average, participants gaining FASTER than **~2% body mass/month** had disproportionately higher body-fat gain (p.50).
- **Garthe et al. [68]**: elite athletes, 8-12 week gaining phase + hypertrophy protocol; nutrition-guided group averaged **~1.5% body mass/month**, self-guided group averaged **~0.5%/month** — no significant difference in lean mass or strength gained between groups → the faster group's extra mass was mostly fat (p.50).
- **Helms et al. [69]**: intermediate/advanced lifters randomized to small surplus / large surplus / maintenance + 8-week hypertrophy protocol — rate of gain had NO meaningful impact on 1RM squat/bench or quad/tricep muscle thickness; those who gained the most weight were more likely to gain more fat; only a weak positive relationship between gain rate and biceps thickness (biceps trained with higher volume/closer to failure) — demonstrating nutrition surplus is only "permissive," not causative, for growth; requires a strong training stimulus (p.50).

**Table 1.7 — Training status & recommended monthly rate of weight gain (p.51):**

| Training Status | Definition | Recommended Rate |
|---|---|---|
| Novice | Adds load/reps every session or week-to-week; phase may last years but can be as short as 6 months with consistent progressive training | 1-2% of body mass/month |
| Intermediate | Progress slows: adds reps in 10-20 rep range week-to-week, or load in lower rep ranges month-to-month; most reach this by year 1, may remain through years 4-5; many never progress past it | 0.5-1.5% of body mass/month |
| Advanced | Much slower gains: adds reps in 10-20 rep range month-to-month, or slowly adds reps/load in lower rep ranges; most lifters never reach this phase | ≤0.5% of body mass/month |

- Worked examples (p.51): novice at 200 lb (91 kg) → 2-4 lb/month; novice at 130 lb (59 kg) → ~1.3-2.6 lb/month. Intermediate/advanced aim for roughly half or a quarter of novice rates.
- [CONVENTION] Advanced lifters without strong nutrition habits may find it more practical to gain at the intermediate rate but should expect to cut more often to manage body fat; primary focus at this stage should be training progression (reps/load), not the scale — forcing faster weight gain mostly adds fat, not results (p.51).
- **Formula**: `Required calorie surplus = Target weekly rate of weight gain (lb) × 500`. Example: 70 kg (154 lb) lifter targeting 1% body mass/month = ~0.7 kg/month = ~0.2 kg (~0.4 lb)/week → surplus ≈ 0.4 × 500 = **~200 kcal/day** (p.55). Use 2-3 week average weigh-ins to fine-tune (p.55).

## Metabolic Adaptation During Weight Gain (p.52-55)
- [SCIENCE] Lean mass contains ~1,800 kcal/kg (about 1/5 the energy density of fat mass), but this doesn't mean +100 kcal/day yields 1 lb/week of pure muscle — several complicating factors (p.52-53):
  1. Metabolic adaptation also occurs during gaining (expenditure can rise ABOVE predicted).
  2. Post-novice, gained weight is rarely pure lean mass; usually a mix of muscle and fat.
  3. Building muscle is itself metabolically costly, raising expenditure further.
- [SCIENCE] Adaptive response is **stronger and more consistent during weight loss than weight gain** — hypothesized evolutionary asymmetry (humans evolved to survive famine, not to stay lean/agile to evade predators) (ref 70) (p.53).
- Levine et al. [71]: participants overfed +1,000 kcal/day × 8 weeks, no training → fat mass gained varied **more than tenfold** across individuals (0.36 kg to 4.23 kg), because non-resting energy expenditure changes varied widely (some burned 98 kcal LESS/day, others up to 692 kcal MORE/day); one extreme responder compensated for ~70% of the surplus via increased expenditure — likely mechanism behind "hard gainers" (p.53).
- Additional expenditure increases with gaining: TEF from eating more, weighing more (burns more just moving), lifting heavier as one gets stronger (p.53-54).
- **Cost of building muscle**: exact energetic cost unknown, but ~20% of resting expenditure goes to daily protein turnover, and muscle protein synthesis drops even with modest deficits — both suggest muscle growth is energy-intensive (ref 72) (p.53-54).
- Figure 1.6 (p.52, see PDF): reductions in expenditure during a deficit ≈ 10% from TEF drop + ~30% from exercise-energy-compensation-type effects + metabolic adaptation averaging **~10-15% lower than predicted**, worse with larger deficit / lower body fat.
- Figure 1.7 (p.54, see PDF): increases in expenditure during a surplus ≈ 10% from TEF rise + unknown scaling cost of building tissue + metabolic adaptation averaging **~0-15% higher than predicted** (highly variable between individuals).
- [CONVENTION] Despite the imprecision, the 3,500-kcal rule remains a "reasonable guide" for estimating the surplus needed (p.54-55) — see formula above.

## Body Recomposition (p.56-59)
- **Definition**: simultaneously gaining muscle and losing fat (p.56). More common than popularly believed — even fat mass staying flat while muscle is gained lowers body-fat % and improves visual leanness.
- Two misconceptions debunked (p.56):
  1. "If you gain weight, you're in a caloric surplus" — false.
  2. "To gain muscle, you must be in a caloric surplus" — false.
- **Refined energy-density figures** (p.56-57) [SCIENCE, more precise than the 3,500 kcal rule]:
  - 1 lb = 454 g; dietary fat ≈ 9 kcal/g → 454×9 = 4,086 kcal/lb of pure fat; but adipose tissue is ~87% fat by mass → **~3,555 kcal/lb of body fat** (rounded to 3,500). 1 kg fat mass ≈ **7,700 kcal** (common rounding).
  - More precise still: 1 kg fat mass metabolizable energy (ignoring water/supporting tissue) ≈ **9,400 kcal/kg**; 1 kg lean tissue ≈ **1,800 kcal/kg** (ref 3) (p.57).
  - Worked example: losing 1 kg lean + 1 kg fat over 4 weeks = 2 kg total lost, cumulative deficit ≈ **-11,200 kcal** (1,800+9,400) (p.57).
  - Worked example: gaining 2 kg lean + losing 1 kg fat = net +1 kg body weight, but actual net energy balance ≈ **-5,800 kcal** (a deficit!) — demonstrating weight gain while in caloric deficit is mathematically consistent with muscle gain (p.57).
- [SCIENCE] Recomposition happens "regularly" when training stimulus is strong enough, especially in untrained individuals with higher body fat (ref 72/73), but even trained lifters have shown lean-mass gains while dieting (ref 74) (p.57).
- Rozenek study control group [66] (no shakes, usual diet): gained ~1.5 kg lean mass AND lost ~0.9 kg fat mass over 8 weeks (net +0.6 kg) while in an estimated small deficit (~6,000 kcal over 8 weeks, ~100 kcal/day) — muscle gain + fat loss simultaneously in a deficit (p.57).
- [SCIENCE] Recomposition efficiency decreases with: lower body fat (ref 32), greater energy deficit (ref 34), higher training status (well-trained lifters near genetic potential grow slowly regardless of surplus/deficit) (p.58).
- [CONVENTION] A trained lifter who makes a substantial positive change to training/recovery environment can plausibly gain some muscle while losing fat in a small-to-moderate deficit, especially if not very lean (p.58).
- Detraining/injury note (p.58): extended time off can cause simultaneous muscle loss + fat gain (weight loss despite caloric surplus) — unpleasant but reversible; "muscle memory" (mechanism still being characterized, ref 75) means regained muscle comes back quickly on retraining (ref 76) (p.58).
- [DISPUTED/edge case] Losing fat in an energy surplus is technically possible if the muscle-building stimulus is powerful enough that the body draws on stored fat, but because fat is >5× more energy-dense than muscle, this requires extreme circumstances for a small magnitude of fat loss; anecdotally reported with heavy PED use; also documented in elderly obese men with significant age-related muscle atrophy starting resistance training + protein supplementation (ref 77) (p.58).
- **Three practical takeaways (p.58-59)**:
  1. Don't over-rely on scale math — a pound isn't always pure fat or muscle; water fluctuations and composition shifts skew the number.
  2. Don't let fear of fat gain prevent purposeful gaining phases — trying to stay shredded while building muscle usually means spinning wheels; use short diet phases (mini-cuts) if fat gain gets too high.
  3. Bulk/cut cycling isn't mandatory — for those not especially lean, or for whom frequent surplus/deficit switching disrupts consistency, eating around maintenance or in a small deficit long-term with solid training still yields slow, steady progress.
