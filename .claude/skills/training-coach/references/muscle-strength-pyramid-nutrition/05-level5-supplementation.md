# MSP Nutrition — Level 5 — Supplementation

> **Source:** *The Muscle & Strength Pyramid: Nutrition* 3e (Helms/Valdez/Morgan), book pp.145-178. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/msp-nutrition.txt` or PDF pp.145-178.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] research-established · [CONVENTION] the authors' specific evidence-based recommendation · [DISPUTED] contested / authors flag uncertainty.

Supplementation sits at the top (least important level) of the Muscle & Strength Nutrition Pyramid — training, energy balance, macros, and food-based micronutrient/fiber/hydration intake (Levels 1-4) account for the overwhelming majority of outcomes; supplements offer at best marginal, incremental effects on top of that foundation (p.145, p.155).

## The Placebo Effect (p.145-148)

- [SCIENCE] A placebo is an inert substance given to participants who believe it might be the real supplement. Belief alone can produce measurable physiological effects — the placebo effect (p.145). Rigorous supplement trials use three arms (supplement / placebo / no-treatment control) to isolate true drug effect from belief-driven effect (p.145).
- [SCIENCE] 2014 weight-loss study (p.147): participants given weight-loss counseling were randomized to (1) placebo told it was a supplement, (2) placebo told there was a 50% chance it was a supplement, or (3) nothing. No group got a real supplement.
  - Group 1 (told "supplement"): self-efficacy declined, belief in supplement effectiveness rose — they credited the "supplement," not themselves.
  - Group 2 (50/50): stable self-efficacy and stable supplement belief.
  - Group 3 (nothing): self-efficacy increased, supplement belief decreased.
  - Across all groups, stronger belief in supplement effectiveness correlated with *less* weight lost.
  - No significant between-group difference in actual weight loss (p.146-147).
- [SCIENCE] 1983 insomnia experiment: participants with lower self-esteem were more susceptible to placebo "arousal"/"relaxation" pills (p.148).
- [SCIENCE] Believing you carry a particular gene variant (regardless of whether you actually do) affected diet/exercise outcomes more than actually carrying the gene (p.148).
- [CONVENTION] Implication for coaches/lifters: because nutrition, training, and lifestyle changes account for ~99% of outcomes, supplements should be adopted because evidence supports them and they fit one's goals — not as a hedge against doubting one's own capacity to change. Overreliance on a supplement as the source of results can erode self-efficacy and locus of control, undermining sustainable behavior change (p.148-149). People with lowest self-efficacy are often most drawn to supplements, and least likely to need/benefit from them (p.149).

## Supplement Safety and Quality (p.149-152)

- [SCIENCE] Financial cost is not the only downside of low-evidence supplements — safety/contamination risk is real, especially for drug-tested athletes (p.149).
- [SCIENCE] Norway doping-control analysis 2003-2020: 26% of athletes with an adverse finding claimed a supplement was the source of the banned substance; despite high evidentiary/financial hurdles to mount this defense at tribunal, roughly half of those claims were substantiated with solid evidence (p.150) — implying supplement contamination is a real, common risk, not just a convenient excuse.
- [SCIENCE] In the US, supplements are not subject to pre-market approval (unlike pharmaceuticals); FDA intervenes only reactively, after adverse events or third-party findings surface (p.150).
- [SCIENCE] Contamination mechanisms (p.150): intentional/negligent mislabeling; use of obscure chemical synonyms of banned substances; cross-contamination from shared manufacturing/storage/transport equipment; upstream contamination from raw-ingredient suppliers unknown even to the brand; increasing anti-doping test sensitivity means even trace, performance-irrelevant contamination can trigger a positive test.
- [SCIENCE] History of contamination rates:
  - 2004 (Geyer et al.): 634 non-hormonal supplements from 215 companies/15 countries — 14.8% contained unlabeled anabolic steroids; 21.1% among companies that also sold hormonal products; 9.6% even among companies not selling hormonal products (p.151).
  - 2007 follow-up: contamination dropped to 0.7% of 597 products, attributed to the 2004 US Anabolic Steroid Control Act and public scrutiny (p.151).
  - 2010s: pre-workouts contaminated with amphetamines (e.g., DMAA); 2015-2017 USADA reported 100 positive WADA tests for the SARM Ostarine; 2017 analysis found 9.1% (10/110) of weight-loss/fat-burning/energy/performance supplements purchased online 2015-2016 contained illegal stimulants (p.151).
  - 2013 FDA report: 70% of supplement manufacturers failed to comply with Good Manufacturing Practices (not proactively enforced) (p.151).
  - FDA-identified contaminated supplements 2007-2016 contained antidepressants, laxatives, erectile-dysfunction drugs, including drugs no longer prescribed for safety reasons (p.151-152).
  - 2023 (Cohen et al.): tested 57 herbal supplements — only 60% contained a detectable amount of the labeled ingredient; among those that did, dose ranged from 0.02% to 334% of label claim; only 11% (6/57) were within 10% of label claim; none of 8 turkesterone products had even 1% of stated dose; 12% (7/57) contained at least one FDA-prohibited substance (synthetic stimulants: 1,4-dimethylamylamine, deterenol, octodrine, oxilofrine; plus the nootropic omberacetam) (p.152).
  - [SCIENCE] 2023 review: multi-ingredient pre-workouts and products marketed as anabolic/performance-enhancing/weight-reducing/fat-burning show the highest contamination rates; overall estimate 10-30% of all supplements are contaminated with undeclared/banned substances — separate from (and likely smaller than) the issue of products simply failing to meet their own label-claim dose (p.152).

## The Supplement Tier System (p.152-156)

- [CONVENTION] Tier system (originated by Dr. Eric Trexler) used to decide whether a supplement is worth the risk (p.152).

| Tier | Definition |
|---|---|
| 1 | Strong evidence supporting reliable and noteworthy effects |
| 2 | Evidence supporting efficacy, but less data, smaller effects, less reliable effects, or more specialized applications than Tier 1 |
| 3 | Insufficient evidence to conclusively determine efficacy |
| 4 | Sufficient evidence that the supplement does NOT have practically meaningful effects for a given application |
| 5 | Sufficient evidence the supplement harms health or performance |

(Table 5.1, p.153.)

- [CONVENTION] Supplements generally start at Tier 3 and move up or down as evidence accumulates (p.153). Most supplements sit in Tiers 3-4 (p.154).
- [CONVENTION] Given the real risks (not just cost), limit supplement use to Tier 1 and occasionally Tier 2 (p.154).
- [SCIENCE] History shows the Tier 1/2 list has narrowed over decades, not expanded: HMB was believed a "next creatine" muscle-building candidate in the mid-2000s based on a meta-analysis showing significant lean-mass gains, but later evidence (especially with adequate protein intake) showed effects were overblown → demoted to Tier 4 (p.154). BCAAs were widely used 2009-2015 to address a supposed post-meal MPS "refractory window," but effects were overstated when protein intake was already sufficient → also demoted to Tier 4 (p.154-155).
- [CONVENTION] Only creatine monohydrate currently holds Tier 1 status, and has done so for >30 years, with a low likelihood of any new supplement joining it soon (p.155). Two implications: (1) odds of a new supplement reaching even Tier 2 are low; (2) odds of reaching/maintaining Tier 1 are extremely low (p.155).
- [SCIENCE] Creatine's effects, while the best-studied and most reliable in the field, are themselves only "trivial to small" for hypertrophy measured directly (ultrasound/CT/MRI) vs. resistance training alone; ~5 kg upper-body and ~11 kg lower-body strength increase; ~1 kg average lean-mass increase vs. training alone (p.156, citing [18-20]). This is presented as the ceiling of realistic expectations for ANY supplement (p.155-156).
- [CONVENTION] Three caveats framing supplement expectations (p.156):
  1. No legal supplement has matched creatine's status to date.
  2. Only a few Tier 2 supplements provide even a measurable effect smaller than creatine's.
  3. The most likely outcome for any new supplement is that it has no effect at all.

## How to Decide Whether to Take a Supplement (p.156-158)

- [CONVENTION] Decision flowchart (Figure 5.1, p.157) — three key questions (p.156):
  1. Does the supplement have a meaningful effect?
  2. Is that effect relevant to your specific goals?
  3. Can you mitigate the risks inherent to taking it?
- [CONVENTION] Flowchart logic (p.157):
  - If Tier 1 → generally fine to consider.
  - If not Tier 1, check Tier 2: are there longitudinal¹ data of a direct²/meaningful³/relevant⁴ effect? If no → don't take it.
  - If yes → can you obtain a third-party-tested or licensed (all ingredients) version?
    - No → don't take it.
    - Yes → could consider taking it, acknowledging risk can't be completely eliminated.
  - Footnote definitions: ¹longitudinal = studied over long enough periods to see real change, not just acute/short-term; ²direct = measures the actual outcome of interest (e.g., hypertrophy) rather than a proxy (testosterone, MPS, EMG); ³meaningful = standardized effect size ≥0.2 (Cohen's d / Hedge's g), ideally from meta-analyses; ⁴relevant = the effect actually applies to the goal (example given: beta-alanine only helps efforts >1 min, irrelevant to strength/bodybuilding).
- [CONVENTION] Best risk-mitigation strategy: only use products that are third-party tested — an independent org (unaffiliated with the manufacturer) verifies manufacturing practices and/or that batches contain what's claimed (p.157-158, Figure 5.2 — see PDF p.158 for certification seal logos).
- [SCIENCE] Third-party testing can also apply to ingredient (B2B) suppliers rather than the finished-product brand — e.g., Creapure® (creatine monohydrate), CarnoSyn® (beta-alanine), KSM-66® (ashwagandha) are third-party-tested branded ingredients licensed into other companies' products; this licensing may not be clearly disclosed on labels (p.158-159).
- [CONVENTION] A licensed/verified single ingredient does NOT guarantee the safety/purity of a multi-ingredient formula built around it — this risk-mitigation approach is most reliable for single-ingredient products using a licensed, tested ingredient (p.159).
- [CONVENTION] Only Tier 1 and Tier 2 supplements are worth discussing further: Tier 3 offers small/unlikely benefit at the same risk profile; Tier 4 has been shown not to work; Tier 5 is actively harmful (p.159). Protein powders are deliberately excluded from this list — treated as protein-containing food, useful only insofar as they help hit daily protein target or nutrient-timing goals (p.159).

## Tier 1 Supplements (p.159-161)

- [CONVENTION] Tier 1 bar: proven long-term strength or body-composition improvement (not just an acute performance boost), backed by a large, high-quality, consistent body of research giving a precise, confident effect estimate. Modern-era Tier 1 supplements are unlikely to be downgraded (p.159).
- [CONVENTION] Future candidates would need ALL of: (1) long-term strength/body-composition improvements, (2) broad applicability across populations/goals, (3) consistent-or-stronger effects across more studies (p.159).

### Creatine Monohydrate (p.160-161)
- [SCIENCE] Mechanism: naturally synthesized from glycine, methionine, arginine; supplies the phosphocreatine energy system supporting the first ~10-20 s of high-intensity effort; supplementation reliably increases strength, power, and muscle mass with chronic use (p.160, citing [18-20]).
- [SCIENCE] No alternative creatine form has outperformed monohydrate; some are less effective and nearly all cost more (p.160).
- [SCIENCE] Dietary creatine (meat) is degraded significantly by cooking; supplemental creatine (vegan-friendly, non-animal-derived) is needed to reach full ergogenic benefit (p.160).
- [SCIENCE] Myths/clarifications (p.160-161, citing [17,24,25]):
  - Not an anabolic steroid; safe for continuous use.
  - Timing doesn't matter — driven by total muscle saturation, not pre/post timing.
  - Loading isn't required — high-dose loading (20 g/day × 5-7 days) speeds saturation, but ~5 g/day steady dosing saturates within a few weeks.
  - Co-ingestion with carbs/protein may speed uptake but becomes irrelevant with consistent daily dosing.
  - Creatine + caffeine likely don't oppose each other, though very high caffeine doses during a loading phase may cause GI discomfort that hurts performance.
  - Side effects are mild/rare, mainly GI discomfort — minimized by steady dosing instead of loading.
  - Scale-weight increase is mainly intracellular water in muscle, not systemic bloating/water retention.
  - Does NOT cause hair loss, dehydration, kidney damage, infertility, fat gain, high blood pressure, increased cancer risk, or excessive urination at recommended doses in healthy individuals.
  - Safe/effective across ages (children through older adults).
  - Vegetarians/vegans may benefit more (lower baseline creatine stores).
  - May benefit brain function/cognition, especially under sleep deprivation, and may reduce severity/aid recovery from traumatic brain injury (unclear if this requires higher doses).
- [CONVENTION] Dose: ~0.03 g/lb bodyweight (~0.07 g/kg) daily — about 5 g for most people — sufficient for performance and muscle-mass benefit (p.161).
- [CONVENTION] Practical note: if starting creatine while dieting, consider a loading phase (20 g/day × 5-7 days) so the associated water-weight bump happens all at once; then use the post-loading weight as the new baseline (p.161).

## Tier 2 Supplements (p.161-169)

- [CONVENTION] Tier 2 qualifies when a supplement (1) lacks enough research to be confident future studies won't demote it, (2) has smaller effects that may not be worthwhile for most people, or (3) has less reliable outcomes due to unknown factors or specialized applications (p.161).
- [SCIENCE] Illustrative demotion case — beta-alanine: previously Tier 2, now considered Tier 3 (or Tier 4) for muscle/strength goals. 2012 and 2017 meta-analyses found benefit only for efforts >1 min; a 2024 meta-analysis narrowed this to tasks lasting 4-10 min specifically; mechanism (muscle carnosine buffering acidosis-related fatigue) doesn't typically apply to strength training or even high-rep bodybuilding; a 2022 meta-analysis found no effect on body composition. May retain Tier 2 (or even future Tier 1) potential for middle-distance runners, team-sport, combat, or CrossFit athletes whose training involves sustained high-intensity efforts (p.161-163).
- [CONVENTION] General note: Tier 2 supplements are more likely to be downgraded than promoted over time; worth is individual — depends on goals, personal response, and tolerance for marginal/uncertain effects; many apply to specific niches only (p.163).

### Caffeine (p.163-166)
- [SCIENCE] ~85% of Americans consume caffeine daily; despite being studied even more than creatine (a 2024 meta-analysis of nine caffeine meta-analyses exists), it sits in Tier 2, not Tier 1 (p.163).
- [SCIENCE] Acute benefits well-established: enhances maximal strength, power, and muscular endurance via central (reduced perceived exertion/pain, general stimulation) and peripheral (enhanced muscle contractility) mechanisms. Benefits seen at doses as low as ~1-2 mg/kg but most reliable at ≥5 mg/kg taken 30-60 min pre-training; effects slightly stronger for muscular endurance than for maximal strength (p.163-164).
- [DISPUTED] Chronic/long-term adaptation evidence is thin — few studies, mixed findings; some show improved strength/power, none yet show superior body-composition change (p.164).
- [SCIENCE] Side effects at ≥6 mg/kg are common: 83% heart palpitations/increased HR, 75% nervousness, 64% increased urine output, 32% GI distress, 30% headaches (p.164). Sleep effects (onset, duration, quality) worsen with dose; a 2023 meta-analysis found a standard coffee (~100 mg) needs ~9 hr before bed, and a standard pre-workout (~220 mg) needs ~13 hr before bed, to avoid sleep disruption; degraded sleep architecture can occur even if the person falls asleep fine (p.164-165).
- [CONVENTION] Use caffeine only in the morning — also supported by data suggesting caffeine may improve AM but not PM training performance; the only sensible exception is pre-competition where performance outranks that night's sleep (p.165).
- [SCIENCE] Tolerance develops with regular use; abrupt cessation can cause withdrawal (headache, fatigue, reduced focus, irritability, low mood, short-term performance drop). Habitual use doesn't appear to blunt caffeine's resistance-training ergogenic effect, likely because effects are both central and peripheral (p.165).
- [DISPUTED] Fat-loss claims: a 2019 meta-analysis found statistically significant but clinically negligible effects on body weight/fat mass; a 2024 meta-analysis found no significant effect of coffee (not isolated caffeine) on weight loss; neither examined resistance-trained populations specifically, so effect on body composition in that context is unclear (p.165).
- [CONVENTION] Dose/protocol: 1-6 mg/kg cumulative daily dose; most consistent ergogenic effect at ≥5 mg/kg taken 30-60 min pre-training; use the lowest dose that works since side effects scale with dose; finish 90% of daily caffeine >12 hr before bed; don't use before PM training; on non-training days limit to 1-2 mg/kg total with no single afternoon/evening dose >0.5 mg/kg (p.165-166, Table 5.2).

### Nitrate/Beetroot (p.166-168)
- [SCIENCE] Mechanism: dietary nitrate → converted to nitrite by oral bacteria → converted to nitric oxide during high-intensity exercise as fatigue accumulates. NO effects beyond blood flow: may enhance muscle contractility, raise cellular energy, reduce acute fatigue, lower the energy cost of exercise (p.166).
- [SCIENCE] Meta-analyses (2021-2024): nitrate doses ≥400 mg (6.4 mmol) acutely increase max reps-to-failure on bench/squat, explosive power, muscular endurance, and maximal strength (more notable in a fatigued state). A 2025 umbrella review of 20 systematic reviews/meta-analyses concluded muscular endurance and power are most consistently improved, with a slightly stronger effect for endurance (p.166).
- [DISPUTED] Long-term hypertrophy/strength-adaptation impact remains unclear due to limited research (p.166).
- [CONVENTION] Why Tier 2 not Tier 1: limited long-term data, AND a vegetable-rich diet (Level 3 recommendations) can supply ergogenic nitrate levels without supplementing — supplementation may be redundant. One study achieved ergogenic effects (≥500 mg nitrate) via a food-based nitrate-rich diet (40 g raw spinach + 80 g cooked collard greens) rather than beetroot shots (p.166-167).
- [SCIENCE] Only 5 of 45 tested commercial beetroot-juice supplements actually contained an effective nitrate dose — if supplementing, choose a tested product that guarantees nitrate content (p.167).
- [CONVENTION] Food sources high in nitrate: celery, beets, spinach, rocket/arugula, pomegranates. Regularly eating these while meeting Level 3 fruit/veg targets likely yields 400-800 mg/day nitrate — enough for ergogenic NO levels without supplements (p.167).
- [SCIENCE] Antibacterial mouthwash blunts/negates nitrate's ergogenic effect (it kills the oral bacteria that convert nitrate to nitrite) (p.167).
- [CONVENTION] Dose: 400-800 mg/day, achievable via diet as above (Table 5.2, p.169).

### L-Citrulline / Citrulline Malate (p.168-169)
- [SCIENCE] Another nitric-oxide precursor, via a pathway separate from dietary nitrate; may also delay fatigue via the urea cycle (clearing ammonia); the malate form pairs citrulline with the Krebs-cycle intermediate malic acid, potentially enhancing aerobic energy production. A 2020 meta-analysis found citrulline reduced perceived exertion and 24-hr post-training muscle soreness (p.168).
- [SCIENCE] 2019 meta-analysis: small but significant effects on strength, power, and muscular endurance. 2021 meta-analysis: significant positive effect on reps-to-failure. 2022 meta-analysis: small, non-significant positive effect on muscular strength (p.168).
- [DISPUTED] Long-term-adaptation evidence still emerging and less compelling than acute evidence; a 2023 meta-analysis (7 studies, overweight older adults) found small lower-limb strength improvement but no significant body-composition change (p.169).
- [CONVENTION] Tier 2 rationale: small but consistent acute effect on lifting performance (more for muscular endurance than strength), limited long-term-outcome research (p.169).
- [CONVENTION] Dose/protocol: 6-8 g citrulline malate or 3-4 g L-citrulline, ~1 hr pre-training (p.169, Table 5.2).

### EPA and DHA (p.169-171)
- [SCIENCE] Long-chain omega-3 PUFAs, essential fatty acids (body cannot synthesize sufficient amounts); roles in cardiovascular, immune, nervous, retinal, vascular, musculoskeletal function as antioxidants, signaling precursors, and inflammation/cellular-energy regulators. Most people (including athletes) on a typical Western diet consume low amounts. ALA→EPA/DHA conversion is inefficient (2-10%) (p.169-170).
- [SCIENCE] Fish oil general-health evidence: reduces depressive symptoms, lowers cardiac-death risk, decreases blood pressure, reduces waist circumference (meta-analyses) (p.170).
- [SCIENCE] Athlete-specific: modest benefits — improved post-exercise muscle recovery, small positive strength effects, potential fat-mass/cardiometabolic-health improvement; effect on skeletal muscle mass remains unclear (p.170).
- [DISPUTED] Effects are small/variable, partly because baseline dietary EPA/DHA intake alters how a given supplemental dose translates to blood levels; athletes may need higher intake (due to higher energy expenditure) to reach levels linked to better outcomes (p.170).
- [CONVENTION] Dose: 1-3 g combined EPA+DHA/day (adjusted to fatty-fish intake) likely sufficient to optimize levels over time (p.170). This meets/slightly exceeds the American Heart Association's 1 g/day recommendation but stays within their guidance to exceed 3 g/day only under physician supervision (p.170-171); aligns with FDA guidance not to exceed 3 g/day combined EPA+DHA, with no more than 2 g/day from supplements (p.171).
- [CONVENTION] ~2 servings of fatty fish/week ≈ 1 g/day EPA/DHA on average. For higher intake, favor low-mercury fish (salmon, herring, sardines, trout) over high-mercury species (swordfish, albacore tuna, dolphinfish, kingfish, shark) (p.171).
- [CONVENTION] Tier 2 rationale: modest effects, more health- than performance-critical, and diet alone can often suffice (p.170).

### Vitamin and/or Mineral Supplements (p.171-173)
- [SCIENCE] Formulations vary widely in dose, ingredient quality, source, bioavailability — "basic multis" are not a standardized class. Supplement users tend to already have adequate/optimal micronutrient intake from food (supplement use correlates with more health-conscious diets generally), so risk of excess intake from added supplementation is real (p.171).
- [SCIENCE] 2002 AMA guidance recommended vitamin supplementation for all Americans, citing deficiencies and lack of evidence of harm at the time (p.171).
- [SCIENCE] 2022 systematic review/meta-analyses (739,803 people, 84 observational studies) for USPSTF: multivitamin/mineral use associated with slightly reduced cancer risk, but NOT associated with reduced all-cause mortality or CVD risk. Beta-carotene (vitamin-A precursor) supplementation associated with increased lung-cancer and CVD-death risk; limited evidence of higher hip-fracture/stroke/kidney-stone risk with vitamin A/E/C and calcium supplementation respectively (p.171-172).
- [SCIENCE] Following this, USPSTF recommended AGAINST beta-carotene and vitamin E supplementation, and stated evidence is insufficient to recommend other isolated micronutrients or multivitamins/minerals on a benefit/harm balance (p.172).
- [CONVENTION] Tier 2 inclusion rationale: not blanket usefulness but potential utility for specific micronutrients in specific circumstances for specific populations. If following Levels 1-3 guidance, micronutrient supplements usually aren't needed (p.172).
- [CONVENTION] Populations that may benefit from targeted (not blanket) supplementation: clinical conditions, low energy availability, dietary restriction, menstruating, pregnant, training in heat/altitude, high training volume/intensity, combined strength+cardio training (p.172).
- [CONVENTION] Any micronutrient supplementation should be based on physician/RD-guided blood work or dietary assessment tied to actual deficiency symptoms/risk — NOT arbitrary "just in case" multivitamin use, which is not evidence-based and can be counterproductive/harmful (p.172).
- [SCIENCE] 2018 ISSN position stand: general micronutrient supplementation rarely improves health/performance, but targeted supplementation may help athletes with confirmed deficiencies (e.g., vitamin C for endurance performance in vitamin-C-deficient individuals) — the same supplement in a non-deficient athlete may be detrimental (p.172).
- [CONVENTION] Context-specific examples given (p.172-173):
  - Electrolyte (e.g., sodium) supplementation can help performance in specific conditions (heat exercise).
  - Iron: menstruating athletes doing aerobic training, especially if vegetarian, low energy availability, and/or confirmed low iron status.
  - Vitamin D: indoor athletes at high latitude in winter, especially if vegan, sedentary outside training, low energy availability, and/or confirmed deficiency.
- [CONVENTION] Dose/protocol (Table 5.2, p.169): as advised by an RD or physician based on an actual blood test — not self-assessment or assumption of deficiency without testing.

## Why Isn't ___ Supplement Listed? (p.173)

- [CONVENTION] The book takes a conservative stance given: high rates of label-claim failure, high contamination rates, unintended negative health effects, psychological-dependency risk, and generally small-at-best effects. Bar to reach Tier 1: overwhelming evidence of safety, minimal downside, reliable positive effect. Bar to reach Tier 2: multiple high-quality, large-scale meta-analyses showing safety, manageable downside, and consistent benefit in at least some contexts. Readers may apply a looser or stricter standard themselves, but the authors don't recommend it (p.173).
- [CONVENTION] The tier list evolves edition to edition — some 2nd-edition entries dropped, some new ones added. Named as "watch list" candidates that might rise to Tier 2 (or could just as easily fall to Tier 4 by the next edition): ashwagandha, betaine, collagen, ginger (p.173).
