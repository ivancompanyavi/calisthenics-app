# MSP Nutrition — Level 4 — Nutrient Timing

> **Source:** *The Muscle & Strength Pyramid: Nutrition* 3e (Helms/Valdez/Morgan), book pp.119-144. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/msp-nutrition.txt` or PDF pp.119-144.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] research-established · [CONVENTION] the authors' specific evidence-based recommendation · [DISPUTED] contested / authors flag uncertainty.

## Diet Periodization Spectrum (p.119-120, Figure 4.1)

Three intermittent-dieting strategies, ordered by length/structure — trade-off is potential surplus size / fat-storage risk vs. strength of evidence for attenuating metabolic adaptation:

| Strategy | Duration | Composition | Surplus/fat-storage potential | Evidence for blunting metabolic adaptation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cheat meal/day | A single meal or day of severely relaxed restriction | Any foods, uncontrolled | Largest | Weakest |
| Refeed | 1-3 days of elevated intake | Largely from carbohydrate | Moderate | Moderate |
| Diet break | 1-2 weeks | Neutral energy balance (maintenance) | Smallest | Strongest |

[CONVENTION] An intentional, planned untracked meal can be a smart adherence tool if used deliberately (not as a spontaneous "cheat") — no need for guilt when framed as a strategy in service of goals (p.119-120).

## Diet Breaks (p.121-127)

**Definition:** Longest periodization strategy, ≥1 week; obesity research sometimes runs breaks >1 month, but athletes/bodybuilders typically cap at 1-2 weeks due to competitive timelines (p.121). Best practice: implement in a monitored, structured way at maintenance, not just "unstructured eating" (p.121).

**Hypothesized mechanisms/benefits** [DISPUTED — mixed evidence]: enhanced lean-mass retention, supported fat loss, reduced metabolic adaptation, easier hunger management, better long-term adherence/maintenance via "practice" eating at maintenance (p.121).

**Key caveat:** Metabolic adaptation is largely driven by the magnitude of fat loss itself (Level 1), more so when dieting below one's normal body-fat range — some adaptation is unavoidable with successful, aggressive dieting (p.122).

### Evidence trail
- **Wing & Jeffery 2003** [SCIENCE] (p.122): 3 groups, 14 weekly sessions; two took diet breaks (3×2-wk breaks, or one 6-wk break) totaling 20 weeks vs. 14-wk continuous group (continuing on their own after). All groups lost similar weight by week 20 — planned breaks did not impede weight loss, unlike unplanned lapses.
- **Byrne et al. 2017 (MATADOR study)** [SCIENCE] (p.122-123): Men with obesity; diet-break group ate at maintenance 2 wks between every 2 wks dieting (30 wks total) vs. continuous (16 wks). Diet-break group lost ~50% more fat mass, only half the drop in resting energy expenditure (REE, adjusted for body comp), and at 6-month follow-up had retained 90% more fat loss than continuous group (which regained more).
- **Peos et al. 2021 (ICECAP trial)** [SCIENCE] (p.123): First diet-break study in resistance-trained, non-obese mixed-sex cohort; 12-wk continuous vs. 1-wk break/3-wk diet cycle (15 wks total). No body-comp/REE advantage found, but training performance, RMR, and fat-free mass transiently rose during breaks (likely glycogen/fluid), not retained after full diet. Better appetite control, diet satisfaction, reduced hunger, ↑PYY (satiety hormone) in break group.
- **Siedler et al. 2023** [SCIENCE] (p.123): All-female resistance-trained cohort, 6-wk continuous vs. 8-wk diet-break protocol. No body-comp/REE advantage; disinhibition (overeating tendency) increased in continuous group, decreased in break group.
- **Interpretation** [DISPUTED] (p.123-124): Byrne's larger effects vs. Peos/Siedler's null findings may reflect (a) better adherence in Byrne's outpatient cohort, or (b) diet breaks providing physiological benefit mainly when metabolic adaptation is substantial (Byrne: ~12kg vs ~8kg fat lost between groups; Peos: 2-3kg lost; Siedler: ~1kg lost, no adaptation observed in either group). Suggests diet breaks may matter more in scenarios of larger fat loss, e.g. bodybuilding contest prep.
- **Poon et al. 2025 meta-analysis** [SCIENCE] (p.124): 12 RCTs, 881 participants. No meaningful body-comp difference between diet-break and continuous groups; diet-break groups preserved ~50 kcal/day more REE on average. Effect larger in obese populations (avg 6kg fat lost) than athletes/lifters (avg ~2kg lost). **Overall: small at best.**

**Value beyond physiology** [CONVENTION] (p.124-125): even with modest body-comp/metabolic benefit, improved appetite regulation and adherence are valuable in their own right — especially in competitive physique prep where the main barrier to elite leanness is prep's difficulty/discomfort (binge/quit risk). For non-competitors, diet breaks give practice at long-term weight-maintenance eating.

### How to take a diet break [CONVENTION] (p.125-127)
1. Most useful for those dieting **≥3 months**, with schedule flexibility (no hard deadline conflict).
2. **Timeline rule:** add an extra week to overall prep timeline for every **4-8 weeks** of planned dieting, to allow room for diet breaks without rushing (p.125).
3. **Don't auto-schedule** every 4-8 weeks — use an autoregulated approach; take a break when diet fatigue/burnout appears, or a trip/vacation/holiday arises. Shifting the goal to simple weight maintenance during such periods is a valid, strategic diet-break use.
4. **Signals to consider a diet break** (checklist, p.126):
   - Diet fatigue: irritability, low motivation, food preoccupation.
   - Training performance declining despite solid nutrition/recovery.
   - Upcoming trip/holiday/social event where strict dieting adds stress.
   - Hit a mental/emotional wall; adherence feels much harder than earlier.
   - 4-8 weeks dieting without a break, weight loss steady but slowing.
   - Want to practice eating at maintenance ahead of post-diet maintenance.
5. **Execution:** goal = eat at maintenance. If unsure of maintenance level, a starting point is **+300-600 kcal/day on deficit days** (depending on maintenance level, body size, deficit aggressiveness) while cutting cardio to ~50% of normal volume or returning to pre-diet habitual step count (p.126).
6. Keep monitoring (weight, intake) during the break just as during the deficit phase.
7. Goal is to eat as much as possible **without gaining body fat**; any weight gain seen is predominantly water/glycogen, not fat.
8. **Duration: 1-2 weeks.**
9. If already running refeeds (see below), a break can simply be "every day is a refeed day," since refeeds already approach maintenance.
10. **Untracked/unstructured diet breaks** (stepping away from tracking, eating "normally") did not impair weight loss in Wing & Jeffery [SCIENCE] and may give a greater mental break than a structured at-maintenance break — but must be pre-planned, not an impulsive/unplanned burnout break (which resembles diet failure, not strategy) (p.125-126).
11. If the goal is specifically the physiological benefit (mitigating metabolic adaptation) without sacrificing fat-loss progress, staying close to maintenance (tracked if possible) is the better bet (p.126).
12. Weight trajectory during a break varies — gain, loss, or maintenance are all normal outcomes; loss can occur via reduced stress/water loss, ↑bowel movements, or a slight rise in expenditure (p.127).

## Refeeds (p.127-130)

**Definition:** Shorter/less-structured than diet breaks, longer than cheat meals; strategically redistributes calories (and sometimes macros) across days of a diet week. Synonyms in practice: carb cycling, calorie cycling, macro cycling, high/low days, cheat days (p.127).

**Two key implementation variables** (p.127): (1) how many days the refeed lasts; (2) how many calories are consumed during it.

**Core recommendation** [CONVENTION]: a **multi-day refeed at ~maintenance** is preferable to a single-day refeed in a large surplus, for body-composition outcomes (p.128).

### Evidence for mechanism/magnitude
- **Dirlewanger et al.** [SCIENCE] (p.128): 3-day, 40% surplus overfeeding (mostly carbs) in 10 healthy women → total daily energy expenditure ↑~7%, leptin ↑28%. A 40% surplus to gain 7% expenditure is a poor trade for fat-loss goals. **Fat overfeeding did not raise expenditure/leptin** — carbohydrate, specifically, drives the effect.
- **Glycogen replenishment**: full replenishment may require ~**10 g/kg (~4.5 g/lb) carbohydrate in a single day** [SCIENCE] (p.128) — e.g., 800g for an 80kg/176lb person — an impractically large surplus for fat-loss goals; better distributed across more days.
- **Luteinizing hormone (LH) pulsatility** (marker of menstrual-cycle stability, disrupted by energy restriction) [SCIENCE] (p.128): a single day of large-surplus eating did NOT restore LH pulsatility in one study; a **48-hour period at roughly maintenance** did restore it in another — despite similar net weekly energy intake between the two study designs. Suggests refeed length/pattern matters independent of total energy.
- **Psychological response** [SCIENCE] (p.128-129): comparing 6 days at 1300 kcal + 1 day at 2700 kcal (weekly total 10,500 kcal) vs. 7 days at 1500 kcal (also 10,500 kcal): similar weight loss, but the refeed group maintained self-regulation ability and had increased positive feelings week-over-week; continuous group's self-regulation and positive experience declined.
- **Campbell et al.** [SCIENCE] (p.129) — most directly relevant long-term study: trained, reasonably lean subjects, 25% net deficit for 7 weeks. Continuous 25%-deficit group vs. group at 35% deficit for 5 days + maintenance for 2 days/week. Both groups lost significant fat; continuous group also lost significant fat-free mass and had notable REE reduction; refeed group did not lose fat-free mass or reduce REE, and retained more "dry fat-free mass" (corrected for water/glycogen). Caveat: single study, small between-group differences, DXA measurement limitations on water/glycogen correction — can't be fully certain of true muscle-mass effect.

### Refeeds at a glance [CONVENTION] (p.129-130)
- **Length:** typically 2 consecutive days at maintenance.
- **Frequency:** after every 1-3 weeks of dieting (based on fatigue, adherence, or training demands).
- **Primary goal:** support adherence, psychological resilience, maintain training performance.
- **Energy intake:** raise to maintenance mainly via carbohydrate (not a surplus).
- **Expected outcomes:** better appetite control/diet satisfaction; temporary REE boost; possible fat-free-mass preservation (evidence still emerging).
- **Key caution:** structured, not a free-for-all/cheat day.

### How to implement [CONVENTION] (p.130)
1. Simplest approach: **2 consecutive refeed days/week at maintenance**, redistributing the weekly deficit over the remaining 5 days. E.g., a planned 500 kcal/day deficit across 7 days becomes a 700 kcal/day deficit on 5 days + 2 maintenance days back-to-back.
2. On maintenance days: cap fat at **60g** to keep carbohydrate-dominant, OR raise minimum carb intake from 1 to **2 g/kg/day** for more flexibility.
3. Alternatives given sparse direct research: single-day refeeds (smaller psychological benefit, less physiological magnitude); or **1-3 "floating" refeed days/week**, scheduled flexibly around social life/travel.
4. **Placement around training:** anecdotally, refeeds help recover training performance — sessions on refeed day 1 (evening), day 2, and even 1-2 days after often feel stronger. Total muscle glycogen resynthesis after exercise can take **up to 24 hours** [SCIENCE] (p.130) — training early on day 1 of a refeed may not yet benefit.
5. **Training/rest day calorie cycling when NOT dieting** [CONVENTION] (p.130-131): largely unnecessary at maintenance/surplus, because training-day vs. rest-day expenditure difference is small — a 90-min full-body resistance session (multiple sets of 10 to failure) burns only ~200-400 kcal depending on body size; 90 min sitting burns 100-150 kcal. So the gap is often only a couple hundred kcal. Only worth syncing higher-cal days with hard training when in an active deficit most days.
6. If reducing rest-day calories outside of active dieting, keep variations to **100-300 kcal or ~10%** — no strong evidence that larger cuts hamper training adaptation, but caution is reasonable since adaptation is an ongoing (not single-day) process.
7. For physique competitors: monitoring physique response to refeeds (peak on refeed day vs. days after) informs peak-week strategy (covered elsewhere in the book).

## Meal Frequency (p.130-132)

[SCIENCE] The claim that higher meal frequency raises energy expenditure ("boosts metabolism") and thereby enhances fat loss has been **repeatedly disproven** across multiple reviews/position stands/meta-analyses (p.130).

[SCIENCE] Meal frequency DOES affect appetite regulation, and to a lesser extent muscle growth/retention (p.130).

- **Practical trade-off**: very few meals leaves long food-free stretches; very frequent meals (every few hours) yields many small, unsatisfying meals and food preoccupation.
- **Appetite thresholds** [SCIENCE] (p.130-131): meal frequency at either extreme — **<3/day** or **>6/day** — can negatively affect appetite while dieting.
- **Outside dieting** [SCIENCE] (p.131): higher meal frequency (~6 meals/day) may aid weight gain, especially for those who struggle with fullness in a surplus — more frequent eating correlates with greater hunger/desire to eat and lower fullness ratings; among elite athletes higher meal frequency correlates with higher energy intake; more frequent meals associate with higher BMI. Likely why offseason bodybuilders traditionally eat ~6 meals/day.

## Time-Restricted Feeding (TRF) (p.132-134)

**Definition:** Eating within a shorter-than-normal daily window, typically **4-8 hours**, often 1-3 meals/day; colloquially "intermittent fasting" (p.132).

- **2023 meta-analysis, 15 studies, 927 participants** [SCIENCE] (p.132): TRF produced significant body-fat reduction, greater with **shorter** eating windows.
- **Mechanism** [SCIENCE] (p.132): TRF protocols don't explicitly instruct calorie reduction; fat loss results from an **unintentional/passive reduction in energy intake** — the main driver of both fat loss and most TRF health benefits. Useful for those who find deliberate calorie restriction hard, especially if kept busy outside the eating window.
- **2024 meta-analysis, TRF + resistance training, 7 studies, 164 participants** [SCIENCE] (p.132): TRF → avg **~175 kcal/day** passive reduction vs. normal feeding; similar strength/muscle-CSA/lean-mass outcomes between TRF and normal groups, but TRF groups had greater fat-mass/body-weight reduction. Most protocols used **8-hour windows** + supplemental protein for moderate-high daily protein.
- **Narrow-window failure modes** [SCIENCE] (p.132-133): very short windows (**4-hour** window) or one-meal-per-day approaches produced **suboptimal lean-mass retention** — too narrow to hit adequate energy/protein or good protein distribution.
- **Adequate-protocol success** [SCIENCE] (p.133): **8-hour windows** with sufficient protein (**≥1.6 g/kg/day**) evenly distributed across 3 meals, with resistance training performed fed, produce short-term strength/hypertrophy outcomes similar to normal feeding.
- **Long-term caveat** [DISPUTED] (p.133): a full year of even well-designed TRF may **blunt hypertrophy** compared to traditional feeding schedules.

### TRF implementation rules (for fat loss without sacrificing muscle) [CONVENTION] (p.133)
1. Feeding window of **at least 8 hours**.
2. Sufficient protein per Level 2 guidance.
3. **At least 3 protein feedings** within the window (e.g., 3 meals, or 1-2 meals + 1-2 protein shakes).
4. Train in the **fed state** (after at least one meal).
5. **Do not use beyond 2-3 months** if the goal is maximizing muscle growth/retention.

## Protein Distribution (p.133-134)

[DISPUTED — outdated] Older belief: moderate protein doses (~**0.25-0.35 g/kg**) every 3-4 hours was required due to a "protein refractory period" (muscle protein synthesis, MPS, can't be re-stimulated too soon after a dose). This was based on short-term lab studies with isolated fast whey protein in a fasted state, and short-term MPS measures don't necessarily predict long-term hypertrophy (p.133-134).

[SCIENCE] Real-world-pattern studies tracking MPS over longer durations show the **total MPS response (area under the curve) scales with protein dose** — larger protein servings digest more slowly, sustaining elevated MPS over a longer period; debunks the "excess protein in one meal is wasted" myth. Some limit likely still exists on how much/little protein per meal without efficiency loss (p.134).

- **Yasuda et al.** [SCIENCE] (p.134): group A — 3 evenly distributed meals each >0.3 g/kg protein (theoretically maximizes per-meal acute MPS); group B — low-protein breakfast (~0.12 g/kg) + only 2 protein-rich meals, but higher total daily protein (1.45 vs 1.3 g/kg). Uneven-distribution group had slightly inferior (not statistically significant) strength/lean-mass gains.
- **Taguchi et al.** [SCIENCE] (p.134): lightweight university rowers, muscle-gain phase, very high protein (**2.6-2.7 g/kg/day**), comparing 3 vs. 6 meals/day. Comparable body-composition outcomes — meal frequency mattered less across 3-6 meals when total protein is sufficiently high.

**Conclusion** [CONVENTION] (p.134): distributing total daily protein (per Level 2) across **3-6 meals/day** gives similar strength/hypertrophy outcomes when total intake is equated — conveniently aligning with the appetite-regulation/adherence meal-frequency range. Flexible, but a **consistent routine** aids long-term adherence and reduces decision fatigue.

## Peri-Workout Nutrition (p.135-138)

### Post-workout nutrition (p.135-136)
[DISPUTED — outdated] The "fast is better, narrow anabolic window" belief was based on short-term lab studies of protein-dosing effects on acute MPS post-exercise.

- **2024 meta-analysis, 237 participants, 79 studies** [SCIENCE] (p.135): muscle remains sensitized to protein intake for **up to 48 hours post-training** (window length depends on training protocol and individual responsiveness). Note: these studies measured short-term MPS response to isolated protein under lab conditions, not long-term real-world muscle gain.
- Whole meals (fat, carb, fiber, non-whey protein, larger doses) extend elevated MPS well into the next meal — when total protein/energy intake is adequate and meals are spaced across the day, amino acids are almost always available for muscle repair/growth (p.135).
- **2020 meta-analysis, 65 studies, 2,907 participants** [SCIENCE] (p.135): protein supplementation improved strength/lean mass **regardless of timing relative to exercise**.
- **Exception** [CONVENTION] (p.135-136): training first thing in the morning after an overnight fast (**>8 hours** without food) — prudent to consume a fast-digesting protein source (e.g., a shake) shortly after training. In all other cases, training between two meals at any time of day suffices to capture peri-workout protein benefits; choose a training time allowing comfortable digestion.
- **Post-workout carbohydrate**: accelerates glycogen restoration but this is **typically irrelevant for lifters** [SCIENCE] (p.136) — a moderate-carb diet, without specific post-workout timing, fully replenishes glycogen within **1-2 days**. Exception: athletes doing multiple exhaustive glycogen-depleting sessions same-day or on back-to-back days for the same muscle group (e.g., combining lifting with conditioning/endurance/sprint/HIIT work, especially while dieting on lower cals/carbs) — should consume carb-containing meals after training in these cases (p.136).

### Pre-workout nutrition (p.136-138)
[SCIENCE] Pre-/intra-workout carbohydrate clearly enhances endurance performance, but this benefit does not extend to isolated strength/power efforts, which don't rely heavily on carb metabolism (p.136).

- **King et al. meta-analysis, 21 studies, 226 participants** [SCIENCE] (p.136-137): pre-/intra-workout carb intake improved total training volume (sets × reps × load) during multiple sets to failure vs. placebo/no-carb. Benefits most consistent in sessions **>45 minutes** and when fasted **>8 hours** (e.g., morning training). Linear relationship between **number of sets performed** and magnitude of benefit; **no** linear relationship between benefit and **carb dose** — more carbs ≠ better performance.
- Mechanistic note [DISPUTED] (p.137): dose-independence challenges a pure glycogen-sparing explanation; resistance training's benefit from carbs may stem more from CNS support/perceived exertion than glycogen replenishment per se, unlike continuous endurance exercise.
- Low carbohydrate/glycogen/blood-glucose availability can cause not just local muscular fatigue but **central, neuromuscular fatigue** [SCIENCE] (p.137).
- **Carbohydrate mouth rinsing** (swish and spit, no ingestion) [SCIENCE] (p.137) — **2024 meta-analysis, 13 studies**: improves resistance-training volume performance; effect scales with session volume (more sets → larger benefit). Likely via oral carb sensors acting centrally.
- **Viscous/semi-solid breakfast** studies [SCIENCE] (p.137-138): a carb-containing OR even a non-caloric viscous placebo breakfast enhanced resistance-training volume and increased fullness similarly; a semi-solid carb breakfast enhanced volume more than a carb-matched liquid, also increasing fullness more. Suggests oral/gastric presence and appetite-blunting — not just carb/energy content — drive some of the effect.

### Pre-workout recommendations [CONVENTION] (p.138)
1. Train in a **fed state** after one of your meals.
2. If the session is long, high-volume, or particularly demanding, that pre-training meal should include **carbohydrates**.
3. Train **~1-2 hours after eating** (individual-dependent) to allow comfortable digestion.
4. If training with less food in the stomach and not hungry, a pre-/intra-workout carb drink (sports drink) still confers benefit.
5. If dieting and calories are tight: **swishing a carbohydrate drink in the mouth for 5-10 seconds and spitting it out**, at the start and halfway point of the session, may boost performance without consuming calories.
