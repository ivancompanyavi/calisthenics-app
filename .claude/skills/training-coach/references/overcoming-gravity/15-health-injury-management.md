# OG2 Ch.15 — Health and Injury Management

> **Source:** *Overcoming Gravity* 2e, Ch.15, book pp.195-211. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/og2.txt` or PDF pp.195-211.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] established research · [CONVENTION] Low's system · [DISPUTED] contested.

## Framing / disclaimer (p.195)
- [CONVENTION] No one-size-fits-all injury prescription exists — recovery depends on nutrition, sleep, general health, training schedule; individual care from a doctor/PT is "absolutely crucial." Info in Ch.15-16 is generalized and explicitly *not* medical advice; if a medical professional's guidance contradicts the book, follow the professional (p.195).

## "The burn," pain, and soreness (p.195-197)
- **"The burn"** [SCIENCE]: from metabolic acidosis — exercising at an intensity that exhausts the muscle's oxygen supply forces anaerobic (enzymatic) glucose breakdown, producing lactic acid as a byproduct; resolves with a few minutes of rest (p.195-196).
- **Pain** [SCIENCE]: complex, biopsychosocial (biological/physiological + psychological + sociological). Acute pain *may* (not always) signal overuse/injury; chronic pain often has no ongoing injury mechanism and is propagated by biopsychosocial factors rather than tissue damage (p.196).
- **DOMS (soreness)** [SCIENCE]: delayed-onset muscle soreness from unaccustomed exercise/stretching, onsets ~24h post-exercise, dull/diffuse, worsened by movement; decreased by prolonged movement/mobility or light exercise (increases blood flow) (p.196).
  - Typically lasts 48-72h, up to 7-10 days in extreme cases; most common returning from a layoff (p.196).
  - Triggers: (1) new exercises, (2) increased volume/frequency, (3) excessive eccentric work (p.196).
- [CONVENTION] Strength/hypertrophy progress does **not** require soreness or "the burn" as a signal — if you're progressing (strength, mass, goals), no need to change the routine regardless of soreness; if not progressing, change something even absent soreness (p.196).
- **Soreness guidelines** (p.196-197):
  - Too sore to move → train very lightly; hydrate, self-massage/foam roll (placebo effect may still help even though studies show limited direct benefit) (p.196).
  - Not too sore → train, but don't overdo it (p.197).
  - Otherwise ignore soreness; it should diminish as training frequency/capacity increases (p.197).
  - **Chronically sore after every session** → regimen likely insufficient adaptation stimulus (e.g., 1-2x/week body-part splits); [CONVENTION] fix by raising frequency to 3x/week full-body, which should dissipate the soreness (p.197).
- **Pain vs. aggravating exercises** [CONVENTION]: chasing pain (or its absence) as the rehab guide is a mistake — the better construct is the "aggravating exercise." Sometimes a painful movement still makes an injury feel/move better afterward; sometimes a pain-free movement leaves it stiffer/worse. Removing aggravating exercises matters more than tracking pain per se: if pain is present but the exercise reduces pain/improves the injury, keep it (p.197).

## Factors that affect propensity for injuries (p.197-199)
Four interacting factors govern tissue integrity/health:
- **Posture**: static presentation of the body.
- **Biomechanics**: the body's movement.
- **Mobility**: joint/muscle capability to move through range of motion.
- **Muscle length-tension relationships**: interactions generating movement force.
(p.197)

- [SCIENCE] Posture and biomechanics reflect CNS control interacting with environment: proprioceptive feedback (static/positional sense) vs. kinesthetic feedback (movement sense). Biomechanics forces continuous CNS recalculation as environment changes — this is why exercise is the primary way to teach correct movement; resistance training both trains the nervous system and strengthens muscle. Maintaining technique under fatigue significantly reduces injury propensity (p.198).
- [CONVENTION] Rehab for things like back pain often re-teaches fundamental developmental movements (rolling, crawling, squatting — the movements infants/toddlers learn from birth to age 3). Modern desk-bound/schooled life "practices" bad positions, degrading movement quality and raising injury propensity; regaining proficiency at fundamental movements decreases injury propensity via carryover from simple to complex movements (p.198).
- **Posture and pain** [SCIENCE/DISPUTED]: studies show bad posture does not always correlate with pain — MRI studies find 20-50% of healthy, pain-free people have bulging/herniated discs, stenosis, and other degenerative findings with no pain. Despite this, [CONVENTION] good alignment is still treated as relevant to quality movement and may indirectly reduce injury propensity (p.198).
- **"Caveman posture" (hunched shoulders, common with desk work)** [SCIENCE] mechanisms of harm (p.198):
  - Internal rotation → decreased subacromial space → increased impingement risk.
  - Posterior shoulder weakness → anterior instability → subluxation/dislocation risk.
  - Increased AC-joint stress moving into overhead position → negatively affects overhead work (e.g., handstands).
- [CONVENTION] Posture also affects cognition/behavior: taller/upright posture correlates with more confidence, decisiveness, perceived leadership by peers; also aids breathing and calm under stress (p.198).
- [CONVENTION] The body adapts to whatever it repeatedly does — including bad patterns, which then produce injury/poor posture/poor movement. Prehab/rehab protocol must continually address both biomechanics and posture re-teaching; correcting ingrained patterns can take more than a few weeks/months of consistent work (p.198).
- **Mobility mechanism** [SCIENCE]: kinesthetic feedback to CNS comes via muscle spindles (located in muscle, regulated by gamma motor neurons), which generate passive tension at end-range. Golgi tendon organs (at muscle-tendon junction) regulate muscle tension/voluntary force output. Both systems regulate posture and biomechanics (p.198-199).

### Four-step injury-management approach (p.199)
1. Goal Setting in Rehabilitation
2. Working Around Injuries
3. Programming Rehabilitation
4. Moving Back into Exercise

## Goal setting in rehabilitation (p.199-201)
- [CONVENTION] Functional goals are required (e.g., for insurance) and give a rehab timeline; a medical professional is generally needed to set these because individual response/healing time is often unpredictable (p.199).
- [CONVENTION] Absence of week-to-week progress in rehab is itself diagnostic — signals something is wrong, same logic as stalled training progress; rehab is described as "a lower-level extension of programming for regular strength and conditioning" (p.199-200).
- **Healing cascade** [SCIENCE] — three universal tissue-healing phases (applies to skin, muscle, even brain) (p.200):
  1. **Inflammation Phase**: immune system clears damaged tissue; growth factors released, initiating proliferation.
  2. **Proliferation Phase**: new tissue laid down via rapid cell division.
  3. **Maturation Phase**: tissue remodeled/matured to handle imposed stresses.
  - Example mechanism: training damages muscle cells → inflammation → satellite-cell proliferation lays new contractile fibers → maturation/remodeling into bigger/stronger muscle (p.200). Same cascade produces hand calluses from bar/lifting friction stress (p.199-200).
- [CONVENTION] "Too much, too soon" during this cascade → aggravation, blisters, wound reopening, scarring, re-injury (p.200).

### Chronological injury-management timeline (p.200-201)
1. **Determine cause of injury** — in bodyweight training usually a specific aggravating movement (e.g., elbow tendonitis from pull-ups).
2. **Eliminate aggravating exercises** — failure to do this is described as "the number one way to make the injury worse"; remove the exercise temporarily even if progressing on it.
3. **Get professional advice** — sports-med doctor/PT identifies the specific weak link/instability, shortening recovery.
4. **Follow the professional's plan**; ask for one (and home exercises) if not offered.
5. **Rehabilitate**, integrated with tissue-remodeling phase:
   - Inflammation is natural/not inherently bad unless excessive — don't exceed the prescribed plan; minimize inflammation.
   - Exercise (not pure rest) is important in healing for both acute and chronic conditions — rest alone is "the worst thing" for an injury.
   - During the inflammatory phase, continued exercise maintains ROM/joint mobility and deters atrophy.
6. **Exercise** — low weight/intensity, high reps, not-to-failure: [CONVENTION] rationale is to increase endurance, decrease re-injury risk, promote blood flow, and provide minor helpful inflammation (p.201).
   - Proliferation/remodeling phase balances: (a) delicate remodeling-appropriate rehab volume vs. (b) enough load to force adaptive remodeling/organization.
   - Exercise must progressively correct posture/biomechanics issues and reinforce good gross movement patterns.
- [SCIENCE] Tissue healing rate varies by tissue: skin heals in weeks; cartilage/labrum repairs may need 3-4 months of proliferation/remodeling due to poor blood supply — functional activities (e.g., throwing) aren't cleared before that time elapses (p.201).
- [CONVENTION] Self-check questions during rehab: is this measurably helping (week/month over week/month)? Is it prolonging recovery (too much or too little), and do I need a professional opinion on that? (p.201).

## Connective-tissue overuse rehab protocols (p.201-203)
- [CONVENTION/DISPUTED-note] A referenced chart (from the University of Maryland, Baltimore) on connective-tissue overuse — "(figure — see PDF p.201-202)" — underlies why cycling is favored for ligament/cartilage rehab (e.g., NFL sideline stationary bikes) (p.201-202).
- **Why cycling works for ligament/cartilage rehab** [SCIENCE], mechanism per factor (p.201-202):
  - **No impact** — ligaments/cartilage are the knee's last line of stabilization; re-testing them under impact right after repair risks re-injury.
  - **High-repetition** — increases joint blood flow, delivering nutrients for repair.
  - **Low-intensity** — applies gentle mechanical stress for remodeling; without this, connective tissue can realign in a disorganized cross-hatch pattern instead of linear (rope-like) alignment, raising re-injury risk.
  - **Longer time** — 10-20+ min sessions accumulate hundreds/thousands of low-intensity reps needed for blood flow and mechanical stress without aggravating the area.
  - **Not-to-failure** — avoids over-stressing/re-injuring healing tissue.
- **General rehab timeframe**: same as a mesocycle, 4-8 weeks — conservative for prehab, "spot-on" for rehab (p.202).
- **Prescriptions for overuse rehab** (p.202):
  - Stabilizer muscles (rotator cuff, scapular muscles): **30-50 reps, not-to-failure, 3-5 sets/muscle** — [CONVENTION] rationale: build endurance so stabilizers have less injury propensity, especially near failure (most injuries occur early in a workout from poor warm-up, or late from fatigue); progress slowly to avoid failure-range reps.
  - Stabilizers used in explosive movement (e.g., pitcher's arm, gymnastics/parkour/martial arts): Charles Poliquin's scheme — **30-100 reps/set for ~3 weeks**, then **8-12 reps**, then **6-8 reps at high tempo**, followed by specific strength + explosive re-acclimation work. Rationale: build endurance to reduce injury risk and increase healing blood flow before layering specific/explosive demands back on.
  - **Progressive rep-taper method** (works for most connective-tissue overuse, not just stabilizers): decrease reps / increase intensity weekly, e.g. **40 reps wk1 → 30 wk2 → 20 wk3 → 10 wk4**, transitioning into the 5-12 rep strength/hypertrophy range while still banking several weeks of high-rep blood-flow/remodeling work and reinforcing good movement patterns.
  - **Volume-ramp method**: start at **40% of prior volume**, add **~20%/week** until back to original load (~4 weeks to full intensity — gives tissue time to strengthen). On relapse: **drop volume 20-40%** (per how the injury responds) and re-ramp at **10-15%/week**.
- **Tempo for these rehab exercises**: **2151** (or similar tempos varying by ~1s: e.g. 1-3/0-2/4-6/0-2) (p.202-203).
  - Mechanism: **1-3s controlled concentric** limits rep speed to preserve technique focus; brief pause; **4-6s eccentric** (muscle lengthening) re-educates the nervous system to fire correctly and "injury-proofs" the tissue; end with a slight pause/rest (p.202-203).

## Why eccentrics dominate rehab (p.203)
- [SCIENCE] Eccentric loading is called out as the single most useful component for treating pain/injury, especially tendonitis/strain prehab-rehab, because:
  - It causes controlled muscle damage → triggers the inflammatory response necessary for healing (especially in chronic injuries).
  - It provides sustained, low-intensity stress that drives remodeling.
  - High reps + contraction cycle blood in/out of the area.
  - Eccentrics preferentially activate fast-twitch fibers — implicated in re-teaching correct muscle activation after injury (p.203).
- [SCIENCE] Injury pain → CNS perceives threat → protective muscle spasm/tightening around the injured area. Eccentric work re-trains the nervous system to lower the threat response and restore motor control; shakiness during early rehab work can be corrected with uniform-speed eccentric movement (p.203).
- [SCIENCE] Clinical use: not-to-failure slow eccentrics have produced 20-30° ROM gains in a single session for frozen-shoulder / impaired rotation patients. Proposed mechanism: eccentric loading lowers CNS threat response, desensitizing gamma motor neurons that govern muscle-spindle tension at end-range, allowing greater ROM (p.203).

### Systematic order of progression for regaining ROM/strength after injury (p.203-204)
1. Do not aggravate the particular area
2. Regain range of motion and mobility
3. Slowly begin to strengthen the area from all angles
4. Correct any imbalances
5. Move back into regular exercise

**Detail per phase** (adjust based on healing) (p.203-204):
1. Relaxation/soft-tissue work on afflicted area — with tendons/connective tissue, massage the *muscle* more than the tendon itself; overuse injuries typically involve muscles so tight that even light movement pulls on and aggravates attached tissue, so loosening the muscle helps significantly.
2. Light stretching/mobility work in all directions for the afflicted tissue (e.g., elbow tendonitis → stretch both wrist flexors and extensors, plus general mobility). Note: [SCIENCE/DISPUTED] stretching does not prevent injury, but can help recovery from an existing injury.
3. Train the affected area with no-impact, high-repetition, low-intensity, not-to-failure method.
4. Strengthen the *opposing* muscle group for joint strength/hypertrophy balance (e.g., golfer's elbow (flexor tendonitis) → reverse wrist curls for extensors; anterior shoulder pain → posterior shoulder/scapula work).
5. Slowly build back to strength/hypertrophy training from lower progressions.

- [CONVENTION] This approach is designed to address posture, biomechanics, mobility, and muscle length-tension relationships simultaneously; additional elements may be needed per injury (p.204).

### Execution factors for prehab/rehab exercises (p.204)
- Must not aggravate the working area.
- Full range of motion with good-form control — partial-ROM strengthening does not restore full operating capacity of the structure.
- Concentric and eccentric phases at smooth, uniform speed — no acceleration (unlike normal strength training); slow eccentric phase is the most important element.
- Quit if approaching failure or if the exercise aggravates the injury — goal is returning to exercise, not risking re-injury.
- [SCIENCE] Jerky/uncontrolled movement increases impulse forces on muscle/tendon/tissue, implicated as a significant factor in conditions like tendonitis; controlled eccentric training restoring smooth movement speeds/completes healing (p.204).
- [SCIENCE] Pain inhibits muscle function directly: noxious stimulus → feedback to motor cortex inhibits force production (independent of actual tissue damage) — golgi tendon organs act as natural motor-drive limiters when pain is present (p.204).

## Time estimates for recovery (p.204-205)
- [CONVENTION] Mnemonic for catastrophic-injury recovery time = number of letters in tissue name: **ligament (8) → ~8 months; cartilage (9) → ~9 months; bone (4) → ~4 months; muscle (6) → ~6 months; tendon (6) → ~6 months.** Explicitly only for catastrophic injuries — return-to-exercise strengthening takes "only a fraction of this time" (p.205).
- [CONVENTION] Progress low-intensity → high-intensity reps to rebuild to full original workload; ~1 month typical for minimal-to-moderate injury (Grade I strain, or 2-3 months of tendonitis); reducing routine volume for that month lets tissue calm and recover, then scale back up.
- [CONVENTION] For minimal injuries: rehab time ≈ one-quarter to one-half of how long the injury has been present (e.g., 4 months present → 4-8 weeks to full health). Depends on individual recovery factors: diet, sleep quality, stress, pain-free rehab capacity, individual response (p.205).
- [CONVENTION] Deliberately conservative protocol — better to spend extra time now to reduce re-injury risk; when in doubt, err conservative on volume (can always increase later; overdoing it risks re-injury) (p.205).

## RICE vs. MEAT (p.205-207)
**RICE** (traditional model) (p.205):
- Rest, Ice (reduce swelling/inflammation), Compression, Elevation.

**MEAT** (p.206):
- **Movement**: mobilize per pain tolerance (treats pain as normal, not something to avoid); movement prioritized over rest immediately post-injury.
- **Exercise**: goes beyond movement to actual rehab/prehab exercise — exclusively no-impact, high-rep, low-intensity, not-to-failure.
- **Analgesia**: control pain with medication rather than ice.
- **Treatment**: broad — includes doctor/PT modalities and alternatives (cortisone shots, hydrotherapy, contrast baths, ultrasound, acupuncture, dry needling, prolotherapy, platelet-rich plasma, extracorporeal shockwave therapy, etc.).

- [DISPUTED, book takes a position] MEAT is essentially PT rehab under another name, and the book argues research favors starting PT from day one (even day zero) — earlier PT start correlates with better outcomes. Exceptions: surgeries needing anchors/sutures to heal before PT begins, and any non-weight-bearing status injury (p.206).
- [DISPUTED] Case against RICE, factor by factor (p.206):
  - Rest is described as generally counterproductive (e.g., sprained ankle) — early movement within pain tolerance preferred; slow ROM movement can actually decrease pain; immobilization → longer recovery (stiffening, decreased motor control).
  - Ice: "hotly debated" — unnecessary if no swelling; if swelling present, compression is more effective than ice; ice's main use is pain-dulling, where analgesics work better.
  - Elevation: unnecessary if using compression.
  - Conclusion drawn: RICE "outdated and inferior" to MEAT.
- **Example protocol — inversion ankle sprain** (p.206-207): work into dorsiflexion and eversion (the *non*-painful directions); plantar flexion/inversion will be painful (mechanism of the sprain). Slow, continued mobilization into dorsiflexion/eversion typically reduces pain within ~10 minutes and may restore walking ability; if still unable to walk, continue with light massage mobilization. Contrast: RICE-treated patients often limp/need assistance for up to a week+; MEAT-mobilized patients (no ice, continued gentle mobilization into the evening) often walk normally, sometimes jog/run, by next morning.
- [CONVENTION] Reiterated: using MEAT without a medical professional is at the reader's own risk (p.207).

## Working around injuries (p.207-208)
- [CONVENTION] Core method: remove aggravating exercises and substitute alternative/supplemental exercises while continuing to actively rehabilitate (not just avoid) the injury (p.207).
- **Alternative Exercises** (p.207): swap a painful exercise for a pain-free variant (e.g., dips → pushups), or drop a couple of progressions on the same movement if the lower rung is pain-free.

### Intra-Limb Exercise (p.207-208)
- [SCIENCE] Atrophy's #1 driver is immobility/disuse — can begin within 1-2 weeks of joint restriction (e.g., a cast).
- [CONVENTION] Example: shoulder injury → use isolation work on the unaffected parts of the same limb (elbow/wrist/finger curls, forearm/grip work) to fight atrophy, provided a doctor hasn't contraindicated it; avoid exercises that use the injured shoulder for stabilization.
- [SCIENCE] Mechanism: using arm/forearm muscles sends neurological output through the arm as a whole; motor-neuron activity has a "radiating effect" that fights atrophy. Severing a motor nerve/spinal cord causes muscle atrophy within days, illustrating how critical ongoing neural drive is (p.208).
- [CONVENTION] Do as much light exercise/mobility as possible without irritating the injury — promotes blood flow, speeds healing, maintains ROM. Any movement that irritates the injured joint is a "major red flag" requiring immediate medical attention; consult the professional if prescribed rehab work is itself painful. Target feeling: end feeling better than at the start, or at least good overall (p.208).

### Opposing Limb Exercise (p.208)
- [SCIENCE] **Cross education**: unilateral strength/skill training on the uninjured limb transfers strength benefits to the opposite (injured, untrained) limb, even though that limb never trained.
- [SCIENCE] Magnitude: cross-education transfer is **~5-10%** strength — modest but valuable, especially past ~2 weeks of injury when atrophy/neurological strength loss risk rises.
- [CONVENTION] Prescription: **low volume, fairly high intensity — a few sets of 3-6 reps** with the opposite (uninjured) arm, mirroring typical strength work, to avoid creating strength imbalances between limbs (p.208).

### Unaffected Limbs and Other Areas (p.208)
- [CONVENTION] After exhausting injured-limb and opposite-limb options, continue training the rest of the body — but note some full-body compound lifts (squats, deadlifts) may interfere with healing; avoid anything that irritates the injury.
- [CONVENTION] Use the downtime to address lagging mobility/weaknesses unrelated to the injury (e.g., tight hamstrings/back; flexibility gaps for press handstand, V-sit, manna). Lower-body injury → shift focus to handstand/rings strength work; upper-body injury → light-intensity leg work for heart rate/blood flow to aid healing generally, plus balance/agility work. Framing: use injury downtime as opportunity, not just a stoppage (p.208).

## Finding a good medical professional (p.208-209)
- [CONVENTION] Look for a well-qualified orthopedic doctor, PT, or chiropractor who listens and connects with the patient's personality; sports-experienced massage therapists/personal trainers exist but are the exception — prefer professionals who work specifically with athletes.
- [CONVENTION] Specialization (shoulder vs. knee etc.) matters less than working with athletes and having a good reputation — those who work with college/high-school athletic teams tend to have the deepest orthopedic-injury knowledge (p.208-209).
- **Threefold plan for finding one** (p.209):
  1. Ask athletic friends/family for referrals.
  2. Contact local schools/universities and ask which physicians/therapists they use for athletic teams.
  3. Ask any medical professionals you know for the name of *their* doctor/PT — "doctor of doctors" / "PT of PTs" heuristic, higher success chance.
- [CONVENTION] A good professional explains not just what they're doing but the *why*/methodology; ask directly if unclear (p.209).

## Good/bad exercises and thinking critically (p.209-211)
Framework for evaluating exercise-safety claims (p.209-211):
1. **"Bad"/"worse" is always relative** — must be compared to a specific alternative and a specific goal. Example: one-arm chin-ups vs. two-handed weighted pull-ups — the former is "worse" specifically for shoulder-injury risk because of uncontrolled torque, not in absolute terms. If someone calls an exercise bad without naming a better alternative, they are likely just stating a preference, not a fact (p.209).
2. **"Bad"/"worse" implies a mechanism of injury** — must be able to name the actual physical mechanism. Example mechanisms given: uncontrolled shoulder torque in one-arm chin-ups → rotator-cuff dysfunction/tendonitis; rounded-back squats → posterior disc fluid pressure on laminal fibers → possible bulging/herniation. Note: naming a mechanism doesn't make a movement absolutely unsafe — rounded-back squats/deadlifts can be built up to carry minimal risk (p.209-210).
3. **Weigh anecdote, experience, and research appropriately** — one anecdote is one data point; 100 similar anecdotes becomes credible; research corroborating 100 anecdotes elevates the claim to reliably "bad" for that method (p.210).
   - Worked example: kipping pull-ups — even with limited direct research/experience, a plausible mechanism exists (fatigued body dropping into bottom pull-up position → rotator cuff/labrum injury risk), and injury-rate signal appears higher than standard pull-ups — sufficient to logically flag kipping pull-ups as elevated-risk, especially for beginners (unlearned technique), previously shoulder-injured athletes, and fatigued athletes — without declaring the movement "inherently bad" in all contexts (p.210).
4. **Exercise as cost-benefit / tool selection** — every exercise carries a risk/reward tradeoff (analogy: chainsaw vs. hand saw — more effective tools carry more inherent risk). "Dangerous" exercises may warrant delay until more experienced; research exercises to know inherent dangers and plan accordingly (p.210).
5. **Always fact-check claimed mechanisms** — a plausible-sounding mechanism can still be false. Example given: gluten — celiac disease is a real gluten-driven condition, but "gluten insensitivity" as commonly claimed appears not to hold up, even though some individuals do show real GI symptom improvement avoiding wheat/grain products (high in fructans, galactans, polyols, which can irritate the bowel) — so a food can cause real symptoms without the popularly-cited mechanism being correct (p.210-211).

## Chapter summary (p.211, book's own recap)
- Propensity for injury (acute or chronic) is driven by overuse tendency, pain location, tissue quality, posture/positioning, and biomechanics; a qualified medical professional should confirm any self-diagnosis.
- Diagnosis-first approach enables directly addressing the cause for faster recovery; absent a ready solution, "wait and see" is a fallback, not preferred.
- Chronological injury treatment (inflammatory → proliferation → remodeling phases) structures work on atrophy prevention, ROM, and strength.
- Rehab loading (sets/reps/volume) must match injury severity and functional interference; light, controlled, high-repetition eccentric exercise is generally most effective across injury types.
- Programming around injuries via intra-limb exercise, opposite-limb (cross-education) exercise, and unaffected-area exercise lets training continue safely; always actively rehabilitate the injury rather than merely avoiding/compensating around it.
- For non-critical injuries, MEAT is presented as more effective than traditional RICE.
- Also covered: finding a good medical professional; critical-thinking framework for evaluating "good"/"bad" exercise claims.
