# OG2 Ch.16 — Lifestyle Factors

> **Source:** *Overcoming Gravity* 2e, Ch.16, book pp.212-217 (chapter summary on unpaginated p. before 218).
> **Verify:** `_sources/og2.txt` or PDF pp.212-217.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] established research · [CONVENTION] Low's system · [DISPUTED] contested.

Framing: lifestyle factors are not the book's primary focus but are "very important to your training" (p.211/212).

## Sleep Quality (p.212-214)

- [SCIENCE] Sleep is "the most anabolic time during the day" — recovery and adaptation to the prior workout occur during sleep, so skimping on sleep is "a surefire way to decrease progress with both strength and hypertrophy" (p.212). Mechanism given: while asleep, the body prepares for the next workout (repair/recovery window), not merely rest.
- [CONVENTION] Needed sleep duration varies by individual, 6-9 hours; most people do well with **7.5-9 hours** (p.212).
- [SCIENCE] Sleep cycles run ~90 minutes; because of this, 7.5 or 9 hours of sleep is preferable to a flat 8 hours — waking mid-cycle (in the middle of deep sleep) causes grogginess, whereas waking during a lighter sleep phase does not. Rationale: align wake time to a cycle boundary, not just hit a target hour count (p.212).
- [CONVENTION] Recommends experimenting to find one's own cycle length and using sleep-tracking apps/alarms that wake you in your lightest sleep phase within a window (p.212).

**Sleep hygiene tactics (p.212):**
- Use a red-light-shifting display filter (e.g., F.lux) after dark — mechanism: blue/white light at night disrupts circadian rhythm.
- Limit electronic device use 1-2 hrs before bed; substitute reading or meditation.
- Remove electronics, outlets, and plugs near the bed.
- Sleep in a pitch-black room (blackout curtains or blindfold).
- Eliminate sound (earplugs) or use white noise if silence itself is disruptive for the individual.
- Sleep in a cool, dry room, ~60-65°F.
- Get to bed early enough to wake without an alarm; aim to be in bed before 10:00pm.
- Keep sleep timing **consistent** — a later but consistent bedtime is preferable to an inconsistent schedule (e.g., 10pm one night, 1am the next) (p.212). [SCIENCE-adjacent circadian-consistency claim, not separately cited]
- Avoid caffeine/stimulants before bed.
- Avoid alcohol/depressants before bed — alcohol may speed sleep onset but degrades sleep *quality* (p.212).

**Physical/behavioral techniques to improve sleep quality (p.213):**
- Single-leg standing to exhaustion (alternating legs) — fatigues hip muscles, purportedly improving sleep quality.
- Esther Gokhale's spine-lengthening routine before bed (sitting → stretching the spine while lying down).
- General physical exhaustion via hiking, sports, lifting, running, etc.
- Deep breathing exercises.
- Deep tissue massage.
- Light stretching or foam rolling.
- Long hot showers (cold showers work better for some individuals).
- Schedule exercise — especially high-intensity — earlier in the day rather than late, implying late high-intensity training disrupts sleep onset (p.213).

**Sleep supplements** (explicitly framed as adjuncts *after* trying the above natural methods first; "always consult a medical professional") (p.213):
- MCTs (e.g., from coconut milk)
- Magnesium — [CONVENTION/DISPUTED-nuance] noted to absorb better topically (Epsom salt / magnesium-salt baths) than orally
- Melatonin
- Phosphatidylserine — described as an anti-cortisol supplement
- 5-HTP (a tryptophan derivative)
- L-theanine
- Valerian root

**Two additional named tips (p.213-214):**
1. Psychological conditioning: reserve the bedroom for sleep or sex only — avoid conditioning yourself to be alert there (TV, intense games, studying), which makes falling asleep harder.
2. Author's personal breathing technique: recline comfortably; breathe with a 6-second inhale (nose) / 3-second exhale (mouth) cadence; roll the eyes back as if already asleep; actively daydream about something unrelated to daily life to prevent frustrated mind-wandering and ease the transition to sleep.
3. Andrew Weil's 4-7-8 technique (cited, not the author's own): inhale through the nose 4 seconds, hold 7 seconds, exhale through the mouth 8 seconds; repeat until relaxed/asleep. [SCIENCE] Mechanism given: elongated breathing + breath-holding calms the nervous system and slows heart rate, promoting relaxation.

## Nutrition (p.214-217)

Framed explicitly as not a nutrition book — the core recommendation is simply to eat nutrient-rich foods and avoid foods one is allergic/sensitive to, which "avoids the polarizing nutrition recommendations of so-called fad diets" (p.214). [CONVENTION]

- Nutrient-rich food categories listed: fruits, vegetables, nuts, seeds, meat, birds, fish, eggs, dairy, grains. These provide needed vitamins/nutrients with relatively low caloric density, aiding leanness (p.214).
- Carbohydrate staples: potatoes, sweet potatoes, rice, yams, various grains — ideally traditionally prepared (soaked) — assuming no allergy/sensitivity.
- Best protein sources: animal meats, especially organ meats.
- Fat sources: nuts, fish, dairy.
- If sensitive/allergic to specific foods, avoid them while maximizing nutrient intake from what remains; consult a nutritionist and/or allergy testing if reactions are significant (p.214).
- [SCIENCE, cited generally as "studies have shown"] Many athletes benefit from raising daily protein intake to **~0.7 g/lb (~1.5 g/kg) bodyweight**; for strength or hypertrophy training this or a slightly higher intake is recommended (p.214).

**Four general statements ("biggest concepts most athletes need to learn," p.214-215):**
1. **Diet modulates weight.** Nutrition governs bodyweight: caloric surplus → weight gain; caloric deficit → weight loss. To lose fat, cut calorie consumption.
2. **Exercise modulates body composition.** Exercise drives muscle gain and/or fat utilization for energy — especially under a hypocaloric diet. Athletes cutting weight for a weight class still train and eat enough protein to preserve muscle. Diet also modulates body composition to a degree (e.g., large fat loss in an obese person improves composition).
3. **Nutrition quality relates to health, and only partly to weight.** More nutrient-dense food → better health. Nutritional quality only partially affects the *speed* of weight loss under a caloric deficit, unless a metabolic dysfunction (e.g., thyroid problem) is already present.
4. **Exercise intensity improves the speed of body-composition change.** High-intensity modalities (sprinting, strength/hypertrophy training via barbell or bodyweight, circuit training) speed body-composition change more than low-intensity exercise (cardio, cycling, endurance sports). Fastest fat loss combines high- and low-intensity exercise.

- [CONVENTION] Debunks "high reps/low weight for toning" as false — toning is simply simultaneous muscle gain + fat loss; fastest route to "toning" is diet control + weight loss + hypertrophy-specific training (p.215).

**Calorie framework (p.215):**
- Calories In = what you eat.
- Calories Out = Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE) = Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) + Activity.
- BMR = energy required to keep the body alive at rest (mostly thermoregulation + organ function); Activity = the remainder of calories burned.

### Weight Loss (p.215)
- Shifting to low-calorie, nutrient-dense foods (vegetables) naturally produces weight loss; contrasted with the Standard American Diet (SAD), whose problem is low nutrients per calorie (e.g., chips, fries, sugary foods, modern processed grains like spaghetti/bread — noted as different a few decades ago).
- [CONVENTION] Eat until **70-80% full** rather than 100% full — filling, nutrient-dense foods aid deficit adherence; eliminate empty calories, especially liquids (juice, soda, lattes) in favor of water/tea/black coffee.
- [SCIENCE] Most people underestimate daily caloric intake, especially from liquids (150-180 kcal/serving) — hence tracking is recommended.
- [CONVENTION] Practical protocol: log food intake for a week (physical or online journal, e.g., FitDay), divide total by number of days to estimate weekly average burn; target **200-500 fewer calories** in subsequent weeks for weight loss. Alternative: use an online TDEE equation to estimate BMR + activity burn as a starting ballpark, then decrease from there.

### Weight Gain (p.215-216)
- [CONVENTION] Simple framing: "eat like a madman." Three required elements for gaining weight as muscle mass:
  1. **High-intensity stimulus** (weights, bodyweight strength training, sprinting) — forces the muscle-mass adaptation.
  2. **Caloric excess** — supplies energy for muscle synthesis.
  3. **Enough protein** — general target **0.7-1 g/lb of target bodyweight** per day (example given: 150 lb person aiming for 200 lb aims for ~200 g protein/day — figure appears as stated in source, though note it reads as "200 g/lbs," i.e., roughly the 0.7-1 g/lb range applied to the goal weight, not current weight) (p.216).
- Only difference vs. weight-loss protocol: increase rather than decrease caloric intake.

### Simultaneous fat loss + muscle gain (p.216)
- [SCIENCE/CONVENTION] Body fat is a mobilizable energy source: an overweight person lifting weights in a caloric deficit can draw on fat stores as the "extra" energy for muscle building. As body composition improves, this capacity diminishes. Cited as a reason resistance training can outperform cardio/high-rep training for simultaneous fat loss. **The higher one's body-fat percentage, the easier it is to lose fat and gain muscle simultaneously** (a diminishing-returns relationship as leanness increases).

### Workout Nutrition (p.216)
- [DISPUTED] Eating 1-2 hours pre-workout can help or hurt performance individually. Mechanism proposed: food in the gut may trigger parasympathetic activation, which can interfere with the sympathetic output needed for exercise performance — but effect is individual; some athletes prefer fasted training. Recommendation: experiment personally.
- [SCIENCE, cited] Referencing a meta-analysis by Brad Schoenfeld ("The effect of protein timing on muscle strength and hypertrophy"): protein-intake **timing** may matter less than **total daily protein intake**. Protein synthesis occurs in ~3-hour spikes in the body — cited as a reason distributing intake may still have some relevance, pending further research (p.216).
- [CONVENTION] Classic recommendation for those optimizing muscle mass gain: divide protein intake into **~30 g portions every 2-3 hours** (aligned to the ~3-hour synthesis-spike rationale above).
- [SCIENCE] Liquid nutrition post-workout reaches muscles faster than solid food — preferable but not strictly necessary. Whole chocolate milk cited as a strong workout drink because it supplies natural whey, casein, and carbohydrate energy together.
- Supplements framed as adjuncts to a good diet, not substitutes. Supplements "proven to work effectively": **whey, creatine, casein, BCAAs** (p.216).
- [SCIENCE] Research has "consistently shown" multivitamins/minerals underperform whole food — prioritize nutrient-rich food. Particular emphasis on ensuring adequate **vitamin D** (sunlight) and **fish oil**. Caution against self-diagnosing deficiencies: insufficient long-term data on supplementing without an actual deficiency. Refers reader to examine.com for current unbiased supplement research (p.216).

## Working Out While Sick (p.216-218)

- [DISPUTED] Explicitly framed as controversial. Core tension: high-intensity training stresses the body, and the same immune system that repairs training-induced damage also fights pathogens — so training hard while sick can slow recovery from illness. Anecdotal pattern noted: people who train while sick get sicker; people nearly recovered who train risk relapse a few days later (p.216-217).
- [CONVENTION] General guidance: avoid resistance training, HIIT, circuit training, or sprinting while sick or recovering — let the body fully direct resources to recovery rather than splitting effort with post-workout muscle repair.
- If training anyway: low-intensity resistance/strength work can be acceptable if muscle damage is minimal; high-rep endurance training can also be acceptable if overall volume is not too high.
- [CONVENTION] Specifically flagged as most damaging and to be avoided: **moderate-to-heavy weight, moderate reps (6-12 range)** — the classic hypertrophy rep range causes the most muscle damage, compounding recovery burden while sick.
- [CONVENTION] Widely-held rule: okay to train through a head cold (kept low-intensity); do not train if illness is in the throat, chest, or lower body. Never train with a fever — the body needs all resources devoted to fighting infection.
- Chest illnesses (pneumonia, bronchitis) are more severe than head colds; doctors typically prescribe bed rest. Noted tension for athletes: total rest/immobility can itself worsen these conditions, and bed rest carries risk of nosocomial (hospital-acquired) infection. Some movement — a short walk to get blood flowing, or deep breathing exercises (especially useful if coughing) — is better than complete immobility, distinct from an actual workout the body "could not handle" (p.217).
- If training while feeling capable: keep intensity light (easy recovery run, warm-up routine); monitor closely and log how you feel. If prone to escalating intensity once warmed up, avoid workouts entirely until fully healthy.
- **General rule**: any exercise performed should leave you feeling *better* than before starting; if it makes you feel worse, stop and rest for the remainder of the day — recovery from illness, like training recovery, happens during rest (p.217).

## Chapter 16 Summary (as stated in book, p.217-218)

- Sleep quality and nutrition strongly influence the body; prioritize adequate sleep and sufficient nutrient intake.
- Diet modulates weight; exercise modulates body composition; nutrition quality relates to health and partly to weight; exercise intensity improves the speed of body-composition change.
- Weight change is a product of calories in vs. out. Athletes: aim for **0.7-1 g/lb/day protein**. Adjust caloric intake per weight-loss or weight(muscle)-gain goal.
- Supplements are not recommended as a general rule — prioritize whole-food nutrients — but can help if a genuine deficiency exists. Particular emphasis: adequate **vitamin D** (sunlight) and **omega-3s** (e.g., fish oil).
- If sick, avoid working out; if you do choose to train, keep it light.
