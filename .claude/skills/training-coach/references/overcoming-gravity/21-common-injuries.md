# OG2 Ch.21 — Common Bodyweight Training Injuries

> **Source:** *Overcoming Gravity* 2e, Ch.21, book pp.263-291. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/og2.txt` or PDF pp.263-291.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] established research · [CONVENTION] Low's system · [DISPUTED] contested.

**Preamble (p.263):** Chapter is explicitly not diagnostic. All content is conceptual/general; any real injury requires a doctor/PT. Info is not a substitute for individualized rehab.

## Tendonitis (p.263-269)

**Etiology [SCIENCE] (p.263):** Overuse injury — tendon's capacity to sustain workload is exceeded. Mechanism: connective tissue adapts *slower* than muscle, so fast progression or high volume can overload tendons even while muscles can handle the load. Causes include muscle imbalances, general overuse, under-recovery, sudden injury.

**Staging — "tendinopathy" [SCIENCE, DISPUTED] (p.263-264):** Literature debates whether discrete stages exist; book presents them as a continuum rather than measurable stages (can't distinguish tendinitis vs. tendinosis without biopsy):
1. Tendonitis ("-itis"/inflammation) — acute overuse; untreated, sore/uncomfortable ~1 week, or 1-2 months + progression to stage 2 if aggravated.
2. Tendinosis — chronic degeneration; inflammation fades as pain becomes full-blown (present even at rest).
3. Tendinopathy progression — partial/full tendon tears possible.

**Signs/symptoms by stage (p.264):** Stage 1: pain/soreness/achiness/tenderness during or after exercise, improves with removing aggravating exercise + mobility/prehab. Stage 2: tight/stiff tendon and surrounding muscle especially on waking; pain may recede once warmed up (deceptive — book warns work capacity should stay low regardless because warmth masking pain leads to overdoing it). Stage 3: see a doctor.

**Typical sites (p.264):** medial epicondylitis/golfer's elbow ← excessive high-intensity pulling; lateral epicondylitis/tennis elbow ← too much wrist extension; triceps tendonitis ← excessive high-intensity pushing; biceps tendonitis ← excessive high-intensity pulling or shoulder compensation; wrist tendonitis ← computer overuse/excess wrist flexion-extension; rotator cuff tendonitis ← excessive overhead work or low-control jerking movements.

**Corrective measures [CONVENTION] (p.264-267):**
- **Avoid aggravating exercises** — "will solve 95% of potential overuse injuries with no further steps needed" (p.264-265). Substitute or drop a progression rung; return as condition improves.
- **Mobility work over rest** [SCIENCE] — recent research favors light movement over total rest because it promotes healing without aggravating, prevents disuse atrophy. 1-2 weeks of mobility work should clear mild tendonitis (initial inflammation promotes healing). Return-to-load protocol: start at 40% volume, add 10%/week — the small increment is deliberate because re-aggravation is easy at this stage; back off immediately at any aggravation sign (p.265).
- **Self-massage** — cross-friction massage (perpendicular to tendon) and myofascial release (parallel to tendon) loosen the muscle so tension isn't constantly loaded onto the tendon. The old "increase blood flow to tendon" rationale is called false per current research (p.265). Massage the region between the joints surrounding the affected area (e.g., shoulder-to-wrist for medial epicondylitis; ankle-to-hip for patellar tendonitis). Dosage: 5-30 min/day if cleared by a doctor, mostly on muscle (p.266).
- **Heat/contrast baths** — supersedes ice per Ch.15's MEAT vs RICE discussion (ice only if swelling present, plus compression). Protocol: 2-5 sessions/day, 15 min each; contrast bath alternates hot/cold water every minute for 5-8 rounds (p.266).
- **Light stretching** — doesn't *prevent* injury (per studies) but is useful for pre/post-hab because pain causes muscle tightening. Stretch the **agonist** muscles (the ones attached to the injured tendon — they get short/tight from pain+overuse and add strain) and strengthen the **antagonist** muscles on the opposite side, correcting the underlying imbalance. E.g., medial epicondylitis: stretch forearm flexors, strengthen forearm extensors; patellar tendonitis: stretch quads, strengthen hamstrings (p.266).
- **Light eccentric exercise [SCIENCE]** — "the most effective protocol for rehabilitation of tendonitis," per consistent research; eccentric work increases collagen formation and normalizes dysfunctional tendon structure, and also re-educates the nervous system to fire correctly when pain-compensation has disrupted normal firing (p.267). Protocol: eccentric phase 5-7 seconds, start very light, tempo ~5121 (per Ch.15) — controlled-but-not-fast concentric with end-range pauses, longer eccentric. Start at 20 reps/set, build to 30-50 reps before increasing intensity/load — high reps let tissue adapt to workload gradually, reducing re-injury risk. Perform not-to-failure so as not to aggravate (p.267). Pain during execution is *not* an accurate indicator of "too much" — rehab can be effective with or without pain present, which is why a medical professional should guide the choice (p.267).
- **Loading progression order** — begin with ~40% 1RM open-chain exercises (e.g., eccentric wrist curls for elbow tendonitis, eccentric leg extensions for knee tendonitis), using the uninjured limb to assist the concentric phase; this lets very small, controllable load increases. Progress to closed-chain exercises (e.g., eccentric stair descent, negative pull-ups) only once open-chain tolerated — closed-chain uses more bodyweight, so progressing too soon risks aggravation (p.267).
- **Other modalities, evidence tiers [SCIENCE/DISPUTED] (p.267-268):** Eccentrics = only intervention with "strong evidence" (multiple RCTs). Others range weak→moderate→no evidence: dry needling, acupuncture, extracorporeal shockwave therapy, ultrasound, electrical stimulation, LLLT, PRP, autologous blood injection, prolotherapy/sclerosing agents, taping, surgery. Moderate evidence exists for shockwave therapy, some prolotherapy/sclerosing agents, and some PRP applications on *some* tendonitis types. Corticosteroids/NSAIDs excluded from the list because they may help short-term but produce negative long-term results [DISPUTED]. Note: interventions are body-region specific — what works for Achilles/patellar tendonitis often doesn't work for shoulder/elbow (lateral epicondylitis research is called out as especially poor long-term) (p.268).
- **Supplements** — only conflicting/no/weak evidence: glucosamine + chondroitin sulfate (3:2 ratio), MSM, cissus quadrangularis, SAMe, fish oil, vitamin C, hyaluronic acid, L-lysine + other collagen/tendon components (p.268).

**Planning a prehab session [CONVENTION] (p.268-269):** Integrate into regular routine (don't fully separate) — remove aggravating exercises, substitute easier progressions, use intralimb/opposite-limb/other-body-part training if the injured limb can't be used. Template:
1. Mobility work (warm-up)
2. Stretching (5-10 min)
3. Self-massage (5-15 min)
4. Light eccentric exercise (1-2 exercises, 1-2 sets × 15-20 reps)
5. Heat or contrast bath (10-15 min)

Worked example — elbow tendonitis (p.269): wrist circles/mobility → light 20-30s stretching per wrist position → light massage → wrist curls at 40% 1RM, 1-3×20 (up to 50) reps at 5121 tempo + antagonist strengthening (1-2 exercises × 1-2 sets × 15-20) → heat 10-15 min. Heat placement is flexible in the sequence.

Training around the injury — injured elbow doesn't mean full rest: legs, core, skill work, and corrective/mobility work for the injured area continue (p.269). Tendonitis at a "stability joint" (elbow, knee) often co-occurs with mobility/strength loss at the joints surrounding it (wrist+shoulder for elbow; ankle+hip for knee) which adds more stress to the injured joint — so work the two surrounding joints too (p.269).

**Return-to-heavy timeline heuristic [CONVENTION] (p.269):** ~1 week of rehab per month injured (e.g., 1 year injured → ~12 weeks to return to heavy/high-volume work). Progress from isolated high-rep work → lighter compound movements → heavy exercise, in that order.

## Muscle Strains (p.269-273)

**Etiology [SCIENCE] (p.269):** Strain = force on muscle exceeds muscle's ability to generate opposing force; most common in high-speed movement but can occur under sustained contraction too. Occurs more often late in workouts (fatigue reduces force-generating capacity). Risk factors: previous strain (residual weakness), static stretching right before athletic activity (desensitized muscle spindles let the muscle overlengthen), older age (less pliable muscle), poor flexibility/mobility, end-of-workout fatigue, general weakness, nerve impingement (reduced motor output → reduced force production) (p.269).

**Mechanism [SCIENCE] (p.269-270):** Muscle damage (incl. DOMS) and strains occur during **eccentric** contraction — even strains that appear "concentric" are actually occurring at the eccentric-to-concentric transition or from excess force tearing the muscle. Vast majority of non-catastrophic strains happen eccentrically: hamstring strains as the knee moves forward/foot lands (hamstring lengthening); back strains during deadlifts as the back rounds and erectors lengthen; biceps/shoulder strains coming down from top of a pull-up; kicking-sport strains after a hard kick as the leg travels up/forward.

**Practical implication [CONVENTION] (p.270):** at-risk individuals should be cautious at high intensity/speed, warm up adequately, and save static stretching for post-workout (exception: pre-existing flexibility deficits that impair technique should be addressed beforehand).

**Signs (p.270):** pain on both lengthening and contraction; sudden/sharp onset; located in muscle belly; swelling/bruising if severe; visible divot/gap if torn.

**Grading (p.270):**
| Grade | Description | Swelling/Bruising | Pain pattern |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Minor tearing | Little/none | Present in soft tissue, variable, may be eccentric-only |
| II | Partial tear | Likely swelling, variable-but-likely bruising | Both concentric & eccentric hurt, pressure hurts, ROM limited |
| III | Complete/near-complete rupture | Swelling + bruising | Divot/gap visible; emergency-room case |

Grade III → ER immediately; Grade II → ER advisable though not always necessary. Remaining guidance targets Grade I and low Grade II only (p.270).

**Acute/inflammatory phase (p.270-271):**
- Preventive baseline: get stronger if weak; increase mobility via static/PNF stretching post-workout if tight; older athletes must warm up properly and do static stretching post-workout only (unless technique requires otherwise). Always maintain proper technique — timed workouts should prioritize technique over speed (p.271).
- **Heat**: usable immediately if no swelling — blood flow to the area is beneficial. [DISPUTED] some controversy over heat-first for acute injury; ice acceptable if sticking to standard protocol. Use compression if swelling present.
- **Anti-inflammatories**: consult doctor; NSAIDs commonly prescribed for Grade I/II; chronic NSAID use risks stomach issues.
- **Keep moving without pain**: prevents tightening + nervous-system pain-sensitization. Never stretch a strain — that's typically how it was caused.
- **Self-massage**: light/superficial, directed toward the heart to clear swelling; do not push hard into tissue at this stage.

**Repair and remodeling phases (p.271-272):** Can overlap if managed well; body repairs/breaks down/lays new tissue; begins 48-96 hours post-injury (marker: swelling reduced, movement feels better). Steps: continue heat (blood flow, loosens tissue, more movement — keep moving pain-free), continue anti-inflammatories as needed, maintain self-massage (can go deeper without inflicting pain), add mobility work (stretch into discomfort but stop short of pain — going into pain risks aggravation). Session template: heat 10-15 min → massage 10-15 min → mobility work 5-10 min (p.272).

**Resuming exercise (p.272-273):** Start very light for control, to avoid re-strain. Isolation work if non-aggravating: low weight, few sets of 15-25 reps, ~5121 tempo (slow eccentric, controlled concentric, pauses). Any twinge → back off immediately. Favor higher reps for endurance since tissue is vulnerable when fatigued. For injuries that can't be isolated (e.g., lower back), use isometrics — non-weighted squats, back extensions, very light deadlifts/good mornings; reverse hyperextensions can help for back strains (use caution). Isolation work can often be daily if kept low-intensity and next-day feel is good — avoid fast progression. Progression order: isometrics → light compound → higher-intensity compound → full workouts, at individual pace.

**Preventative measures [SCIENCE — "popping sarcomere theory"] (p.272-273):** Prior strain raises re-strain risk, so: improve mobility/flexibility (integrated into warm-up/cool-down, soft tissue work, dynamic+static stretching where appropriate); make muscle more resistant to damage via repetitive eccentric work. Mechanism: eccentric exercise causes individual sarcomeres to distend as muscle lengthens (this is the damage); excess/localized distension = a strain, but the body's inflammatory response adds *additional* sarcomeres to the muscle, increasing future damage resistance. Practical application: bulk of prehab should be slow eccentric work, especially if the sport/routine demands explosive movement (example: sprinter with hamstring/groin strain uses eccentric hamstring curls with 6-10s negative, higher reps). Progress toward 6-10s eccentric phases on compound lifts (deadlifts, good mornings, hyperextensions, RDLs) before resuming explosive work; avoid aggressive load increases; maintain technique.

**Conclusion (p.273):** Grade I / low Grade II generally recover without difficulty; high Grade II needs the same approach but longer timelines through each phase; Grade III requires medical care. Key success factor is patience/discipline through the protocol stages, not shortcutting.

## Tension Headaches (p.273-274)

**Etiology [CONVENTION] (p.273):** If headache/base-of-skull pain occurs only during/shortly after exercise, likely cause is improper technique or excessive muscle tensing. A craned/arched neck shortens muscles at the base of the skull → cramping, or restricts blood flow to the brain → headache. (Headache from blunt trauma/accident or otherwise unusual → see a doctor — could be more serious.) Common during squats, deadlifts, pull-ups (straining to keep back straight → neck arch/crane, especially reaching chin-to-bar), also dips/handstand pushups with excessive neck hyperextension.

**Mechanism/why it matters beyond pain [SCIENCE] (p.274):** Neck arching/craning narrows the intervertebral space nerves emerge from, potentially compressing them and temporarily reducing force output to muscles (analogous to a limb "falling asleep" from impingement or blood-flow occlusion). Proper neck form both prevents injury and optimizes strength/power expression.

**Corrective measures (p.274):** fix technique; heat and massage to loosen neck muscles; mobility work (non-painful neck ROM before/after exercise); static stretching for particularly tight muscles. If persistent, see a PT/chiropractor/massage therapist — sub-occipital release massage technique specifically called out as effective.

## Costochondritis / Tietze Syndrome (p.274)

**Etiology (p.274):** Soreness/discomfort/pain at the sternum or rib-cartilage junctions (deeper chest pain → see doctor). Typically occurs in those new to rings/parallel bars/bodyweight training when pectorals are used intensely — RTO support work, ring pushups/dips, and pressing that stretches the chest, manifesting at the bottom of the movement (elbows behind body, hands at armpit level). Back lever, front lever, pull-ups, one-arm chin-ups can also aggravate. Mechanism: pecs attach at sternum and partly on costal cartilage; if connective tissue is undertrained, deep stretch movements can pull the cartilage origin away from the sternal facets → popping/soreness/pain.

**Corrective measures (p.274):** rest, remove offending exercises and substitute non-aggravating ones; keep muscles moving through full non-weight-bearing, non-painful ROM to prevent tightening and stimulate blood flow; massage to soothe and prevent tightening/pulling; heat once feeling better, to stimulate blood flow; anti-inflammatories/NSAIDs for severe cases, fish oil "good in all cases." Retest offending exercises after 1-2 weeks; extend the timeline if reaggravated.

## Thoracic Spine / Scapula / Ribs (p.274-277)

General rule: any scapula/shoulder pain warrants a PT/medical evaluation, especially with chronic thoracic-spine/scapula/rib issues — pain source may be remote from pain location (p.274-275).

**Neck (p.275):** Pain within an inch of spine, with restricted neck movement → likely vertebral facet or rib issue (from awkward sleeping or fast movement). Usually self-resolves with stretching tight muscles and continued movement; massage/hot showers/heat packs help; mobility work in the non-painful range. If unresolved after a couple days or worsening → see chiropractor/PT promptly (delay risks the neck tissue becoming excessively tight, requiring more visits and training time).

**Scapulas (p.275):** Discomfort/pain around shoulder blades commonly from poor posture, weak back muscles, stress, routine imbalance (typically insufficient pulling work), weak scapular retractors. Mobilization: lacrosse/tennis ball rolling on tight areas; foam rolling and stretching of chest, anterior/posterior shoulders, back, lats. Bilateral issue → suggests postural/structural cause; unilateral → technique, overuse, dominant-side asymmetry, or scoliosis — self-diagnosis discouraged, see a professional (p.275).

**Band-and-wall exercise (figure — see PDF p.276) (p.275-276):** Two setups: (1) Theraband forcibly retracting the scapula(s); (2) stand parallel to wall, hand against wall extended outward, step toward wall with elbow behind body to force scapular retraction. Three-step sequence performed in this position:
1. Deep breathing — inhale through nose 4s, exhale through mouth 8s (optionally hold breath 5s + puff chest for tension then release) — builds and releases tension to loosen the anterior shoulder.
2. Scapular retraction holds — pull shoulder blades toward spine, hold 10s, repeat 3-5× per side — reorients muscles toward better posture.
3. Shoulder blade shrugs — full elevation/depression range (~4-6 inches), pause 3-5s at top and bottom.

Rationale (p.276): the exercise deliberately over-exaggerates alignment so the body settles into a more neutral posture afterward; deep breathing relaxes/releases tension; the retraction+elevation/depression sequence re-orients muscles to proper resting length. Reported effective for gymnastics, parkour, climbing, and Olympic lifters' overhead position. If dysfunction persists after a week, see a medical professional.

## Lower Back (p.276-280)

**General framing [SCIENCE] (p.276):** Back injury usually comes with a correctable mechanical issue (even permanent mechanical issues are typically not disabling — cites studies that many non-athletes have asymptomatic bulging/herniated discs). Pain triggers a dysfunction/spasm feedback loop: pain "turns off" segmental spinal stabilizers (notably transverse abdominis timing), body compensates by over-recruiting "global" muscles (e.g. spinal erectors) for stability, which explains why global muscles feel tight after back injury and why foam rolling/massage alone doesn't fix it — it's a stability/motor-timing issue, not a tissue-tightness issue. Fix: retrain correct timing of core activation via deep segmental-stabilizer work; tightness eases naturally as timing corrects.

**Rehab exercise rationale [SCIENCE] (p.276-277):** Many rehab exercises mirror toddler developmental movements (rolling, crawling, head control) because a toddler's nervous system hasn't yet learned to coordinate these fundamental patterns — same re-learning applies to adult rehab. E.g., segmental rolling activates the core with correct timing and teaches independent inter-vertebral stabilizer firing. Progression: activate muscles at proper time → stabilize the injured area → isolation work → compound movements, back to baseline.

**Etiology of general lower back pain (p.277):** Common in lifters and non-lifters; contributing factors: footwear, sitting posture, heavy lifting, poor posture, biomechanics — combined with poor technique, risk compounds "exponentially." Compound bodyweight lower-body work (squats/deadlifts) can cause issues; pure bodyweight exercise rarely does if technique is good, but rounding the back during stretching is a common cause.

**Flexion-based injury mechanism [SCIENCE] (p.277):** occurs from lower back rounding under load — the back is strongest with natural curvature; a rounded back under load puts discs at risk of failure.

**Three common categories (p.277):** bulging/herniated discs; SI joint slip or rotation; strained/pulled muscles. Debilitating pain or neurological (sensory/motor) deficits → immediate medical attention (motor deficits → consider the ER) (p.277).

**Bulging/herniated discs (p.277-278):** pain centered directly on/around the spine; if low enough, can impinge nerve roots → radiating pain (radiculopathy), most commonly presenting as sciatica (buttock pain radiating into the leg, sometimes to the foot). Action: orthopedic evaluation (may need MRI) → typically PT before surgery/injections; avoid aggravation (risk of permanent condition); mobility work eases inflammation-related symptoms in the interim. Individual variation is large; exercises may help, hurt, or be neutral — try from this list and self-select (p.277-278): knee side-to-side rocking (lying, knees bent), crawling, segmental rolling, cat-camels, bird-dogs, glute bridges, side bridges, McKenzie method exercises, reverse hyperextensions.

**Reverse hyperextensions (figure — see PDF p.278) (p.278):** particularly effective for lower-back strength/stability/hypertrophy rebuilding and as prehab before returning to squats/deadlifts; "very high success rate." Light kettlebell swings can also help.

**General back-rehab principle (p.278):** aggravating-exercise-avoidance rule applies; pain is not an accurate progress indicator except in the initial days; non-painful mobility work is heavily emphasized because avoiding pain avoids the sensitization/tightening feedback loop; focus on ankle/hip mobility+strength and knee/arch/lower-back core strength+stability.

**SI joint issues (p.278-279):** Mechanism — during improper lifting/bending, one side may fail before the other (dominant-side asymmetry); if the load isn't deloaded or the muscle doesn't strain instead, the SI joint can slip/rotate. Also from uneven impact loading (e.g., asymmetric landings in gymnastics/parkour/martial arts). Location: pain just above/beside the "butt crack," can also present as sciatica/radiculopathy. Self-locating landmark: iliac crest (level of L4-L5 disc) → follow down/back to find the SI joint (~4 inches long). A slipped SI joint may show as one functionally shorter leg (asymmetric iliac crest height); rotated SI joint may show no leg-length discrepancy. Diagnostic heuristic for SI issue: pain 1-2 inches off-center from spine, 1-2 inches below the iliac crest, in the bony pelvic region (p.279). Action: chiropractor/PT; interim self-care: massage to keep muscles loose, heat near but not directly on the SI joint. [CONVENTION/caution]: self-correction of SI issues not recommended without experience — a correct technique should resolve the issue within 1-3 attempts; repeating an ineffective technique (e.g., repeated back-cracking) risks serious damage (p.279).

**Strained/pulled lower back muscles (p.279):** pain below the ribs, above the SI joint, more than two inches from the spine, in the muscle belly — typically spinal erectors (iliocostalis, longissimus, spinalis) but also quadratus lumborum etc. Doctor visit not required unless severe (tissue discoloration/need for painkillers → see doctor). Use the muscle-strain protocol described earlier in the chapter. Differential diagnosis triage (p.279): >2" from spine + muscle belly + not SI area → likely strain; within 2" of spine + radiating → likely disc issue; between iliac crest and butt crack, slightly offset → likely SI joint issue.

## Anterior Instability (p.280)

**Etiology (p.280):** Shoulder pain + sensation the shoulder could dislocate/sublux — caused by imbalance: overdeveloped anterior shoulder muscles, weak posterior shoulder muscles, weak rotator cuff/other stabilizers. Develops from disproportionate pushing-vs-pulling volume (or the reverse) — example given: a football player who bench presses heavily without matching pulling work, or a climber/swimmer whose sport emphasizes pulling over pushing (imbalance can run either direction).

**Detection (p.280):** large discrepancies between pushing and pulling levels on progression charts (>1 full level apart) signal likely imbalance.

**Corrective measures [CONVENTION] (p.280):**
- High-rep rotator cuff work, ~3-5×50, working down toward 5-12 reps as strength improves.
- Scapular work like LYTPs (detailed in next chapter).
- Skew training ratio toward the underworked direction — e.g. 2:1 or 3:2 pulling:pushing if pushing-dominant.

Expect push/pull levels to equalize; monitor progression charts for new imbalances; shoulder feeling more stable during previously-imbalanced exercises signals correction is working.

## Shoulder Impingement (p.280-281)

**Etiology/mechanism [SCIENCE] (p.280):** Occurs under the acromion when soft tissue (muscle/tendon/bursa/fascia) is compressed between the acromion (scapula) and the greater tubercle of the humerus. Pain is superficial (deep shoulder pain is rarely impingement), located under the acromion at front/side of shoulder. AKA "swimmer's shoulder"/"painful arc syndrome" — occurs specifically in the 70-120° arm-elevation arc (0°=arm at side, 180°=fully overhead). Overhead arm movement normally requires external humeral rotation to prevent the humeral head rubbing the acromion; failure of this rotation, or muscle tightness/"binding" that prevents scapular upward rotation, causes impingement.

**Risk factors [CONVENTION] (p.280-281):** deadlift high pulls / upright rows (internally-rotated, upward humeral movement) create "ideal conditions" for impingement — not inherently injurious but riskier. Rotator cuff fatigue lets the humeral head ride up into the acromion. Predisposing genetic factor: oddly-shaped acromions (uncontrollable) combined with poor technique and risky exercise choice (controllable) (p.281).

**Higher-risk exercise categories (p.281):** overhead press, especially handstand pushups — flared elbows increase risk, more so with fatigue; this is why handstand pushup progressions are not recommended for beginners. Wide-grip/behind-the-neck exercises (wide-grip pull-ups, behind-the-neck pull-ups, iron cross) — place shoulder in a similarly vulnerable position as flared elbows, and fatigue the rotator cuff faster. Any pulling/pressing that internally rotates the humerus in abduction — rare in bodyweight training except possibly back-lever pull-ups. None are "inherently dangerous," just riskier; safe with sufficient technique caution (p.281).

**Corrective measures (p.281):** eliminate offending exercises → isolation work → gradual return to compound movements; add rotator cuff work and scapular work (LYTPs) as prehab. Rotator cuff loading progression: start endurance-focused (30-50 reps), then alternate with strength/hypertrophy work (5-12 reps) as it improves. Also: rest, non-painful mobility work, contrast baths; fish oil + NSAIDs if severe.

## Acromioclavicular (AC) Joint Issues (p.281-283)

**Etiology (p.281):** More commonly from impact injury than bodyweight training, but relevant to those with loose/previously-injured AC joints or doing explosive calisthenics. AC joints connect scapula to the rest of the body via the clavicle; normally near-immobile but allow slight movement/twist for scapular elevation and overhead reach. Sprain/ligament tearing → excessive AC joint movement → easily aggravated (p.281-282).

**Self-treatment protocol [CONVENTION] (p.282):** each step ~1-4 weeks depending on severity; healing depends heavily on diet and sleep.
1. Rest; massage surrounding muscles (prevent tightening, increase blood flow); fish oil/NSAIDs; heat if needed.
2. Once improving: non-painful mobility work + rotator cuff strengthening for shoulder stability.
3. If AC joint is loose: rotator cuff muscles tend to tighten (compensating for multi-joint instability) — stretch them regularly (internal/external rotation stretches) to maintain ROM.
4. Specific rotator cuff exercises: internal/external rotation, 3-5 sets × 30-50 reps, not to failure.
5. Gradual return to compound movements.

Caveat (p.283): this protocol is for pain/inflammation localized *at* the AC joint specifically; pain underneath or behind it may be a different condition (many nerves/vessels/muscles run beneath the AC joint) — especially with radiating pain, see a professional.

## Radiculopathy of the Arm / Radiating Pain (p.283-285)

**Definition [SCIENCE] (p.283):** Not one condition but a category — nerve irritation from impingement/inflammation causing weakness, numbness, difficulty controlling muscles, or radiating pain; can arise anywhere along the nervous system (spinal cord, nerve root at vertebral exit, thoracic outlet, brachial plexus, named nerve branches into arm/forearm/hand). Pain location can be remote from the causal site (e.g., hand pain from a wrist or neck issue). Strong recommendation to consult a professional before self-treating nerve issues (p.283).

**Nerve gliding / neural flossing [SCIENCE] (p.283-284):** Nerves, like muscles, can get bound down by scar tissue; studies show gliding/flossing can help avoid surgery in nerve-damaged athletes. Anatomy basis: brachial plexus (C5-T1 roots) → 5 named nerves; axillary and musculocutaneous nerves are short (near shoulder/arm); median, ulnar, radial are longer and thus more prone to aggravation. Definitions: **gliding** = moving one end (e.g. only the hand or only the neck) to stretch the nerve toward one side; **flossing** = moving both ends simultaneously so the nerve is "flossed" through surrounding tissue.

**Gliding protocol (p.284):** find symptom-provoking position → release tension by backing off until symptoms subside → glide by moving one component toward the spine and the other away from the spine simultaneously (e.g. median nerve: hand returns to neutral while head moves away, then reverse) → oscillate, staying below symptom threshold (should feel like stretch-level discomfort, not pain).

**Flossing protocol (p.284):** find symptom-provoking position → release by backing off at one end while adding tension at the other → simultaneously add tension at one end while releasing the other → reverse and repeat.

**End positions (figure — see PDF p.284-285):**
- Median nerve glide: arm out at 90°, slightly behind body, elbow straight, palm forward; pull wrist back until tension; if none, add head-tilt away.
- Ulnar nerve glide: arm abducted, elbow bent at shoulder height; wrist extended (palm up, fingers toward head); fully bend elbow, fingers toward ear; if no tension, try "eyeglasses" (externally rotate shoulder, thumb+forefinger frame the eyes); if still none, add head-tilt away.
- Radial nerve glide: hand at side, palm backward; fingers pushed toward floor from the shoulder; flex wrist ("bellhop" position); if no tension, add head-tilt away.

**Dosage (p.285):** ~2 sets × 10-15 reps, once/day initially, monitor next-day response before adding a 2nd session; can build to 3-5 sessions/day. Only tension should be felt — pain/tingling/numbness/cold sensation means back off. Tension may radiate along the whole neck-to-hand path or only part of it; some evidence of felt tension in chest/back/legs; sciatic-nerve gliding may produce tension felt in the arms too (p.285).

**Carpal tunnel as a case study in misdiagnosis (p.285):** One of the most commonly misdiagnosed wrist/hand conditions. Tight muscles elsewhere can impinge nerves and mimic carpal tunnel — implicated muscles: pectoralis minor, latissimus dorsi, subscapularis, pronator teres (plus neck/arm/forearm muscles generally); rounded-forward shoulders can produce carpal-tunnel-like symptoms via tensioning/imbalance near the shoulder.

**Correction protocol (p.285):** improve thoracic spine posture (foam rolling, lacrosse ball, soft tissue work) + wrist mobility sequence: heat → massage → wrist flexor stretching (in that order); may also need wrist extensor strengthening if imbalanced. Sample daily plan: 10-15 min heat + 10-15 min massage + 10-15 min wrist extensor strengthening + 10-15 min wrist flexor stretching/mobility, performed 1-3×/day, 3-5 days/week (p.285).

## Wrist Issues and Forearm Splints (p.285-286)

**Context (p.285):** Wrist health is critical in bodyweight training because wrists bear full bodyweight in many exercises; those without a weight-bearing-wrist sports background overuse them easily. Most common site: little-finger side of the hand — usually triangular fibrocartilage complex (TFCC) aggravation. TFCC functions like the knee meniscus (keeps the joint moving/functioning) and is aggravated by overuse or jerky movement.

**Workaround (p.285):** neutral-wrist techniques (support holds on parallettes/rings) if bent-wrist positions hurt too much.

**Three-step recovery process (p.285-286):**
1. Remove aggravating exercises; light mobility work; heat/massage as needed; anti-inflammatories if needed. Continue until full pain-free ROM (Ch.11 has prehab exercises).
2. Re-strengthen + regain ROM: loosen tight wrist extensors/flexors. Rice bucket (open/close fingers, rotate wrists) and seated flexion/extension are called out as particularly effective, plus wrist pushups and wrist curls; extensor-specific work recommended because flexors are usually already stronger than needed. No compound work unless pain-free.
3. Gradual return to compound exercises; re-evaluate explosive/power work carefully once wrist strength is regained.

Each phase: days to weeks depending on severity; use discomfort (not pain) as the guide — crossing into pain risks a significant setback. Beginner wrist exercises (from Ch.17) (p.286): stretch wrists palms-flat pointing in all directions (5-10 reps/direction); stretch wrists back-of-palm-down pointing in all directions (5-10 reps/direction); palms flat on ground, pulse fingers to lift palms off ground; one palm flat, use other hand to lift/mobilize each finger individually. If no improvement after 2 weeks, see a medical professional — typical overuse injuries should show improvement in that window, and failure to improve suggests something more serious (p.286).

**Forearm splints (p.286):** pain along forearm bone/muscle after finishing an exercise or isometric hold (not during) — sharp pain on release. Indicates weakness/imbalance, commonly flexors overly strong relative to extensors. Correct with rice bucket exercises, wrist curls, and other extensor-focused work. Can also manifest at the shoulder, or as shin splints in runners.

## Joint Cracking, Popping, Clicking, Snapping, Crunching (p.286-289)

**Cracking/popping mechanism [SCIENCE] (p.286):** Cavitation theory — joint capsule volume increases on stretch → synovial fluid pressure drops → dissolved gas forms bubbles → bubbles collapse (cavitate) → cracking/popping sound.

**Evidence on arthritis link [SCIENCE, DISPUTED] (p.287):** A 50-year single-subject case study of daily knuckle-cracking found no resulting arthritis, and "most of the literature" agrees. A conflicting study found joint manipulation (knuckle/back cracking) could damage cartilage and potentially hasten arthritis onset. Another study linked knuckle cracking to joint swelling and grip-strength loss, but confounded with manual labor, nail-biting, smoking, and drinking — suggesting personality/habit/stress traits, not cracking itself, may drive both the cracking behavior and poorer joint outcomes. No other cited study found significant long-term effects.

**Laxity-specific guidance [CONVENTION] (p.287):** People with more lax/"double-jointed" articular capsules should reconsider cracking, since swelling+grip loss correlated with cracking in the confounded study. Applies to hypermobile populations and situationally to pregnant women (elevated relaxin → increased ligamentous laxity). Lax joints generally are linked to lower strength and greater orthopedic injury risk (loose connective tissue reduces force transmission); common in sports with heavy flexibility demands (e.g. rhythmic gymnastics) since high flexibility volume stretches the joint capsule. Recommendation: those with looser joints should avoid intentionally cracking them, especially if cracking seems to progressively increase joint ROM over time. Strength training can partly compensate for lax joints/protect against injury (p.287).

**Degenerative cracking (p.287):** Involuntary cracking accompanying degeneration is called crepitus; if joints seem to be degenerating, don't crack intentionally.

**Back-cracking specifically [DISPUTED] (p.287):** tends to promote long-term instability. No strong evidence of harm from cracking other joints, but general heuristic: "much easier to loosen something than to tighten it" — so if in doubt, don't crack at all.

**Knee injury → osteoarthritis link [SCIENCE] (p.287):** Cites a study (Jonas Thelin et al.) finding knee injuries were the sole significant contributor to sports-related knee osteoarthritis risk once weight, height, genetics, occupation, and smoking were controlled for — reinforcing caution around knee injury generally.

**Joint clicking (p.287-288):** Pain-free clicking, especially longstanding, generally requires no action — common with lax joints, which strength work can address. New-onset clicking (not previously present) warrants review of recent activity to rule out a developing issue. Clicking generally reflects issues with posture, biomechanics, mobility, or muscle imbalance in limb joints (ankle, knee, hip, wrist, elbow, shoulder). Progressive/symptomatic clicking is concerning; chronic non-progressing clicking is not (p.288).

**Knee clicking mechanism [SCIENCE] (p.288):** Menisci (extra cartilage between femur/tibia) get impinged easily with any technique deviation. Quadriceps dominance / hamstring weakness (common with desk-job/sitting lifestyles) is a key contributor: if hamstrings don't engage properly during walking/squatting, the femur glides excessively anterior on the tibia, which (a) impinges anterior menisci → clicking + accelerated cartilage wear/osteoarthritis risk, (b) strains knee ligaments, (c) increases patellofemoral torque → over-recruits quads, worsening the quad:hamstring imbalance, and (d) may cause patellar maltracking (p.288). Other joints (ankle, hip, wrist, elbow, shoulder) can similarly transmit dysfunction to neighboring joints — injuries are rarely isolated.

**Corrective technique — box squatting [CONVENTION] (p.288):** helps counter quad dominance by cueing sitting back onto the hamstrings/glutes, shifting weight toward the heels, keeping shins upright, reducing patellofemoral torque, and reducing excess anterior femoral glide — also teaches proper squat pattern.

**Shoulder clicking (p.289):** 2nd most common clicking site; no extra cartilage like the knee, but greater ROM/mobility than any other joint makes it easily destabilized by posture/biomechanics/mobility/imbalance issues. "Anterior" clicking often signals tight anterior shoulder muscles/joint capsule limiting posterior humeral glide on the labrum — stretching those structures can eliminate the clicking by freeing posterior glide.

**Snapping/crunching (p.289):** Indicates actual injury — tissue rubbing/snapping where it shouldn't move that way (e.g. IT band snapping syndrome, triceps snapping syndrome; can also occur with nerves/tendons). Root cause again posture/biomechanics/mobility/muscle imbalance (same 4 factors as clicking). Palliative fix: soft-tissue massage + anti-inflammatories, but full resolution requires addressing all 4 attributes, generally via doctor/PT evaluation. If underlying connective tissue is structurally missing/damaged (e.g. post-accident), the 4-attribute fix may not suffice and surgery may be required. Crunching specifically suggests articular cartilage dysfunction/damage — needs medical evaluation.

**Quick-fact summary (p.289):**
- Cracking/popping does not itself increase arthritis risk.
- Those with lax joints should avoid cracking to prevent further destabilization; prioritize strengthening muscles/tendons, especially if laxity is increasing over time.
- Pain-free (especially longstanding) clicking is usually not a concern.
- New-onset clicking can escalate — assess posture/biomechanics/mobility/imbalances and address via strength, mobility, and soft-tissue work.
- Snapping/crunching signals injury — needs professional care.

## Muscle Cramping (p.289-290)

**Etiology [SCIENCE] (p.289-290):** Cramps most often occur during active flexibility work (e.g., active straddle compression, L-sit → quad/hip-flexor/ab cramping; high-level V-sit/manna → triceps + same areas). Also common in straddle work (glutes), pushing (triceps), pulling (biceps). Mechanism: muscles most likely to cramp when moved into a short ROM under strong contraction ("active insufficiency"); a lack of ATP (needed to release myosin heads from actin and pump calcium back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum) causes sustained intense contraction, which then cuts off local blood flow/oxygen, perpetuating the cramp (p.290).

**Management (p.290):** Cramps typically dissolve if you continue using the cramping muscle (analogous to soreness dissolving with continued exercise) — try foam rolling, static stretching, massage. Less flexible athletes cramp more easily; cramping is normal for beginners and decreases with progress. In-session strategy: keep performing active compressions even with cramping present — the body adapts to short-range contractions over the session, reducing cramp frequency; massage between sets if needed. Persistence through this eventually minimizes cramping issues.

**Cramping outside of exercise (p.290):** treated as a separate issue — check hydration and micronutrient intake, particularly magnesium, sodium, potassium. Magnesium specifically flagged as important for muscle relaxation and commonly deficient in modern diets; supplementation may be needed.

## Chapter Summary (p.291)

Reiterates the chapter's disclaimer: OG2 is not diagnostic; a qualified medical professional (doctor/PT/chiropractor) should determine what's actually happening before applying this chapter's advice. The pain-alleviating procedures described have worked well in practice, but bodies and injuries are individual, and undiagnosed injuries can be worsened by the wrong exercise or rehab technique — always seek medical evaluation before self-rehabilitating. All content is informational only; implementing corrective measures is at the reader's own risk.
