# OG2 Ch.2 — Physiology of Strength and Hypertrophy

> **Source:** *Overcoming Gravity* 2e, Ch.2, book pp.10-20. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/og2.txt` or PDF pp.10-20.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] established research · [CONVENTION] Low's system · [DISPUTED] contested.

## What Is Strength? (p.10-11)
- [CONVENTION] Core equation: **Strength = Neural adaptations × Muscle cross-sectional area** (p.10). Force output also depends on joint angle of muscle insertion and limb length, but these are untrainable so the book excludes them from the working equation (p.10).
- [SCIENCE] Neural adaptations account for the majority of strength training's effect; muscle cross-sectional area is the mechanism of hypertrophy. The two overlap substantially — bigger muscle is generally stronger muscle (p.10).
- [DISPUTED/addressed] Common beginner fear that added muscle mass hurts bodyweight-to-strength ratio is called largely unfounded: elite bodyweight athletes (gymnasts) and weight-class athletes carry large muscle mass relative to size; net-negative effects only appear at bodybuilder-level extreme mass. Absent PEDs, "you can never have too much muscle mass" for these purposes — strength and hypertrophy goals are non-conflicting in the long run (p.10-11).

## The CNS, Motor Units, and Muscle Fiber Types (p.11-13)
- [SCIENCE] A motor unit = one motor neuron + all fibers it innervates; each motor unit innervates fibers of only one fiber type (p.11).
- [SCIENCE] Motor unit / fiber type continuum, low→high threshold (p.11-12):
  | Motor unit | Fiber type | Color | Traits |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | LTMU (low-threshold) | Type I (slow-twitch) | Red (high mitochondria) | High endurance capacity; **least** hypertrophy potential |
  | MTMU (medium-threshold) | Type IIa | Pink | Adaptable — biases toward strength/power *or* endurance depending on training imposed; training specificity is "king" |
  | HTMU (high-threshold) | Type IIx (fast-twitch) | White | Fatigues fast (anaerobic only), contracts fast; **most** hypertrophy and strength/power potential |
- [SCIENCE] "Slow/fast-twitch" names reflect contraction *speed*, not just energy source (p.12).
- [SCIENCE] **Henneman's size principle**: motor units recruit smallest→largest. LTMUs are physically smaller and need lower activation potential, so they fire for light-force tasks; HTMUs need high force demand to activate (e.g., near-max lifts) — and during near/max effort, LTMUs remain active alongside HTMUs (p.12).
- [CONVENTION] Because HTMUs carry the greatest hypertrophy/strength/power ceiling and MTMUs adapt toward whatever quality is trained, strength/hypertrophy training should use heavy loads or difficult bodyweight movements to preferentially recruit HTMUs and push MTMUs toward HTMU-like qualities (p.12).
- [CONVENTION] **Tempo prescription and its mechanism**: move weight/bodyweight near-maximal, or move lighter loads with rapid/accelerating intent. If the movement is heavy/slow by necessity, execute with maximal *intent* to move fast even though actual velocity is low — this still recruits/fatigues HTMUs and trains MTMUs toward HTMU qualities. If intensity is light, accelerate through the rep for the same recruitment effect (p.12).
- [SCIENCE, tentative] Emerging evidence: high-repetition training may still yield solid strength gains provided high intensity is trained at least every other week — noted as useful for those managing overuse injuries or preferring higher reps (p.12).

## Neural Adaptations for Strength — Six Mechanisms (p.13-16)
Six primary nervous-system routes to strength besides hypertrophy (p.13):

1. **Recruitment** — increase in the number of motor units activated for a movement.
   - [SCIENCE] Golgi tendon organs at musculotendinous junctions provide inhibitory feedback that limits force output to prevent injury in untrained people; training reduces this inhibition, raising recruitment (p.13).
   - [CONVENTION] This effect is maximized around **85-90% 1RM (~3RM)**. *Why:* that intensity is what most strongly disinhibits the Golgi tendon organ brake and drives maximal fiber recruitment — hence advanced strength-focused programming often sits in the **1-3RM range** (p.13).
2. **Rate Coding / Firing Rate** — decreased time between electrochemical signals to muscle, raising contraction speed.
   - [SCIENCE] Begins once all motor units in a muscle are already recruited (via max effort or fatigue) — nervous system's next lever is firing frequency, not more units (p.13).
   - [SCIENCE] For large locomotor muscles, onset ≈ **90-92% 1RM (~3RM)**; in finer forearm muscles rate coding can start as low as **50% 1RM**; postural muscles (core, calves) lean heavily on rate coding (p.13).
   - [CONVENTION] Rate coding matters little for pure strength programming but matters for hypertrophy: muscles that rely more on rate coding tend to be slow-twitch-dominant and respond better to **higher reps** (forearms, calves, core). Two-joint, fast-twitch-dominant muscles (hamstrings, biceps, glutes) respond best to harder exercises at **lower reps**. *Practical implication:* alternate rep ranges/rest times per muscle group if one style stalls (p.13).
3. **Synchronization / Intra-muscular Coordination** — reduced lag between motor units firing together, vs. the random/staccato pattern of untrained recruitment.
   - [SCIENCE] Analogy: tug-of-war pulled in unison beats the same people pulling out of sync — same total force input, more net output (p.13-14).
   - [CONVENTION] Frequently repeated skills/exercises show the greatest recruitment/synchronization gains — this is the physiological rationale for **repeating movements multiple times per week**. Cited external examples: Rippetoe's beginners squat 3×/week; Bulgarian weightlifting protocol trains Olympic lifts 2-3×/day, 6-7 days/week; high-frequency technical practice is standard in gymnastics/running/swimming (p.14).
4. **Contribution / Inter-muscular Coordination** — how well timed and correctly the different contributing muscles fire relative to each other; this is essentially technique quality (p.14).
   - [CONVENTION] Rationale for prioritizing proper technique early: an untrained brain sequences movement (e.g., scapular stabilization before a pull-up pull) inefficiently; correct technique practice is what improves this coordination safely (p.14-15).
5. **Antagonist Inhibition / Reciprocal Inhibition** — reduced resistance from the muscle(s) opposing the one contracting.
   - [SCIENCE] Reflex analogy: striking the patellar tendon fires the quads while simultaneously inhibiting the hamstrings (p.15).
   - [CONVENTION] Rationale for **paired/superset sets alternating a push and a pull**: the natural relaxation of one muscle group after fatiguing it induces reciprocal inhibition that can enhance the antagonist's contraction (p.15).
6. **Motor Learning** — automatic, cortex-wide adaptation from repeated movement practice (motor planning areas, primary motor cortex, cerebellum).
   - [CONVENTION] Framed as not directly, consciously trainable — the actionable lever is *quality of attention*: "practice makes perfect" is corrected to **"perfect practice makes perfect"** — full concentration during practice teaches correct patterns; inattentive reps teach sloppy patterns (p.15).

## The Role of the Central Nervous System (p.15-16)
- [SCIENCE/CONVENTION] The CNS, like muscle, has a stress threshold needed to drive adaptation, and a finite recovery capacity (p.15).
- [CONVENTION] **"Pool" analogy for work capacity**: training removes water from the pool; sleep, rest days, nutrition, and recovery methods put water back *and* deepen/widen the pool over time (raising overall work capacity). Removing more than is replaced pushes toward overreaching/overtraining, where performance plateaus or declines (p.15).
- [CONVENTION] **Overreaching**, properly planned, is training past a plateau specifically so that a subsequent rest period yields a supercompensated return above prior baseline — this is the mechanistic justification for scheduled **deload/recovery weeks**: the pool's capacity has grown during the program block, but the water isn't topped back up until the deload is taken (p.15).
- [CONVENTION] Exercise-taxation ordering example: deadlifts recruit large amounts of musculature and are unusually fatiguing, which is why beginner programs place them near the *end* of a session — placing them early would degrade performance on everything after (p.16).
- [SCIENCE, hedged] **CNS fatigue** is explicitly called an "ill-defined concept" with no confirmed overarching physiological mechanism — candidate explanations include willpower depletion (a finite resource) and neurotransmitter depletion; comparison drawn to the mental exhaustion of hours of test-taking. Evidence cited: elite powerlifters can't heavy-deadlift multiple times/week; elite sprinters can't sprint at full intensity multiple times/week — no elite athlete gives 100% most of the time, which is why competitive routines are periodized to peak (p.16). The book explicitly flags this as an area to "take with a grain of salt" pending more research (p.16).
- [CONVENTION] Bodyweight-training analogue of CNS-taxing work: **supramaximal eccentrics and isometrics** (i.e., harder than a normal concentric rep — e.g., jumping to the bar and lowering slowly when a full pull-up rep isn't yet possible, or holding a weak-point position statically). These fatigue the body more than standard concentric reps; heavy weekly (or even single-session) use of these means a training plateau may signal a need for **more rest, not more work** — counter to the beginner instinct to add volume (p.16).
- [CONVENTION] Deload periods should be built in roughly **every 4-8 weeks** to allow full fatigue recovery; these double as connective-tissue (tendon/ligament) recovery windows, since connective tissue is typically the first tissue type affected by overuse — critical when progressing toward advanced bodyweight skills (p.16).

## Mechanisms of Hypertrophy (p.16-18)
Three primary pathways (p.16):

1. **Mechanical Tension** ("HTMU"/fast-twitch-fatigue hypertrophy)
   - [SCIENCE] Driven by high-intensity stimuli — heavy weights, fast movements. Sufficient high-intensity mechanical tension triggers mass added to compensate; inverse case is cast-immobilization atrophy from zero loading/neural drive (p.16-17).
2. **Eccentric Damage and Microtrauma** (popping sarcomere theory)
   - [SCIENCE] Intensity heavy enough to damage muscle, but light enough to accumulate sufficient reps/time to *do* the damaging — this cumulative exposure is termed **time under tension**, the physiological basis for talking about "volume" (total exercises/sets/reps per muscle group) as a hypertrophy driver (p.17).
   - [SCIENCE] Damage triggers satellite-cell donation/repair; satellite cells ("muscle stem cells") fuse with damaged fibers to repair them and are the proposed mechanism behind **muscle memory** — previously-fused satellite cell nuclei allow rapid re-acquisition of muscle after a training layoff (p.17).
3. **Metabolite Accumulation, Local Growth Factors, Hypoxia, Glycogen Depletion**
   - [SCIENCE] Driven by low-intensity, high-repetition/high-volume, sustained work. Cited examples: cyclists' large quadriceps, rowers' large backs, manual laborers' cumulative daily load, tradespeople (hammer/pipe work) developing large forearms from chronic low-intensity work (p.17).

- [SCIENCE][DISPUTED-resolved] The classic **sarcoplasmic vs. myofibrillar hypertrophy** distinction (sarcoplasmic via 8-20 rep metabolic accumulation → bodybuilder "large but less dense" muscle; myofibrillar via 1-8 rep strength work → weightlifter/gymnast "dense" muscle) is described as a decades-old belief now shown by recent research to be **likely a misnomer** — muscle biopsies show cellular components increase proportionally regardless of rep range used; visual density differences are attributable to hydration, subcutaneous fat, and intramuscular fat rather than a distinct hypertrophy subtype. Explicit takeaway: **"there is no such thing as myofibrillar or sarcoplasmic hypertrophy, just hypertrophy"** — the body adds usable muscle regardless of the rep-range route used to obtain it (p.17-18).
- [CONVENTION] Divergent programming for strength-athletes vs. hypertrophy-seekers still matters via **frequency and progressive-overload structure**, not fiber-type-targeting: gymnasts may train multiple sessions/day, 6-7 days/week (mostly skill work, with strength gains as a side effect since "strength is a skill"); pure hypertrophy seekers may prefer split routines that load high volume onto target muscles (p.18).
- [SCIENCE] **Isometrics and eccentrics** span multiple hypertrophy pathways simultaneously: isometrics bias toward mechanical tension + metabolic adaptation; eccentrics bias toward mechanical tension + eccentric-damage hypertrophy most heavily (p.18).
  - [SCIENCE] Both recruit HTMUs immediately (from the first rep) to sustain contraction, given the difficulty of the movement (p.18).
  - [SCIENCE][DISPUTED-noted] Counterintuitively, studies indicate **1-second eccentrics outperform longer eccentrics (e.g., 6 seconds)** for stimulating hypertrophy and strength — because fast eccentrics preferentially activate HTMUs (highest hypertrophy/strength/power ceiling), whereas longer eccentrics occlude blood flow and add time-under-tension that biases adaptation toward metabolic, slow-twitch-style adaptations instead (p.18).
  - [CONVENTION] Isometric hold duration has a **"sweet spot"**: too short → insufficient volume to force adaptation; too long → adaptation biases toward endurance rather than strength/hypertrophy. Exact durations deferred to later routine-construction chapters (p.18). The book flags these hypertrophy mechanisms overall as still being clarified by ongoing research — take with a grain of salt (p.18).
- [CONVENTION] Practical volume/frequency takeaway (p.18): there is effectively no "unwanted" hypertrophy except where it pushes an athlete out of a weight class. **Overall volume, in the context of frequency, is the dominant hypertrophy lever** — the volume threshold needed for further hypertrophy rises as muscle size increases (beginners need relatively little volume for visible hypertrophy vs. bodybuilders needing high volume). Frequency matters independent of formal "workouts" — occupational repeated loading (construction, furniture moving) produces hypertrophy from cumulative mechanical stress alone.
- [CONVENTION] Because the book treats rep-range choice as secondary to overall volume/frequency management, it deliberately does not prescribe one "best" rep range — but as a general anchor, **5-15 reps tends to give beginners adequate volume for hypertrophy** (p.18). Further reading pointer: Brad Schoenfeld and Mike Zourdos' published hypertrophy/volume research (p.18).

## Open and Closed Kinetic Chain Exercises (p.18-20)
- [CONVENTION] **OKC (Open Kinetic Chain)**: limbs move freely through space (weighted or not). Weighted OKC = isolation exercises (leg extensions, hamstring curls). Bodyweight/upper-body examples: biceps curls, triceps extensions (p.18).
- [CONVENTION] **CKC (Closed Kinetic Chain)**: the limb/hand/foot is fixed against ground or apparatus while the body moves relative to it. Weighted CKC: squats, deadlifts, Olympic lifts. Nearly all bodyweight movements are CKC — dips, pull-ups, pushups, handstand pushups, single-leg squats (p.18-19).
- [CONVENTION] Bench/overhead press are called a **hybrid**: hands fixed (CKC-like) but the load itself must be stabilized in space (OKC-like) (p.19).
- [SCIENCE] Comparing a semi-OKC press variant to a pure-CKC analogue (military press vs. handstand pushup): the CKC exercise tends to allow **greater strength output** — e.g., after subtracting arm weight, handstand-pushup-equivalent load typically exceeds comparable press strength. Attributed to co-contraction and increased kinesthetic feedback in CKC movements (p.19).
- [CONVENTION] **Strength application**: CKC exercises are more applicable for building upper-body strength, but progress tracking is harder than simply adding barbell weight (p.19).
- [CONVENTION] **Hypertrophy application**: CKC and semi-CKC barbell compound lifts (squat, deadlift, bench press) tend to work best for pure hypertrophy goals. Specific noted phenomenon: the overhead barbell press builds more muscle mass than the handstand pushup, because the handstand pushup demands much more body-stabilization effort, which reduces the force/mechanical-tension available for the prime movers — less hypertrophy stimulus despite being a harder skill (p.19).
- [CONVENTION] If the goal is purely maximal hypertrophy, primarily barbell-type training is favored as the faster route — bodyweight training can still build an impressive physique, "it will just take longer" (p.19).
- [CONVENTION] **Rehabilitation application**: OKC exercises dominate early rehab because they isolate specific weak structures without demanding full-body stabilization (e.g., ankle-sprain rehab starts OKC, progresses to CKC squats/standing drills as ROM and strength return; elbow tendonitis rehab starts with isolation work before progressing to pull-ups/compounds) (p.19).
- [CONVENTION] Summary framing: CKC exercises emphasize core/extremity stabilization due to ground/apparatus interaction, which is why bodyweight training leans on **progressions rather than added external weight** — though adding weight (e.g., weighted vest) does make CKC bodyweight progressions more linear/trackable. OKC's regulability via incremental weight makes it preferable when isolating an injury or specific weak point (p.19-20).

## Chapter 2 Summary (Book's Own Recap, p.20)
- Strength = Neural adaptations × Muscle cross-sectional area.
- Neural adaptations are enhanced by training near maximal intensity: recruitment, rate coding, synchronization, contribution, reciprocal inhibition, and motor learning ("growth and pruning").
- Muscle cross-sectional area increase = hypertrophy.
- For strength + hypertrophy: execute movements as explosively as possible with good technique, to recruit/stimulate HTMUs (fast-twitch, greatest strength/hypertrophy potential).
- "Practice does not make perfect; perfect practice makes perfect."
- Work capacity increases with training (bigger "pool"); training depletes it, recovery replenishes and expands it — programs should include planned deloads.
- Eccentrics and isometrics are effective but should not dominate a program because they are extremely CNS-taxing.
- Hypertrophy mechanisms: mechanical tension; eccentric damage/microtrauma; local metabolic/hypoxic factors. There is no myofibrillar-vs-sarcoplasmic distinction, "just hypertrophy."
- Beginners tend to do best with a **5-15 rep range**; for maximal hypertrophy, **5-20 reps** (and beyond, with programming variation).
- CKC movements are generally better for generating strength and muscle mass — CKC bodyweight movements skew toward strength generation, CKC barbell exercises skew toward hypertrophy generation (with overlap).
- OKC exercises are good for targeting specific weaknesses or rehabilitating injuries.
