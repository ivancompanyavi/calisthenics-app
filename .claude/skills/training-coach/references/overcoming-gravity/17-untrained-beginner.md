# OG2 Ch.17 — Untrained Beginner: Routine Construction and Progression

> **Verify:** `_sources/og2.txt` or PDF pp.221-230.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] established research · [CONVENTION] Low's system · [DISPUTED] contested.

## Introduction (p.221-222)
- [CONVENTION] The book's example routines are illustrations, not prescriptions — the actual goal is teaching how to *construct* a routine that fits an individual's goals, recovery capacity, and other activities, not to copy examples verbatim (p.221).
- [CONVENTION] It is normal/common for athletes (especially those with a weight-lifting or competitive-sport background) to show uneven strength vs. flexibility/skill development across the progression charts — not everyone tracks uniformly (p.221).
- [CONVENTION] Weak areas may be where the fastest progress is made, provided comparable time is invested across all aspects of development; working on weaknesses (not just strengths) keeps push/pull skill levels uniform and helps remain injury-free (p.221).
- [CONVENTION] Athletes progress faster in some exercises than others due to individual ability and anthropometry (limb lengths); response options are to decrease volume of the fast-progressing exercises or to play to strengths — either way, stay structurally balanced (p.221-222).
- [CONVENTION] Routine notation varies (exercise: sets x reps, or sets x reps of exercise, etc.) across coaches/athletes — readers must be able to parse multiple notations (p.222).

## General Needs of the Untrained Beginner (p.222)
- [CONVENTION] Core needs at this rank: (1) get introduced to fundamental exercises and attain good proficiency; (2) use higher rep numbers to solidify movement patterns and build connective-tissue strength; (3) focus heavily on individual weaknesses (e.g., desk-job posture issues, poor mobility common in sedentary individuals); (4) get on a generalized, balanced routine starting high-rep and transitioning to traditional strength training (p.222).
- [CONVENTION] Lowest-ability aim: develop basic strength, prepare joints for upcoming ability levels, and gain mobility — especially shoulder girdle, back, and hips. Untrained-beginner population includes those new to exercise or returning after time off, athletes of advanced age, those with previous injuries, or those with limited time to train (p.222).
- [CONVENTION] Upper-level skills (back lever, front lever, planche, other isometrics) should NOT be part of the routine at this stage — basics and high-rep work plus mobility must come first to prepare joints/connective tissue (p.222).
- [CONVENTION] Sample goals given (arbitrary/hypothetical, from the Ch.5 sample routine, to illustrate progressing toward a goal set — not literal targets to copy): 10 Freestanding Handstands, 5 Strict Muscle-ups, 5s Planche, 5s Front Lever, 10 Pistols, 10 Vertical V-Sits, 5s Iron Cross (p.222).
- [CONVENTION] In the Level 1-4 range of the progression charts, only a few exercises reach down to Level 1-2; at these levels the routine centers on fundamental upper-body work: wall handstands, lower-level L-sit progressions, pushups, pull-ups, dips, rows (p.223).

## Warm-Up (p.223-224)
Structure: Blood Flow → Mobility → Positional Drills.
- **Blood flow** [CONVENTION]: 10-20 burpees, 60s crawling (or ~100m variable distance). For untrained beginners, a pushup-burpee may be too intense and detract from the workout — squat thrusts are a better heart-rate/blood-flow option; for those very deconditioned or below Level 1, substitute jumping rope, jumping jacks, or light jogging (p.223).
- **Mobility** [CONVENTION]: tailored to the individual's needs/goals; sample list (15x wrist circles, shoulder circles, bodyweight squats, other needed joints, 60s support-hold work on bars/rings/chairs, 5x Skin the Cat/German Hangs) may be insufficient for someone untrained or with prior range-of-motion issues/injuries — those individuals need more. Extended wrist warm-up protocol given for computer users/previous wrist injury (p.223-224):
  1. Stretch wrists palms-flat, hands pointed in all directions; each direction individually, 5-10 reps in/out of position.
  2. Stretch wrists with back of palm on ground, hands pointed in all directions; each direction individually, 5-10 reps in/out of position.
  3. Palms flat on ground, pulse fingers into ground to lift palms off ground.
  4. One palm flat on ground; other hand lifts one finger at a time to mobilize each finger individually.
  - [CONVENTION] Mobility work can be time-consuming; if too much to fit inside a workout, batch it into a single dedicated rest-day routine — reduces workout-day mobility load and frees more time for skill building on training days (p.224).
  - [SCIENCE/CONVENTION mix] Rationale for lower-level flexibility/joint work: it accustoms joints to moving through end range of motion without heavy load, which prepares them to handle heavier loads later (e.g., transitioning standard handstands to one-arm handstands) (p.224).
- **Positional drills** [CONVENTION]: 30-60s of Plank, both Side Planks, Reverse Plank, Hollow, Arch Holds. Rationale: performed at low skill level specifically to build the habit of maintaining body tension/correct positioning before connecting exercises at higher difficulty — described as critical prep for performing advanced bodyweight exercises correctly; can be phased out as strength/technique improve, but vital early on. If training a sport/discipline needing specific positioning (martial arts, dance), practice those positions here too (p.224).

## Skill Work (p.224)
- [CONVENTION] Recommended skill work for untrained beginners (unless training for a specific sport requiring more): 5-10 minutes of handstand work against a wall — this figure includes rest time (p.224).
- [CONVENTION] Practical expectation: inverted holds may be only 5-10s per set in early sets; with 1-2 min rest between sets, total accumulated handstand time in a session may be only 30-45s initially, rising to 60s+ as strength improves (p.224).
- [CONVENTION] Handstand work stresses the wrists heavily for those unaccustomed to bodyweight-on-wrists loading. Mitigate with additional mobility work; if wrists remain weak, reduce handstand volume — may mean only 30s of handstand practice per workout for the first few months until wrist conditioning catches up. Framed explicitly as acceptable: better to build slowly and stay healthy than aggravate joints and need rehab, and progress will ultimately be faster by starting slower (p.224).

## Strength Work (p.224-227)
Baseline example routine (targets vertical push, horizontal push, vertical pull, horizontal pull; balances shoulder-girdle musculature) (p.224):

| Exercise | Sets x Reps | Rest | Tempo |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jumping Pull-ups | 3x5→15 | 3 min | 50x0 |
| Jumping Dips | 3x5→15 | 3 min | 50x0 |
| Ring Rows | 3x5→15 | 3 min | 10x0 |
| Pushups | 3x5→15 | 3 min | 10x0 |
| Squats (pistol progression or barbell) | 3x5→15 | 3 min | 10x0 |
| Deep Step-ups | 3x5→15 | 3 min | 10x0 |
| Tuck L-Sit | 60s total, as many sets as needed | not to failure | — |
| Compression Work | 3x10s | — | — |

- [CONVENTION] Changes made relative to the Ch.5 sample routine, and why (p.224-225):
  1. All exercises modified to the athlete's appropriate current level — e.g., starting at 3 dips, 5 pull-ups, 8 pushups, 9 rows, etc. Differences between exercise starting reps are natural; each is still individually progressed over subsequent workouts (p.225).
  2. An elongated 5s eccentric added to jumping pull-ups/dips specifically because these use a feet-assisted (jump-assisted) concentric — the added eccentric supplies useful strength stimulus that the assisted concentric doesn't (p.225).
  3. Rep ceiling raised from 8 to 15 across the board. [SCIENCE/CONVENTION] Rationale: untrained beginners need more total reps to strengthen connective tissue before making larger progression jumps; higher reps carry no detriment to progressing in either hypertrophy or strength for this population — hypertrophy is unaffected because *all* fiber types are capable of hypertrophy and need training regardless of rep range; strength gains for beginners come from performing many reps rather than specifically low-rep work, because beginners have inefficient neural pathways (lack of motor-unit recruitment and synchronization/rate coding), so the neural-efficiency advantage of low-rep near-max work is less relevant at this stage than simply accumulating volume (p.225). [SCIENCE tag applies to the neural-inefficiency mechanism; CONVENTION to the specific 8→15 number.]

### Prehabilitation, Isolation, Flexibility, Cool Down (p.225-226)
Example block: 3x1-min Rice Bucket (wrists), 3x10 Biceps Curls, 3-5x30s Splits Holds, 3-5x30s German Hangs, 3-5x20s Back Bridges, 1 min Deep Breathing (nose in, mouth out) (p.225).
- [CONVENTION] Amount of this work is individually adjustable up or down based on need. Use this slot for extra wrist work if handstands are aggravating wrists, wall slides/shoulder stretching if overhead position for handstand is inaccessible, scapular work (retractions/depressions) and rotator-cuff work for shoulder health, and back/leg flexibility (yoga/Pilates suggested if extensive mobility/flexibility is needed) (p.225-226).
- [CONVENTION] Isolation work for weak links is generally NOT needed at this point unless deliberately adding hypertrophy/strength work for a specific muscle (e.g., biceps) — priority stays on compound exercises to improve gross motor movement (p.226).
- [CONVENTION] An untrained beginner entering with unusually high existing strength could integrate basic static holds (wall/freestanding handstands, L-sits, possibly elbow levers) rather than staying purely at the fundamentals stage (p.226).

### Choosing a Direction at Levels 3-4 (p.226-227)
- [CONVENTION] As progression reaches Levels 3-4, exercise choice branches into a varied set of movements/statics: Wall HeSPUs, Back Lever, Front Lever, Planche, Muscle-ups, Rings Pushups, Dips and L-Dips, Pull-ups and L-Pull-ups, Rows (p.226).
- [CONVENTION] Explicit heuristic: don't chase all skills simultaneously — spreading effort too thin sets you back. Pick two pushing goals and two pulling goals and build the routine around them; select one overarching "theme" (p.226).
  - **Isometric-focused theme**: Wall HeSPUs, Back Lever, Front Lever, Planche, Muscle-ups, Rows (p.226).
  - **Overall-strength-focused theme**: Wall HeSPUs, Muscle-ups, Rings Pushups, Dips and L-Dips, Pull-ups and L-Pull-ups, Rows (p.226).
- [CONVENTION] Rationale for theme choice: if the primary goal is static-hold skills, build the routine around those isometrics; if training for a sport, bias the routine toward general movement strength and add static positions later once strength across many ranges of motion has improved. No universally right/wrong choice — depends on the athlete's own priorities (p.227).
- Warm-up, skill work, and prehab/flexibility/cool-down sections stay the same across themes; only the strength-work portion changes (p.227).

Example strength blocks by theme (p.227):

**Isometric-focused:**
- Jumping Pull-ups: 3x5→15, 3 min rest, 50x0 tempo
- Jumping Dips: 3x5→15, 3 min rest, 50x0 tempo
- Ring Rows: 3x5→15, 3 min rest, 10x0 tempo
- Pushups: 3x5→15, 3 min rest, 10x0 tempo
- Squats (pistol progression or barbell): 3x5→15, 3 min rest, 10x0 tempo
- Deep Step-ups: 3x5→15, 3 min rest, 10x0 tempo
- Tuck L-Sit for 60s total (as many sets as needed, not to failure)
- Compression Work: 3x10s
- X seconds of Frog Stand or SA Frog Stand (planche)
- X seconds of Tuck Back Lever or Advanced Tuck Back Lever
- 3x5→15 of Wall HeSPU or Muscle-up Work
- X seconds of Tuck Front Lever, or 3x5-12 of Wide Rows Progression
- Squats, Deep Step-ups, Tuck L-Sit, Compression Work repeated as above
- [CONVENTION] For isometric hold sets/durations ("X seconds," "Y sets"), refer to the isometric charts to determine set count and hold duration relative to the athlete's current maximal hold (p.227).

**Purely dynamic-focused:**
- Dips and L-Dips: 3x(3→15), 3 min rest, 10x0 tempo
- Pull-ups and L-Pull-ups: 3x(3→15), 3 min rest, 10x0 tempo
- RTO Pushups: 3x(3→15), 3 min rest, 10x0 tempo
- Rows: 3x(3→15), 3 min rest, 10x0 tempo
- Squats (pistol progression or barbell): 3x5→15, 3 min rest, 10x0 tempo
- Deep Step-ups: 3x5→15, 3 min rest, 10x0 tempo
- Tuck L-Sit for 60s total (as many sets as needed, not to failure)
- Compression Work: 3x10s
- Optional substitutions: Appropriate Muscle-up or Assistance Work 3x(3→15), 3 min rest, 10x0 tempo; Wall HeSPUs 3x(3→15), 3 min rest, 10x0 tempo (p.227).
- [CONVENTION] At this level, athletes may not need to work all the way up to 15-20 reps — if already accustomed to high reps, stopping at 12 can be helpful; however, if experiencing connective-tissue soreness or overuse risk, stick with higher reps specifically to train connective tissue without overloading intensity (p.227).

## Common Setbacks (p.228-230)

**Routine Balance** (p.228-229):
- [CONVENTION] Poor posture (e.g., from a desk job) → add an extra horizontal rowing exercise to build back strength/mass and counteract forward head/shoulder hunching; pair with targeted posture-correcting stretching. Poor posture doesn't necessarily cause injury directly, but correcting it improves appearance, feel, technique proficiency, and unrestricted range of motion (p.228).
- [CONVENTION] Athletes from a pulling-dominant background (swimming, rowing, rock climbing) may have the opposite imbalance and instead need an extra horizontal pushing exercise (p.228-229).
- [CONVENTION] Diagnostic heuristic for imbalance: compare progression-chart levels between pushing and pulling. If pushing sits more than one-to-two progressions ahead of pulling, or if pulling is at or above pushing's level, an imbalance likely needs correcting. Note: pushing tends to be naturally stronger than pulling, so some gap is expected/normal (p.229).

**Length of the Routine** (p.229):
- [CONVENTION] A full-body routine (recommended at this level because it includes built-in off days) may take a beginner considerable time to complete given how much it contains. It can be shortened by moving non-strength components (mobility, prehab, flexibility) to rest days — good for those wanting to train something every day (p.229).

**Tempo Modifications** (p.229):
- [CONVENTION] If technique breaks down on the X (acceleration/concentric) portion of a 10x0 tempo, switch to a 1010 tempo instead. Rationale: perfecting technique via uniform, controlled movement takes priority over strictly honoring a tempo prescription that compromises form (p.229).

**Strength Progression** (p.229):
- [CONVENTION] Linear repetition progression (5-5-5 → 6-6-6 → 7-7-7) is typically the best method at this level and can carry an athlete through most beginner levels up to Level 5 (p.229).
- [CONVENTION] If stuck after a workout or two on a given exercise, switch that specific exercise to repetition addition (5-5-5 → 6-5-5 → 6-6-5 → 6-6-6) or another progression method, while continuing linear progression on exercises that are still advancing normally — exercises are allowed to progress at different rates/methods simultaneously within the same routine (p.229). Example given: Pushups continue 5-5-5→6-6-6→7-7-7→8-8-8 (linear) while Dips shift to 5-5-5→6-5-5→6-6-5→6-6-6 (addition) (p.229).
- [CONVENTION] Rationale for uneven progress across exercises, even within the same muscle group: multiple factors can be at play, including fatigue carried over from one exercise blunting progress on the next (e.g., pushup fatigue slowing dip progress). Framed as normal — individual variation means no "one size fits all" rule for where a person will stall (some stall on pushups, others not until one-arm pushups or high-level planche work) (p.229-230).

**Paralysis by Analysis** (p.230):
- [CONVENTION] There is no perfect routine; "the best routine is the one you are actually doing." Starting an imperfect routine and adjusting as you go beats spending weeks designing a theoretically optimal one before starting — the person who starts immediately banks that many weeks of real training progress. Seek outside advice only after a routine is already running (p.230).

**Routine Mania** (p.230):
- [CONVENTION] Constructing a routine, trying it briefly, then abandoning it for a "new and improved" version is counterproductive because strength and hypertrophy both depend on progressive overload, which requires repeating the same exercises over time to progress on them. Sticking with an original routine beats routine-switching after only two weeks (p.230).
- [CONVENTION] Minimum recommended routine duration for beginners: four weeks (progress is naturally visible over that span). General mesocycle length: four to eight weeks (p.230).
- [CONVENTION] Untrained beginners may see some progress in as little as two-to-three workouts, but significant progress in physique or strength typically takes three to four weeks to become apparent (p.230).

**Overuse Modifications** (p.230):
- [CONVENTION] Untrained beginners often need substantial routine modification as issues surface. Common issue: progression pace outstripping connective-tissue tolerance — response is to raise the rep ceiling from 15 to 20, and/or add more prehabilitation and mobility work (p.230).
- [CONVENTION] No fixed universal amounts of prehab/mobility/flexibility work can be prescribed — individual variation is too high; the underlying concepts must be learned and applied per-person. Two-step process when an area (example given: wrists) is overworked (p.230):
  1. Eliminate, reduce volume of, or substitute the offending exercise(s).
  2. Add additional prehabilitation, isolation, mobility, or flexibility work for that specific area as needed.
- [CONVENTION] Example application: if handstands aggravate wrists, eliminate handstands until wrists recover, substitute other wrist-supported exercises (supports, planks) that condition the wrists without aggravating them, and add extra wrist-specific work (dumbbell wrist curls, rice bucket exercises) (p.230).
- [CONVENTION] If uncertain, consult a medical professional (orthopedic sports doctor, physical therapist), more experienced coaches/athletes, or online communities — book names r/bodyweightfitness and r/overcominggravity specifically (p.230).

**Avoiding Injuries** (p.230):
- [CONVENTION] Learning to modify a routine based on how the body responds — particularly regarding potential overuse injury — is framed as THE most important long-term training skill. [SCIENCE] The single strongest predictor of an injury is a previous injury (p.230).
