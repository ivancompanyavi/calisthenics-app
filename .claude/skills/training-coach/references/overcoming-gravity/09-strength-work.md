# OG2 Ch.9 — Strength Work

## Overview and template (p.90)
[CONVENTION] The general template for an effective full-body strength routine: two pushing exercises, two pulling exercises, two legs exercises (p.90). If combining bodyweight and barbells with additional goals (hypertrophy, power), that work also goes in the strength-work section. Power work (Olympic lifts: snatch, clean, jerk) is explicitly out of scope for this chapter (p.90).

Sample strength routine given as reference throughout the chapter (p.90):
- Pull-ups: 3x5→12, 3 min rest, 10x0 tempo
- Dips: 3x5→12, 3 min rest, 10x0 tempo
- Wide Ring Rows: 3x5→12, 3 min rest, 10x0 tempo
- Rings Pushups: 3x5→12, 3 min rest, 10x0 tempo
- Squats (pistol progression or barbell): 3x5→12, 3 min rest, 10x0 tempo
- Deep Step-ups: 3x5→12, 3 min rest, 10x0 tempo
- L-Sit: 60s total, as many sets as needed, not to failure
- Compression Work: 3x10s
- Planche Isometrics: 5x12s, 3 min rest
- L-Sit Pull-up Eccentrics: 3x(3x7s) or 3x3 with 10s eccentrics

Notation legend: Exercise, Sets x Reps with progressive principle, Exercise Order, Rest Time, Tempo (p.90).

## Types of exercises and notation (p.90-92)
Three contraction types (p.90):
- **Concentric** ("dynamic") — has both eccentric and concentric components; the concentric portion is hardest. Pushing movements (pushups, dips, HSPU) go eccentric-then-concentric; pulling movements (pull-ups) go concentric-then-eccentric (p.90-91).
- **Isometric** ("static positions") — muscle length constant throughout (planche, front lever, back lever, iron cross). Handstands are technically isometric but classified as skill work because their dominant limiting factor is balance, not raw strength — once strength develops, the trained attribute shifts to balance (p.91).
- **Eccentric** — slow controlled lengthening, typically performed with assistance to reach the top/start position (e.g., pull-up eccentrics: jump/step to top, lower slowly). A subset of concentric movements used when the athlete cannot yet perform the concentric portion but can still train the pattern and drive strength/hypertrophy gains (p.91).

Notation conventions (p.92):
- Concentric: Sets x Repetitions, e.g. 3x5.
- Isometric: Sets x Time Held, e.g. 3x10s.
- Eccentric: Sets x (Cluster repetitions x time of eccentric), e.g. 3x(3x10s) = 3 sets of 3 reps of continuous 10s eccentrics. Also written 3x3 of 10s eccentrics.
- Bodyweight training records Sets x Reps or Sets x Time (not Weight x Reps x Sets as in barbell training) (p.92).

[SCIENCE] Strength continuum across contraction types (p.92): isometric contraction ≈ 100-120% stronger than concentric; eccentric contraction ≈ 100-150% stronger than concentric. Percentages vary by training factors and muscle group. Low adopts working assumptions of isometric ≈ 100-120% and eccentric ≈ 120-150% of concentric strength to derive volume-equivalence rules used throughout the chapter (p.92). [CONVENTION] Practical implication: the order of strength capability is **eccentrics > isometrics > concentrics** — i.e., if you can chain eccentrics you can likely also hold an isometric partway through, and if you can hold isometrics you can likely perform at least one concentric rep (p.92).

## Concentric repetitions (p.92-94)
[CONVENTION] Rule 1 — "Perform your maximum repetitions, minus one. Three sets minimum." Rationale: performing set 1 to failure causes performance depreciation in later sets (e.g., 10-8-7 to-failure vs. 9-9-9 not-to-failure across 3 sets = more total reps: 27 vs ~25). Not-to-failure training is preferred for strength because strength work is fundamentally about maximizing nervous-system adaptations — recruitment, synchronization, rate coding — which are best driven by consistent near-maximal force output across sets rather than by accumulating fatigue (p.92-93). [SCIENCE/DISPUTED] Studies suggest to-failure training is preferable when the primary goal is hypertrophy or endurance, since to-failure volume drives mechanical damage and metabolic stress that stimulate those adaptations more than not-to-failure volume does (p.93).

[CONVENTION] Rule 2 — "Rule of Fifteen": aim for a minimum of 15 total repetitions per exercise across all sets; with two exercises per muscle group that's ~30 total reps for that group. Guideline total-volume ranges per pushing/pulling/legs exercise (p.93-94):
- Strength: 25-50 total repetitions
- Hypertrophy: 40-75+ total repetitions

Set/rep chart to hit ~15 total reps depending on max reps per set (p.94):
| Max reps/set | Sets x Reps |
|---|---|
| 1 | 6-10 sets of 1 rep |
| 2 | 5-8 sets of 2 reps |
| 3 | 5-6 sets of 3 reps |
| 4 | 4-5 sets of 4 reps |
| 5 | 3 sets of 5 reps |
| 6+ | 3 sets of remaining reps |

Below ~3 reps/set the system "breaks down" — required set count balloons impractically (e.g., 8 sets of 2, or 15 sets of 1). [CONVENTION] At these lowest levels, target only 4-10 total reps and supplement with isometrics, eccentrics, or assisted concentrics until max reps/set rises (p.94).

[CONVENTION] Assisted concentric exercises (bands, partner, machine, elevation) can substitute for the 4-10 rep floor at very low strength levels by making the movement easier (e.g., elevate upper body for pushups; loosen hand spacing for diamond pushups) (p.94).

[CONVENTION] Rule 3 (hypertrophy-only, optional): beginners should aim for ~10 total sets per muscle-group category (pushing/pulling/legs/core) regardless of rep range, splittable across multiple exercise variants within that category (e.g., 5x5 pull-ups + 5x5 rows = 10 sets total for pulling) (p.94-95). Caveat: these three rules are general guidance for beginners/intermediates; exceptions apply for prehab/rehab or edge-case low-rep situations (p.95).

## Isometric holds (p.95-98)
[CONVENTION] Core comparison formula developed by Low from "extensive practical research": 1 concentric repetition ≈ 2 seconds of isometric hold, valid up to ~15 reps or ~30s of hold — beyond that point, work becomes endurance-biased and stops producing good strength/hypertrophy progress (this is the in-practice upper end of the optimal hypertrophy range) (p.95).

Guideline: 60-75% of max hold time is a good target intensity for volume/set programming to ensure progress (some coaches recommend 50%) (p.95-96). "Maximum hold" is defined as the longest hold achieved stopping one second short of failure (i.e., if failure would occur at hold+1s, max hold = that measured time +1) (p.95).

(Chart referenced as "Finding Your Own Sweet Spot" — the underlying data table is a figure with no extractable text; see PDF pp.95-96.) Chart columns explained (p.95-96):
- **Hold Time** — the per-set target hold, a fraction of max hold (varies by athlete).
- **Total Sets** — recommended number of sets at that hold time; can be expressed as a range.
- **Total Time** — cumulative hold time across sets; most useful as a progress-tracking metric beyond the beginner phase (e.g., 6 sets x 6s = 36s vs. 5 sets x 7s = 35s — the second is a genuine, if small, improvement despite fewer sets/lower total time).
- **Sweet Spot** — an arbitrary but broadly-applicable set/rep recommendation for most athletes; adjust by removing a set if exhausted/not progressing, or adding a set if undertrained.

[CONVENTION] "Sixty-Second Method" (alternative protocol, uses 50% of max hold as volume) — all sets sum to 60s total (p.96):
| Max hold | Protocol |
|---|---|
| 6s | 20 sets of 3s holds |
| 8s | 15 sets of 4s holds |
| 10s | 12 sets of 5s holds |
| 12s | 10 sets of 6s holds |
| 20s | 6 sets of 10s holds |
| 30s | 4 sets of 14s holds |
| 40s | 3 sets of 20s holds |

Tradeoffs (p.96-97): the 60-second method may better protect connective tissue by using a slower, lower-intensity progression (50% vs. 60-75%), but its shorter hold times can require 10-20 sets, appreciably lengthening the workout. The 60-75% method achieves an "excellent tradeoff between sets, volume, and intensity" with fewer sets at lower hold times, reducing overuse-injury propensity. Example at 10s max: 12x5s@50% = 30 total "reps" (via the 2s=1rep formula) vs. 5-6x7s@60-75% = 17-18 "reps" (p.97). [CONVENTION] Troubleshooting rule (applies to all routines, not just isometrics): if progress stalls, reduce volume first — this restores recovery time and lowers CNS/connective-tissue strain; only add volume if reducing it still doesn't resolve the plateau (p.97).

## Eccentric cluster repetitions / "chaining" (p.97-100)
[CONVENTION] Comparison formula: 1 repetition ≈ 3 seconds of an eccentric movement (no direct research comparison exists per the book — this is derived from athlete observation) (p.97).

[SCIENCE] Eccentric exercise preferentially recruits fast-twitch fibers the faster it is performed — a quick ~1s eccentric produces a greater hypertrophy response than a slow ~6s one. Tradeoff: very fast (1s) eccentrics require an excessive number of sets to accumulate adequate volume; very slow eccentrics (20-30s) cause metabolic-acidosis "burn" that does not optimally drive strength or hypertrophy. Sweet spot: 3-10 second eccentrics (p.97-98).

[CONVENTION] Cluster/chaining protocol rationale: eccentrics are used to break through plateaus or build toward unilateral movements (e.g., one-arm chin-up), not as a permanent routine fixture. Progression is: increase hold time first (toward 7-10s), then decrease inter-cluster rest — because increasing hold time raises volume/tension (driving strength/hypertrophy) faster than merely removing rest (p.98).

Empirical claim: most athletes who can perform 3 consecutive eccentrics at uniform lowering speed, 7-10s each, through full ROM, with zero rest between them, can also perform 1 full concentric rep of that movement — this generalizes even to advanced movements (one-arm pushup, one-arm chin-up) and eccentric-entered isometrics (e.g., front lever via inverted-hang eccentric lower) (p.98).

Ordered progression protocol (p.98-99):
1. Start: 2-3 sets of 2-3 cluster reps of 3s eccentrics, 3 min rest.
2. Increase hold time in 1s increments (holding cluster count and inter-set rest steady) up through 4s, 5s, 6s, 7s, 8s — reaching the 7-10s target range before touching rest times.
3. Once in the 7-10s range, decrease *inter-cluster* rest (not inter-set rest, which stays fixed) in 10-30s increments — larger decrements (up to full minutes) tolerable for lower-level progressions (e.g., chin-ups); smaller decrements (10s) required for higher-level/unilateral progressions (e.g., one-arm chin-up) — example ladder: 3:00 → 2:40 → 2:20 → ... → 1:00 → 0:40 → 0:20 → no rest.
4. At "no rest" between clusters, the athlete has typically gained at least one concentric rep of the movement.

[CONVENTION] Non-responder fallback: if the standard rest/hold-time manipulation doesn't yield progress, systematically increase set count instead — up to 3-5 sets of 2-3 clusters, then up to 3-5 sets of 3-5 clusters. Empirical claim: 5 sets of 5 cluster reps of a 10s eccentric with zero inter-cluster rest reliably yields at least one concentric rep (p.100).

[CONVENTION] Eccentric-work hygiene: eccentrics take a bigger recovery toll than isometric or concentric work and should be used sparingly — as a plateau-breaker, not a routine staple. Never use more than one or two eccentric exercises in a routine, and each must occupy a separate category (pushing/pulling/legs/core) (p.100). Unilateral eccentric exercises (e.g., one-arm chin-up) effectively double CNS training stimulus since each side must be trained — this requires proportionally more recovery, and insufficient recovery during a mesocycle of unilateral work risks plateau (p.100).

## Examples of repetitions across the three exercise types (p.100-102)
Comprehensive summary formulas restated (p.100-101):
- Concentric: max reps -1, 3 sets minimum; Rule of Fifteen (≥15 total reps/exercise); 25-50 total reps for strength, 40-75+ for hypertrophy; beginners aim for 10 sets/muscle-group for pure hypertrophy.
- Isometric: (chart referenced — figure, see PDF pp.95-96; no additional text given in this section).
- Eccentric: start 2-3 sets of 2-3 clusters of 3-5s eccentrics @ 3 min rest; progress to 2-3 sets of 2-3 clusters of 7-10s eccentrics with no cluster rest; if extra volume needed, move to 3-5 sets first, then 3-5 cluster reps.

Unified comparison formula (p.101): **1 concentric rep = 2s isometric hold = 3s eccentric**, with total workout volume target of 25-50 reps for strength / 40-75 reps for hypertrophy.

Worked example applying the sample routine, converting sets/reps to total-rep-equivalents (p.101):
- Pull-ups 3x5→12 = 15-36 reps; same for Dips, Wide Ring Rows, Rings Pushups, Squats, Deep Step-ups.
- Muscle-group totals across two exercises each: Push (Dips+Ring Pushups) = 30-72 reps; Pull (Pull-ups+Rows) = 30-72 reps; Legs (Squats+Step-ups) = 30-72 reps.
- 3x5 across two exercises = 30 total reps: solidly in the strength range, slightly below optimal hypertrophy range.
- 3x8 across two exercises = 48 total reps: within both strength and hypertrophy ranges.
- 3x12 across two exercises = 72 total reps: solidly within hypertrophy range.
- [CONVENTION] Being at the fringe of a range is not a problem: e.g., 3x5 with two exercises (30 reps) still stimulates good hypertrophy despite being below the "optimal" band; the number of quality near-failure sets matters more for hypertrophy than exact total-rep count (p.101-102).

Isometric/eccentric-to-rep conversions worked (p.102):
- Planche Isometrics 5x12s = 60s isometric / 2 = 30 rep-equivalents.
- L-Pull-up Eccentrics 3x(3x7s) = 63s / 3 = 21 rep-equivalents.
- [CONVENTION] Substitution rule: when adding a planche isometric or eccentric-pull exercise, remove one of the corresponding concentric pushing/pulling movements from the routine rather than stacking on top of it (p.102).
- Planche isometrics at 5x12s fall within the "3 sets of 10 reps" strength/hypertrophy equivalence band (17s max-hold-equivalent). Once 3 sets of 3 cluster reps of 7-10s eccentrics with no rest is achievable (≈3x20 rep-equivalent), training is shifting away from strength/hypertrophy toward endurance — a signal to transition off eccentrics onto concentrics (p.102).

## Sets (p.102-104)
[CONVENTION] Three is not a magic number — it is used because 5-15 reps across roughly 3 sets naturally lands in the 25-50 (strength) or 40-75 (hypertrophy) total-rep ranges. More sets are warranted when per-set max reps are low: e.g., 3-6 reps/set x 3 sets x 2 exercises = only 18-36 total reps/muscle-group — decent for strength but insufficient for a hypertrophy stimulus, warranting 4-6 sets instead (p.102-103). Cited external references: StrongLifts (classic barbell beginner program) uses 5x5; some intermediate hypertrophy routines use up to 12 sets; 8-10x3 "works very well" for combined strength+hypertrophy (p.103). Downside of low-rep/high-set schemes: long workouts given 3-5 min inter-set rest requirements (p.103).

[CONVENTION] To bias a routine toward hypertrophy: add 1-3 sets per exercise (4-6 sets total) to push total reps into the hypertrophy range. Caveats: added volume risks overuse injury (back off if joints/tendons/connective tissue are sore) and risks stagnation if recovery can't keep pace (back off if progress stalls, then resume) (p.103-104).

## Exercise order (p.104-107)
[SCIENCE/CONVENTION] Fatigue accumulates within a workout and creates an "exercise bottleneck" — most effort/quality goes into the first exercise, least into the last, because performance degrades set-over-set (e.g., a 3x5 protocol may go from "1 rep short of failure" on set 1 to struggling on set 3). [CONVENTION] Claim: percent of maximal effort drops "a couple of points" per additional set; by 15 total sets (3 sets x 5 exercises) an athlete is operating at only ~90-95% capacity — a decline typically invisible unless exercise order is varied (p.105-106).

[CONVENTION] Rule: order exercises to match goal priority — put the movement you most want to improve first in the routine (e.g., planche isometric first if planche is the primary goal), because first-position exercises receive the least fatigue-degraded effort (p.104-107). Reordering example given: moving planche/L-pull-up eccentrics to the top, then legs, then the remaining upper-body work — with the note that moving dips/rows earlier visibly improves their quality (p.106-107). [CONVENTION] Exercise order may be freely adjusted mid-microcycle or mid-mesocycle (e.g., for time constraints); it need not stay fixed for the full 6-8 week cycle (p.107).

## Rest times (p.107-110)
[SCIENCE] ATP (adenosine triphosphate) powers muscle contraction; after a near-failure set, much ATP converts to ADP+phosphate and must regenerate before the next set is not muscularly limited. Approximate ATP-replenishment curve (p.108-109):
| Time | % ATP replenished |
|---|---|
| 30s | 50% |
| 60s | 75% |
| 90s | 88% |
| 120s | 95% |
| 180s | 99% |

[CONVENTION] Because strength work's goal is to maximize *neural* factors, rest ≥3 minutes between strength sets is "strongly recommended" — insufficient rest lets muscular fatigue (not CNS capacity) become the limiting factor, blunting the intended neural-strength adaptation (p.109). Recommended rest by goal (p.109-110):
- Endurance: 30-90s between sets (intentionally trains contraction in a fatigued/ATP-depleted state).
- Hypertrophy: 60-240+ seconds (vary rest — shorter rest with easier/lighter work drives to-failure fatigue that stimulates "alternative hypertrophy pathways"; must still hit adequate total volume).
- Strength: 180-300+ seconds (minimum 2 min, 3+ min preferable, to avoid muscular-fatigue-limited CNS stimulus).
- Combined strength+hypertrophy: overlap the two ranges, trimming ~1 minute off each end → 180-240s recommended — nearly full ATP replenishment for strength effectiveness while still allowing some hypoxia-driven hypertrophy near set end, and enough intensity/volume for both adaptations (p.110).

## Paired sets (p.110-113)
[CONVENTION] Rationale: a minimal 2-push/2-pull/2-leg x 3-set routine is 18 sets; at 3 min rest that's 54 min, at 5 min rest 90 min — excluding warm-up/skill/flexibility work, which can push total time past 2 hours, impractical for most schedules (p.110-111).

[CONVENTION] Paired-set method: alternate two exercises that use *opposing* muscle groups (e.g., planche [pushing] paired with front lever [pulling]) so that performing the opposing exercise substitutes for rest — muscles recover passively while the other muscle group works. Rest between each half is cut in half (e.g., 5-min single-exercise rest → 2.5 min between paired exercises) (p.111). Worked time comparison (p.111-112): normal sets — 3x planche @5 min rest = ~15 min, plus 3x front lever @5 min rest = ~15 min, total 30 min. Paired sets — 1 planche + 2.5 min rest + 1 front lever + 2.5 min rest = 5 min per round x 3 rounds = 15 min total (half the time). [CONVENTION] Caveat: this method may not produce optimal strength gains because the shortened rest may not allow full muscular/nervous recovery, but gains remain "very similar" — a reasonable option for those unwilling to do long workouts (p.112).

[CONVENTION] Anti-pattern: extending paired sets into triple sets or full circuits is not recommended — three couplets of two exercises taxes the body the same as two triplets of three exercises time-wise, but cycling through more exercises per rest interval is not beneficial for strength/hypertrophy goals, and is a particularly poor choice for hypertrophy (p.112). Push/pull or upper/lower splits are a further time-saving alternative but "not recommended unless" the athlete has another sport or heavy skill-work demands requiring reduced strength-training frequency — the tradeoff being decreased per-exercise frequency (p.112).

Terminology distinctions (p.112-113), explicitly *not* to be confused with paired sets:
- **Paired sets**: alternating exercises of *opposing* muscle groups to save time while preserving strength-work quality.
- **Super sets**: two exercises for the *same* muscle group performed back-to-back with no rest, for a strong hypertrophy stimulus.
- **Drop sets**: same exercise repeated with no rest, load decreased at failure, for a hypertrophy stimulus.

## Tempo (p.113-115)
[CONVENTION] Tempo notation: 4-digit format, e.g. standard "10x0": 1s eccentric, 0s pause at bottom, "x" = explosive concentric, 0s pause at top (order reversed for pulling movements like pull-ups, but notated the same way) (p.113-114).

[SCIENCE] 10x0 is recommended for strength and hypertrophy because it leverages **Henneman's size principle** (detailed in Ch.2): low-threshold motor units (LTMUs, slow-twitch) are recruited before medium- (MTMUs) and high-threshold (HTMUs, fast-twitch) units, in increasing order — this ordering conserves energy since LTMUs resist fatigue better. Explosive-intent acceleration at good technique recruits the full motor-unit pool during the rep, maximizing neural strength factors (recruitment, synchronization, rate coding). [SCIENCE] Studies cited: fast repetitions are superior for strength and power in untrained subjects (p.114).

[SCIENCE/DISPUTED] For hypertrophy: any tempo bias toward fast-twitch fiber recruitment from explosive concentric ("X") tempo exists but is "not significant." Hypertrophy is relatively similar across tempos provided total time-under-tension/volume is matched and sets go to failure — 10 reps at 10x0 ≈ 5 reps at 5050 tempo in hypertrophic effect, since damage/growth scales with stress exerted regardless of exact tempo. Cited claim: beginners may see similar hypertrophy results at 40% 1RM and 85-90% 1RM intensities (p.114).

[CONVENTION] Clarification on "explosive" at high intensity: near 1-4 RM loads, movement *intent* to move quickly (not actual bar/body speed) is what recruits fast-twitch fibers — so slow-looking reps at near-maximal difficulty still count as "fast" tempo work (p.114).

[CONVENTION] Reasons to deliberately use slower tempos (p.114):
- Potentially lower injury risk.
- Forces working through full ROM in otherwise-explosive lifts.
- Emphasizes greater control.
- Beneficial during injury recovery while connective tissue heals.

Specific slow-tempo use cases: 2020 or 2121 when first teaching a movement (extra time to focus on technique, pause to catch breath/refocus); 4040 or 4141 during injury/rehab (prioritizes correct muscle-firing sequence over strength/hypertrophy — particularly effective for shoulder injuries prone to compensation patterns); slower eccentric phase (e.g., 5120) to teach control and build eccentric-strain resistance, useful for muscle-strain prevention and for eccentric-portion strain rehab; slower eccentrics also usable for tendonitis (p.114-115). [CONVENTION] Default for all routines built later in the book: 10x0 tempo, with room to experiment once more experienced (p.115).

## General strength vs. isometrics — movement-based training (p.115-117)
[CONVENTION/DISPUTED] Alternative approach (author's stated preference: "this author" favors movement-based routines): isometric skills (crosses, straddle planche, full front lever) can be built without much dedicated isometric training, by training primarily through concentric movement work. Claimed benefits: more balanced strength development across full ranges of motion, faster overall progression, and better ability to handle unexpected movements (relevant for parkour, martial arts) (p.115).

[SCIENCE] Cited finding: isometric training only confers strength within about 30 degrees of the specific joint angle/range trained. For the shoulder specifically (described as the "lynchpin of upper-body strength"), 30 degrees is only about one-tenth of its total rotary range — cited as a reason not to rely solely on isometric work (p.115-116).

[CONVENTION] Programming without isometrics: replace planche/front lever/back lever isometric holds with additional concentric exercises targeting the same qualities — e.g., planche → planche progression pushups, pseudo planche pushups, other ring pushup variants, dips, HSPUs; front lever → front lever row progressions, bent-over rows, one-arm dumbbell rows. Eliminating the isometric hold does not require abandoning the underlying skill goal (p.116).

[CONVENTION/DISPUTED] However, for athletes specifically pursuing bodyweight isometric skills (planche, front lever, back lever), the book "strongly recommends" keeping isometric exercises in the routine (p.116) — i.e., the movement-only approach is presented as valid but not the recommended default for isometric-skill-focused athletes.

Three common bodyweight-training goal archetypes and how to program around isometrics (p.116-117):
1. **Specific-skill focus** (e.g., planche) — base the majority of pushing work on the specific goal: train the isometric hold itself plus the alternative movement exercises (progression pushups, pseudo planche pushups, dips, HSPUs) to build both the specific joint-position strength and general muscle mass/neural strength needed.
2. **All-around straight-arm/bent-arm strength with isometric goals** — begin with isometric holds to build connective-tissue capacity; once achieved, relegate the holds to warm-up/cool-down maintenance work, freeing routine space for new movements/isometrics.
3. **Pure overall strength (no specific isometric concern)** — isometric skills (e.g., straddle planche on rings) are typically achieved simply by getting generally stronger via pseudo planche pushups, ring dip progressions, ring HSPUs — the strength transfers effectively without dedicated isometric practice.

[CONVENTION] Beginner recommendation: support work, positioning drills, German hangs, and other straight-arm work in the warm-up, followed by handstands as skill work — this builds connective-tissue strength before isometric work begins. Beginners should reach proficiency (roughly level 5-6) in dips, rows, pull-ups, and pushup variations before transitioning routines toward back lever and planche work (p.116-117).

## Core work (p.117-119)
[CONVENTION] Core work sits in the strength portion of the routine, placed toward the end. Pairing handstands and manna as "opposing shoulder movements" is still encouraged conceptually but need not be scheduled together in time — coupling them at the *start* of a workout was found to cause excess fatigue that degraded subsequent pushup/dip/HSPU set quality (p.117).

Sample core block: L-Sit for 60s total (as many sets as needed, not to failure); Compression Work 3x10s (p.117).

[CONVENTION] L-sit rationale: tests core strength directly, progresses toward V-sit and manna, and carries over to press handstands and other shape-holding movements. Compression work rationale: develops core strength and active flexibility simultaneously, broadly applicable to handstand presses and general flexibility goals (p.118). Compression work follows the concentric-vs-isometric pattern seen elsewhere; many upper-body strength exercises (e.g., front lever progressions) already have a core-holding component built in (p.118).

Compression work protocol, ordered (p.118):
1. Stretch hamstrings for 30 seconds.
2. Sit with arms straight, hands by knees.
3. Legs straight, pull knees to face by contracting the core.
4. Hold 10 seconds. (Cramping is a sign of correct execution.)
5. Repeat 3-5 times.
6. If knees easily reach the face for most sets, move hands closer to the toes (progression).

[CONVENTION] Placement flexibility: compression work fits well at the end of strength training or alongside end-of-workout flexibility work, or alternatively at the beginning alongside L-sit/straddle-L/manna work if flexibility work needs to move later to accommodate handstand press technique work (p.118). Other recommended core exercises: ab wheel, hanging leg raises, V-ups, dragon flags, weighted decline sit-ups (the last called out as good for hypertrophy) (p.118).

[CONVENTION] Posterior-chain/back caveat: the chapter deliberately omits dedicated lower-back core exercises, on the assumption that lower-body weighted work (cited elsewhere in the book as the most effective way to build lower-body strength/hypertrophy) already provides adequate lower-back stimulus. If not using weights, substitute bodyweight posterior work: glute-ham raises, reverse hyperextensions, or similar — because most bodyweight leg exercises (squats, pistols, lunges, sprinting) do not adequately train the back. Warning: excessive abdominal work without matching back work is cited as a cause of eventual back pain/injury — train both sides of the core (p.118-119).

## Performance and termination of workouts (p.119-121)
[CONVENTION] Beginners get bigger/stronger and reinforce technique primarily by completing workouts; it is rarely a good idea to quit a workout partway through as a beginner, even when motivation is low — pushing through builds work ethic/consistency and beginners are often more capable than they perceive; personal records sometimes occur on low-motivation days precisely because the athlete pushed through (p.119).

[CONVENTION] As skill/fatigue levels rise, judgment calls become more nuanced. Guidance by experience level (p.119-120):
- **Beginner**: push through fatigue; finishing the routine and working through stress is usually better for body and psyche than quitting, except in the flagged scenarios below.
- **Intermediate**: quality of work matters more — if skill-work quality is poor, try one or two more exercises before deciding; if still no improvement, ending early without guilt is acceptable.
- **Advanced/elite**: full autoregulatory judgment — experienced athletes can anticipate (even pre-workout) whether they'll perform poorly on certain exercises/skills that day and may choose to skip or terminate before starting; this requires accumulated experience to use well (p.120).

[CONVENTION] Three flagged scenarios where terminating (or modifying) a workout is explicitly recommended (p.120):
1. Sick.
2. Sleep-deprived for multiple days in a row.
3. Joints/tendons/connective tissue sore, painful, achy, or twingeing in a way likely to worsen with continued training.

For scenarios 1-2: full termination is reasonable since workout quality will be poor and continuing risks worsening the condition — rest is described as vital and undervalued especially by younger athletes (p.120-121). For scenario 3: terminate only the aggravating *portions* of the routine, substituting rehab exercises for those parts while continuing the rest as planned — total workout elimination is explicitly discouraged because it risks habituating workout-skipping, which can cascade into general inactivity rather than actual injury rehabilitation ("becoming an inactive couch potato") (p.121).

## Chapter 9 summary (as given, p.121-122)
The book's own numbered recap:
1. Three exercise types: concentric, isometric, eccentric.
2. Exercise notation: sets x repetitions.
3. Concentric repetitions: 15+ reps per exercise, stay 1 rep short of failure, target a chosen total-rep count for strength or hypertrophy.
4. Use isometric charts to find sets/hold times; stay in the 60-70% hold-time range, aim for 40-70 seconds overall. *(Note: this restates the 60-75% figure given earlier in the chapter body as "60-70%" and gives a 40-70s overall target not explicitly derived elsewhere in the extracted text — reproduced as written.)*
5. Structure eccentric exercises by systematically increasing hold time then decreasing rest time.
6. 1 concentric rep ≈ 2s isometric hold ≈ 3s eccentric movement.
7. Order routine exercises with the most important ones first.
8. Rest times per set: 30-90s endurance; 60-240s hypertrophy; 120s or 180-300+s strength. *(Note: the book's own summary line lists "120 or 180-300+ seconds" for strength, which is looser than the 180-300+s stated in the chapter body — reproduced as written.)*
9. Shorten a workout by pairing exercises that don't share muscle groups.
10. Tempo: 10x0 is standard; changing tempo changes the outcome for endurance, isometric work, and other exercises.
11. In movement vs. isometric work, movement-based training can be superior.
12. Core work (compression) improves active flexibility; good exercises: ab wheel, hanging leg raises, V-ups, dragon flags, weighted decline sit-ups (good hypertrophy default). Balance anterior and posterior core.
13. Gauge workout performance and terminate only when necessary, without guilt — everyone has bad days.
