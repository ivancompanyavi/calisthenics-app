# OG2 Ch.6 — Population Considerations

> **Source:** *Overcoming Gravity* 2e, Ch.6, book pp.59-70. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/og2.txt` or PDF pp.59-70.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] established research · [CONVENTION] Low's system · [DISPUTED] contested.

## Understanding Populations (p.59)
[CONVENTION] Beyond the beginner/intermediate/advanced split covered via the progression charts, the book defines four additional, non-mutually-exclusive population axes that should modify how a routine is constructed: (1) sedentary vs. active, (2) young vs. old, (3) sport-specific vs. recreational, (4) uninjured vs. injured. An individual can fall into several of these simultaneously, and all applicable considerations should be layered together when building their routine (p.59).

## Sedentary vs. Active (p.59)
[CONVENTION] Sedentary splits into two subtypes; active splits into five.

**Sedentary:**
- Purely Sedentary
- Previously Athletic Sedentary

**Active:**
- Non-Weight-Lifting Active
- Weight-Lifting Active
- Bodyweight Active
- Bodyweight Prepared
- Sport-Specific

### Purely Sedentary (p.59-61)
[CONVENTION] Never exercised. Two governing factors:
1. **Poor movement/body awareness** → start slowly; prioritize correct technique over progression speed; may need to slow tempo or withhold progression entirely if position is even slightly off (p.59-60).
2. **No prior adaptation to exercise** → connective tissue adapts slower than muscle, so easier exercises + higher reps are prescribed to build technical mastery and blood flow while minimizing stress on slow-adapting connective tissue; high-intensity work would outpace joint/connective-tissue readiness and risks overuse injury (p.60). [SCIENCE-adjacent mechanism, stated as book's own reasoning]

Prescriptions: most purely sedentary start at L0-4 (usually L0-2) on the OG charts; sets of 5 reps building to 12-20 reps per set, technique-focused, for several months before reducing rep ranges (p.60).

Overweight/obese individuals are placed in this category too, with an added priority on caloric deficit/healthy diet, plus even slower chart progression than typical for this population — the book states obesity is commonly accompanied by systemic chronic inflammation, which impairs recovery and fat loss, so weight loss combined with slow progression reduces joint/connective-tissue burden and overuse-injury risk [SCIENCE, cited as physiological rationale] (p.60).

### Previously Athletic Sedentary (p.60-62)
[CONVENTION] Former athletes/regular exercisers, defined as ≥3 months of inactivity. [SCIENCE] Cited detraining timeline: endurance begins degrading after 3-5 days of inactivity; strength begins degrading after ~5-7 days; exact rates depend on training history and any prior overreaching/supercompensation (p.61). After ~3 months there is a fair assumption of significant capability loss from prior peak.

Two risk factors specific to this population:
1. **Mind conditioned to hard work but body has lost adaptations** → they progress unusually fast because neuromuscular "muscle memory" returns quickly, and this speed itself is the danger. Overdoing volume/intensity too soon risks severe soreness or rhabdomyolysis (life-threatening; red flags: massive soreness + localized swelling/edema + reduced ROM + brown urine → emergency room) (p.61). [CONVENTION] recommendation: hold back for the first 3-4 weeks of the program.
2. **Connective tissue lags behind strength/muscle-mass return** — strength and muscle mass "come back rapidly" but tendon/ligament integrity does not, so this population is the most at-risk for tendonitis and connective-tissue injury (p.61-62). On signs of connective-tissue compromise (twinges, achiness, generalized joint/tendon discomfort): drop to a lower progression and work higher reps (15-20+, not to failure) — higher-rep work pushes blood into the area to aid connective-tissue healing [SCIENCE-flavored rationale attributed to the book]. If even that aggravates symptoms, fully back off that progression and add prehabilitation/isolation/non-painful mobility work. Most cases resolve in 1-2 weeks, after which resume progressing using at least 12-15 reps as the bar before advancing (rather than lower rep counts) (p.62).

### Non-Weight-Lifting Active (p.62-63)
[CONVENTION] Runners, swimmers, bikers, recreational sport players without strength training. Same general cautions as Previously Athletic Sedentary apply, but this population may incur connective-tissue injuries *faster* than the Purely Sedentary because they are driven to push themselves despite having no strength-training conditioning (p.62).

Frequency/recovery trade-off consideration: if already performing high-frequency activity (e.g., running 6x/week, sport 3-4x/week), adding a 3x/week bodyweight routine on top may exceed recovery capacity. [CONVENTION] Recommendation: start with 2 workouts/week, reduce 1-2 days of other activity, re-evaluate fatigue/progress after 2 weeks before adding a third session (p.63).

Because this population tends to be motivated/disciplined, coaches can lean on higher reps to cement movement patterns and correct sport-driven imbalances (e.g., climbers' overdeveloped pulling vs. underdeveloped pushing/chest) via targeted balancing work (p.63).

### Weight-Lifting Active (p.63)
[CONVENTION] Defined threshold: ≥6 months to 1 year of consistent weight training qualifies for this category; more experience (1 vs. 10 years) yields proportionally more structural adaptation transfer to bodyweight work (p.63).

Transfers well to most bodyweight exercise without incident, EXCEPT straight-arm strength exercises (planche, front lever, back lever, straight-arm press handstands) — the population's baseline strength causes these to progress "much too quickly," which is itself a hazard because connective tissue and skill haven't caught up. [CONVENTION] Mitigation: continually self-monitor for achiness, soreness, dullness, pinching, or stinging around joints/tendons/ligaments (p.63).

Imbalance check: for weight-lifting active trained on "beach muscles," compare pushing vs. pulling levels on the OG charts — pushing normally sits at roughly the same level or one grade higher than pulling; a 2+ grade discrepancy signals a weakness needing correction (p.63).

### Bodyweight Active (p.63-64)
[CONVENTION] ≥6 months experience with generalized bodyweight exercises (pushups, dips, HSPU, pull-ups, rows, squats, pistols, etc.), possibly including advanced single-limb variations. Primary failure mode: difficulty sticking to a structured strength routine — many in this group come from an endurance/haphazard training background (high reps, many sets, to failure) and perceive structured strength training as "not doing much," causing adherence problems (p.64).

### Bodyweight Prepared (p.64)
[CONVENTION] Already experienced with most straight-arm isometric strength exercises; typically already in structured bodyweight training (gymnasts, b-boys, some parkour athletes). Primary risk: high consistency/drive means they resist reducing training even when overuse injuries or pre-injury signs (achiness, tingling, joint/connective-tissue soreness) appear — ignoring these is described as "a recipe for developing long-term injuries." [CONVENTION] Needed: proper future time orientation/planning so they rehabilitate fully rather than pushing through (p.64).

Both Bodyweight Active and Bodyweight Prepared already carry bodyweight-specific adaptations; the shared concern across both is training consistency and early issue identification (p.64).

### Sport-Specific (p.64)
[CONVENTION] Competitive athletes, evaluated case-by-case — defer to the athlete's own sport coach for strength/conditioning recommendations. Sports skewing toward weight training (football, basketball) should reference the Weight-Lifting Active guidance; sports skewing toward bodyweight training (wrestling, gymnastics, military) should reference the Bodyweight Active guidance. The key added consideration for this population isn't a fundamentally different training approach but *time/recovery allocation* — balancing strength work, conditioning, and sport-specific practice (expanded later in the cross-training chapter) (p.64).

## Young vs. Old (p.63-64)
[CONVENTION] Core distinction: older populations have decreased resiliency — potential decreased flexibility/mobility, chronic prior injuries, overweight/obesity, disease (diabetes, hypertension), impaired movement quality, other functional limitations. Programming implication: prepare the body pre-workout and modify workout structure when technique cannot be properly executed (p.63-64).

[DISPUTED/SCIENCE-noted] Pre-workout static stretching has generally fallen out of favor across the fitness field over the past decade because it may reduce maximal muscle contraction ability during subsequent strength work — but the book notes an exception: static stretching pre-workout can be beneficial when the goal is a mobility gain needed to correctly perform a technique. General rule stated: prioritize safety over performance when constructing the routine (p.64).

**Warm-up duration formula** [CONVENTION]: 10-15 minutes base warm-up, +additional minutes equal to age ÷ 4, reasoned as compensating for older bodies being generally less efficient across all workout phases and reducing injury risk (p.64):
- 20-year-old: 10 + (20/4) = 15 minutes
- 40-year-old: 10 + (40/4) = 20 minutes
- 60-year-old: 10 + (60/4) = 25 minutes

Younger athletes (especially single-digit ages): primary concern is decreased coordination from incompletely developed neurological pathways, plus a tendency to sacrifice technique for rep count (perceived invincibility). [CONVENTION] Coaching tactic: gamify — pair children up and have them police each other's technique errors, since children enjoy pointing out peers' mistakes, effectively creating a peer double-check system (p.64).

Older populations with flexibility/mobility limitations: increase mobility/flexibility work while decreasing strength-work volume due to reduced training capacity — mobility/flexibility sections of the routine may legitimately exceed the strength section in size, because a foundation of movement quality and ROM must be established before strength is layered on top; skipping this step is called one of the easiest routes to injury (p.64). Additionally, a 3x/week full-body routine may not suit older trainees who cannot recover from a large single-day workout; a split routine spreading work across more days may be needed to avoid excessive fatigue and injury from concentrating everything into one session (p.64).

## Sport-Specific vs. Recreational Training (p.64-65)
[CONVENTION] Core distinction is *emphasis*: in sport-specific training, strength/conditioning is a means to an end (on-field performance is primary, S&C is supplemental); in recreational/other training, emphasis can be split evenly or weighted toward training itself rather than performance outcomes (p.64-65).

Sport-specific training is always attribute-training-focused, and which attribute dominates depends on the sport's energy-system demands (p.65):
- Long-distance runner: mostly endurance work, with some power/strength/plyometrics.
- Sprinter (track and field): mostly maximal strength/power/plyometrics, minimal endurance training.

Seasonality: most sports (football, basketball, soccer, track and field, and — per the book — increasingly gymnastics as competition structures evolve) have in-season vs. off-season phases. In-season training maintains/prepares competitive ability; off-season training is used to build up abilities for the next season (p.65).

Exercise-selection difference: sport-specific programs choose exercises for effectiveness toward specific attributes, not personal preference or enjoyment — e.g., squatting/deadlifting with free weights favored over leg press or sissy squats; some programs avoid explosive lifts like power cleans for valuable athletes due to injury risk, i.e., effectiveness is balanced against safety/liability (p.65). Recreational/general trainees have latitude to choose movements they enjoy, guided by the book's structural-balance principles to avoid injury-prone paths, with the rest of programming direction left to individual goals (p.65).

## Uninjured vs. Injured (p.65-66)
[CONVENTION] Two sub-groups: (1) currently injured, (2) previously injured but rehabilitated. Injured trainees need differently structured workouts than uninjured trainees; injury programming balances healing the affected part(s) against maintaining safety in surrounding-area training (full treatment deferred to the health & injury management chapter) (p.65).

Key behavioral point: injury should never be a reason to stop training altogether — ceasing all activity due to injury-related fear commonly leads to full sedentary behavior, and since diet often doesn't adjust downward accordingly, this frequently produces unwanted weight gain/obesity as a secondary consequence of the injury (p.65-66). The injury itself is described as merely "a catalyst to a chain of poor decision-making" that must be actively countered.

**Post-rehabilitation prehabilitation protocol** [CONVENTION]:
- Continue rehab-style exercises at the end of the routine after full rehabilitation, to (a) build resilience against re-injury and (b) maintain awareness that special care is still warranted (p.66).
- Rationale cited: "the number one predictor of your next injury is a previous injury" — re-injury of the same body part is more likely than a novel injury elsewhere (p.66). [SCIENCE-flavored claim attributed to the book, not sourced to a specific citation in this excerpt]
- Prehabilitation also limits compensation patterns that develop even after a body part is "fully healed" (example given: a right knee injury causing favoring of the left leg during squats, which can itself produce a new imbalance or tendonitis in the opposite hip/knee) (p.66).
- **Duration formula**: minimum = half the length of the rehabilitation period; maximum = the full length of the rehabilitation period; extend indefinitely if miscellaneous issues (compensation patterns, new injuries) arise. Worked example: 2 months of rehab from tendonitis → 4-8 weeks of post-rehab prehabilitation (p.66).
- Practically, this "will typically look like a couple of additional exercises at the end of your routine" (p.66).
- Explicit deferral to professionals: consult an orthopedic doctor or physical therapist for confusion or specific questions about implementing rehabilitative/prehabilitative measures (p.66).

## Chapter 6 Summary (p.67, as stated in book)
[CONVENTION] Restates the population framework: modifications by population matter because they materially affect how one should train, both to make appropriate progress and to prevent overuse injury; consult a medical professional before implementing a routine. Populations covered:

**Sedentary:** Purely Sedentary · Previously Athletic Sedentary
**Active:** Non-Weight-Lifting Active · Weight-Lifting Active · Bodyweight Active · Bodyweight Prepared · Sport-Specific

**Application (p.67):** if an individual falls into any of these categories, apply the chapter's specific details when constructing a routine — different groups have different modification needs, and this must be accounted for in program design.
