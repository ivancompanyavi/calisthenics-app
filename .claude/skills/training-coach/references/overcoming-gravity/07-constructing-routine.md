# OG2 Ch.7 — Constructing Your Workout Routine

> **Source:** *Overcoming Gravity* 2e, Ch.7, book pp.71-80. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/og2.txt` or PDF pp.71-80.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] established research · [CONVENTION] Low's system · [DISPUTED] contested.

## Routine structure taxonomy (p.71)
Three general structure types: full-body routines, splits, and body-part splits. [CONVENTION] Best type depends entirely on the athlete's goals — no universally "best" routine (p.71).

## Full-body vs. split: the frequency argument (pp.71-73)

- Full-body routines work more muscles via compound, gross-pattern movements and drop isolation work, vs. splits which mix compound + isolation (p.71).
- [SCIENCE] Full-body is recommended for beginners because early strength gains are driven by *non-specific* neurological adaptations — recruitment, synchronization, rate coding — which respond to overall training frequency, not muscle-group-specific volume (p.71).
- With equal session count, full-body hits each muscle group 3-5x/week vs. a typical split's 1-2x/week (p.71).
- [SCIENCE] Some neural adaptations (contribution, motor learning) are *movement-specific* — repeating and progressing the *same* exercise builds strength faster than exercise variety, because the nervous system is learning that specific movement pattern, not general strength (p.71-72).
- Push/pull with a rest day between ≈ 2x/week per exercise; full-body ≈ 3x/week per exercise — a 50% frequency increase (p.72).
- **Worked example (p.72):** beginner training planche.
  - Split: 52wk × 2 sessions/wk ≈ 100 planche workouts/year.
  - Full-body: 52wk × 3 sessions/wk ≈ 150 planche workouts/year.
  - Gap = 50 workouts; at 2 sessions/wk that gap takes 25 weeks (~6 months) to close → the split athlete is ~6 months behind after one year, nominally (conservatively, "easily three to four months" behind allowing for non-linear training effects) (p.72-73).
  - Mechanistic point: an extra 3 months of training at early planche stages can be worth almost a full progression level — frequency compounds (p.73).
- [CONVENTION] Full-body routines "add up over the course of a year" and are therefore more effective than splits for beginner/intermediate athletes (p.73).
- [CONVENTION] Beginner default: 3x/week full-body, spaced ~48h apart (e.g., M/W/F), with a 72h rest after the third workout of the week — because muscles need rest between sessions, and 48h is "a good general guideline" even though [DISPUTED] "there is no solid research to suggest that forty-eight hours is the optimal amount of rest time for most beginners" (p.73).
- [CONVENTION] Isolation work is best reserved for the opposite ends of the spectrum: injured athletes (rehab-specific) or elite athletes (fine-tuning) — beginners/intermediates should default to full-body with few exceptions (p.73).
- Splits (push/pull, straight-arm/bent-arm, upper/lower) are not disparaged outright — they can work well for bodyweight training when volume/recovery factors demand it; higher-level athletes with heavy skill/sport work (e.g., twice-daily practice sports) may need splitting to manage volume (p.71-73).

### Day-abbreviation / scheduling conventions (p.73)
- M/W/F, Tue/Thu/Sat, M/W/Sat, Tue/Thu/Sun are the four canonical 3x/week 48h-spaced patterns given as examples (p.73).
- Rule of thumb: space workouts 48h apart, with a 72h rest after every third workout; choose the schedule that fits life logistics (p.74).
- [CONVENTION] A bi-weekly (per-14-day) schedule can be used instead of weekly — yields 7 sessions per 2 weeks vs. 6 for a strict weekly MWF pattern, i.e., a frequency boost by decoupling the cycle from the calendar week (p.73-74).
- Alternate patterns for constrained schedules (limited gym access, shift work): Wed/Sat/Sun, or Mon/Tue/Wed, Mon/Tue/Thu, Mon/Wed/Thu for shift workers (p.74).

## Four types of splits (p.74)
[CONVENTION] Splitting can be effective for intermediate/advanced athletes or when other life/training factors constrain full-body training (e.g., can't devote all training resources to bodyweight work, full-body is too strenuous/time-consuming). Four recommended split types:
- Push/Pull
- Upper/Lower
- Straight-Arm/Bent-Arm
- Push/Pull/Legs

No isometrics/movement-only day exists as a category because there aren't enough static exercises alone to fill a full routine and drive progress — hence straight-arm/bent-arm folds movement-based straight-arm work in alongside isometrics to reach sufficient volume (p.74).

### Push/Pull Split (pp.74-76)
- Splits legs + upper body into two routines; common practice = 2 push + 2 pull days/week (4 total volume days) (p.74).
- Mechanism/tradeoff: spreading volume over 4 days instead of 3 full-body days lowers per-session systemic stress, freeing capacity for other training demands; and stacking all pushing (or pulling) work into one day increases per-muscle-group volume in that session, which benefits strength/hypertrophy goals (p.74).
- Main disadvantage: legs get split across pushing days (squats — anterior chain/quads) and pulling days (deadlifts/hamstring work — posterior chain/glutes/hamstrings), producing two *consecutive* days of leg stress — pushing moves weight away from center of mass, pulling moves it closer, and squat vs. hip-hinge patterns fall on their respective sides. Problematic for sport athletes needing genuine leg recovery (p.74-75).
- [CONVENTION] Recommended per-workout volume for push/pull: 5-6 exercises per session at "regular" volume (e.g., 2 squat variations + dips + pushups + handstand pushups, +planche for a 6th) — this is "too much volume" for most beginners (p.75-76).
- [CONVENTION] Beginners needing a split should instead use a *lighter* split: 3-4 exercises/workout, roughly 2 legs + 2 upper, or 1 leg + 3 upper, or 1 leg + 2 upper, based on stamina/goals (p.76).
- Schedule examples given: Example 1 (M-push/Tu-pull/rest Wed/Th-push/F-pull, weekend free) and Example 2 (M-push/rest/W-pull/rest/F-push/Sat-pull) — both yield 8 workouts over 2 weeks (4 push + 4 pull) (p.75).
- Example 3 (6/1 pattern: 6 workouts/1 rest day) vs Example 4 (3/1/2/1 pattern across 2 weeks, reversible to 2/1/3/1) — the asymmetric pattern gives one muscle group an extra workout in week 1; reversing order in week 2 balances it out. Over 2 weeks: 6/1-style = 12 workouts (6 push/6 pull); 3/1/2/1-style = 10 workouts (5 push/5 pull) — explicit tradeoff between speed of progress and workout frequency density, "up to you how quickly you would like to reach your goals" (p.76-77).
- Comparison to full-body: full-body still wins on frequency — 6 workouts/muscle-group vs. push/pull's 4-5 — but push/pull sessions are shorter since each contains fewer exercises; choice often comes down to schedule constraints, not just efficacy (p.77).

### Upper/Lower Split (pp.77-78)
- Divides upper-body (torso/arms/chest) from lower-body (legs/core) work (p.77).
- Use case: athletes whose sport/other activity already loads the lower body heavily (e.g., running) — sectioning leg lifting to days without that other activity limits fatigue stacking and speeds recovery (p.77).
- Disadvantage/mechanism: the upper body has more planes of motion than the lower body, so bodyweight upper-body work needs more exercises to cover all those planes — an upper/lower split therefore requires *increasing* per-session exercise count relative to full-body to achieve equivalent stimulus (book's example: full-body's 2 push + 2 pull exercises becomes 3-4 push + 3-4 pull in upper/lower) (p.77). Risk: not enough energy/volume tolerance to complete all needed reps across all planes; too many stacked exercises in one session can itself reduce workout effectiveness (p.77).
- Scheduling structurally mirrors push/pull split (same Example-1/2/3/4 templates apply, substituting Upper/Lower for Push/Pull) (p.77-78).
- [CONVENTION] Real-world sport-scheduling rule: for leg-intensive sports (parkour, football, basketball, track), schedule sport-specific practice on the day *before* (not the day of, and not after) the lower-body lifting day — this keeps sport practice fresh (post rest-day) and lets the lower-body session target whatever the sport work revealed as still needed, then gives a rest day after the leg session for full recovery (p.78).
- [SCIENCE] Rationale for ordering: performing sport-specific technique work on fatigued muscles is an injury risk and impairs technique acquisition — muscles must be fresh for optimal skill/technique training (p.78). This can require stacking sport-specific work and upper-body training on the same day to keep legs fresh for the sport.

### Straight-Arm/Bent-Arm Split (pp.78-79)
- Upper-body-focused split: straight-arm days = static holds/handstands; bent-arm days = full-ROM movements (dips, pull-ups, rows, pushups, handstand pushups). Legs are trained on *both* days despite not being named in the split (p.78).
- Advantage: stacking all straight-arm work (straight-arm press handstands, front lever, back lever, planche) into one day concentrates that training and creates more rest days to let connective tissue (shoulders, elbows, wrists) recover from the high stress straight-arm work imposes (p.78-79).
- [DISPUTED]/tradeoff: good for athletes who dislike exercise repetition since it offers movement variety — but this is also its core weakness: [SCIENCE] strength training requires repeating and progressing the *same* exercises, so added variety directly reduces strength-training potential. "Make this trade-off only if you despise a repetitive exercise routine" (p.79).
- Scheduling: same two-part templates as push/pull and upper/lower apply (p.79).

### Push/Pull/Legs Split (pp.79-80)
- Separates upper push, upper pull, and legs into three distinct days — true bodybuilding-style split (p.79).
- [CONVENTION] Recommended only for athletes not concerned with progress speed, who are highly injury-risk-averse, or who are older and want to go slow (p.79).
- [SCIENCE/CONVENTION] Mechanism/caveat: a three-way split makes it very difficult to get sufficient frequency per movement to progress well; effective for hypertrophy, but strength progress on each movement is slow and prone to plateauing. Explicit caution: beginner/intermediate athletes should avoid any split with three or more parts (p.79).
- Four scheduling examples given, each trading frequency for pacing:
  1. **Example 1** — 1x/week each of push/pull/legs (Mon push, Wed pull, Fri legs, rest Tue/Thu/Sat/Sun) — lowest frequency, most conservative (p.79).
  2. **Example 2** — 4 workouts/week, effective frequency 1.33x/week per routine averaged over 3 weeks (rotating push/pull/legs start point each week) (p.79-80).
  3. **Example 3** — 3/1/2/1 weekly pattern, effective frequency 1.67x/week per routine over 3 weeks, again rotating the starting movement each week to balance exposure (p.80).
  4. **Example 4** — 6/1 pattern, 2 workouts/week of each routine (Mon push/Tue pull/Wed legs/Thu push/Fri pull/Sat legs/Sun rest) — highest frequency version of this split (p.80).
- General principle: rotate/reverse the order of routines across weeks so no single muscle group is systematically favored or disadvantaged by the schedule; adapt rest-day placement (e.g., only resting between upper-body sections) to fit work/school/sport schedule, while maintaining balance among the three (or more) trained groups over time (p.80).

## Chapter 7 Summary (p.80)

**Knowledge Base:**
- [CONVENTION] Frequency is the key driver of fastest progress toward chosen goals — select whichever routine template maximizes frequency in the shortest time, *as long as there are no injury concerns* (p.80).
- For beginners focused on bodyweight strength, full-body workouts are the best template, but the routine must still fit the athlete's schedule (p.80).
- Five total templates, in order of general recommendation: Full-Body Routine; Push/Pull Split; Upper-Body/Lower-Body Split; Straight-Arm/Bent-Arm Split; Push/Pull/Legs Split (p.80).

**Application:**
- [CONVENTION] Schedule construction must account for the whole of an athlete's life — work, school, family, friends, recreation, leisure — strength and skill goals should not come at the expense of everything else; build a routine that works with, not against, one's life (p.80).
