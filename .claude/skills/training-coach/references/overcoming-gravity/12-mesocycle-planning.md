# OG2 Ch.12 — Mesocycle Planning

> **Source:** *Overcoming Gravity* 2e, Ch.12, book pp.151-170. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/og2.txt` or PDF pp.151-170.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] established research · [CONVENTION] Low's system · [DISPUTED] contested.

## Intra-mesocycle factors — framework (p.151)
- [CONVENTION] Ability is split into four bands mapped to the book's progression-chart levels: Beginner 1-5, Intermediate 6-9, Advanced 10-13, Elite 14-16 (p.151). Each band gets its own scheduling/exercise-selection/cycle-termination rules below.
- [CONVENTION] Typical mesocycle structure: 4-5 microcycles of 1 week each, followed by a rest/deload week (p.151, figure structure).
- [CONVENTION] Weekly (7-day) scheduling is the default only because people are culturally conditioned to think in weeks; 5-day, 6-day, or 2-week schedules are equally valid (p.151).
- [CONVENTION] Mesocycles run 4-8 weeks, then a deload — this cadence is framed as the primary lever against injury and as the mechanism for reading plateau/response data via a training log (p.151).
- Elite-level programming is explicitly out of scope for this chapter: at that level, strength work is mostly about overcoming specific weaknesses and chaining/sequencing strength skills, and transitional movements matter because moving in/out of static skills demands unusual body control (p.151-152).

## Beginner level (1-5): exercises and recommendations (p.152)
- Covers basic bodyweight movement from wall handstands, pushups, dips through pull-ups, ring rows, muscle-ups, and early isometric-hold progressions (p.152).
- Even athletes arriving with outside strength background may still need to start here for skills (handstands, isometrics) because raw strength doesn't substitute for connective-tissue/skill practice; if arriving strong, redirect effort to mobility and joint prep instead of skipping ahead (p.152).
- [CONVENTION] Eccentrics are NOT recommended at beginner level (tough on joints/connective tissue not yet conditioned), with two exceptions: using eccentrics specifically to build toward a first pull-up or dip (p.152).
- [CONVENTION] Levels 1-3 specifically are recommended for women (framed as typically having lower strength-to-bodyweight ratio at the outset) and for anyone on a weight-loss program (p.152).
- Mobility target concretely stated: as levels rise (e.g., L-sit → V-sit progression) hip/hamstring compression flexibility requirements increase; straight-arm press handstand also needs significant compression. Goal markers: chest to knees in pike/straddle, hands flat to floor standing (p.152).
- [CONVENTION] Higher reps (build to 15-20 reps) are recommended for untrained beginners before advancing progressions — mechanism given: ensures adequate technique practice and keeps connective tissue healthy (p.152).
- Mobility/strength focus areas called out: shoulders, elbows, wrists, thoracic spine — cross-referenced to Ch.11 prehab (p.152).

## Beginner level: weekly scheduling (p.153)
- [CONVENTION] Use linear progression or linear repetition progression as the default beginner method; switch methods only once plateaued (p.153).
- [CONVENTION] Expect to progress nearly every workout at this stage; the limiting factor to watch is connective-tissue integrity, not strength/CNS — scale back and add mobility/prehab work if joints complain (explicit priority: health over progress) (p.153).
- Consistency is framed as the single most important beginner variable; sporadic training (1-2x/week or skipped weeks) is called out as the reason people stay beginners "five years or more" (p.153).
- [CONVENTION] Default beginner template: full-body, 3x/week (e.g. M/W/F or Tue/Thu/Sat), giving a rest day between sessions and two off-days after every three workouts (p.153).
- Untrained-beginner needs: learn fundamentals to proficiency; favor higher reps to cement movement patterns and build connective-tissue strength; prioritize personal weakness areas (e.g., posture from desk job); start high-rep/general and transition toward traditional strength training (p.153).
- Trained-beginner needs: emphasize consistency over avoiding all skipped sessions; [CONVENTION] train mainly in the 5-8 rep range for muscular development/strength; keep push/pull balance; add correctional volume (commonly more horizontal pulling for those coming from push-dominant backgrounds) as imbalances emerge; allow time for connective tissue/joint/bone adaptation (p.153-154).

## Beginner level: ending a cycle (p.154)
- [CONVENTION] Two valid mesocycle-termination triggers: (1) plateau — no progression/rep increase across all exercises for a full week, or (2) a fixed 4-8 week block regardless of progress, to bank a recovery week (p.154).
- [CONVENTION] Prefer the fixed 4-8 week block ending if joints/connective tissue are getting sore — reasoning: at beginner level, connective tissue is more often the limiting factor than nervous-system fatigue or muscle recovery (p.154).
- If progress is still happening, continue the cycle rather than forcing an end (p.154).

## Intermediate level (6-9): exercises and recommendations (p.154)
- Target skills: solid handstands progressing to one-arm work, rings handstands, freestanding/straight-arm-press handstand pushups, full back/front lever, straddle planche, past-vertical V-sit, and multi-plane pull/push work (p.154).
- [CONVENTION] Eccentrics become useful here, specifically because pulling exercises respond particularly well to them — named applications: back lever, front lever, one-arm chin-up, and straight-arm handstand eccentrics (pushing) (p.154).
- Gate criterion to progress past intermediate: near-maximal compression flexibility (chest to knees in pike, chest to floor in straddle, hands flat to floor standing with legs straight) — stated as a hard requirement, not optional (p.154-155).
- Once flexibility is adequate, joint/connective-tissue prep becomes more important because upcoming moves (iron cross, one-arm chin-up, back lever, planche) are harder on joints and can cause serious injury without specific preparation (p.155).
- Gray-highlighted chart progressions are specifically for building shoulder/elbow connective-tissue strength en route to iron cross and one-arm chin-up readiness, and are reusable for other connective-tissue-demanding movements (p.155).

## Intermediate level: weekly scheduling (p.155-156)
- [CONVENTION] Typical entry point: ~12-24 months of consistent bodyweight training; heavier athletes (175 lb+) may need longer; athletes from a strength-sport/barbell background should NOT rush here — they may have the raw strength but lack the connective-tissue conditioning for straight-arm work, raising injury risk (p.155).
- [CONVENTION] Rule of thumb for adding a workout day: add one extra day per year of consistent training — framed as "safe and conservative"; adding earlier risks overtraining/burnout (p.155).
- If moving from 3x/week to 4x/week, [CONVENTION] drop to two exercises per push/pull category for at least one cycle, then rebuild volume as conditioning improves (p.155).
- Overtraining warning signs to monitor when adding frequency: loss of appetite, worse sleep quality, decreased motivation — response is to drop back to 3x/week until better base is built (p.155).
- Split options mentioned for more recovery: push/pull, upper/lower, straight-arm/bent-arm; [DISPUTED] three-part splits (push/pull/legs) are "not the best option at this stage" but usable if schedule demands it (p.155-156).
- [CONVENTION] Expected progression cadence: every few workouts to weekly. If simple intra-/inter-exercise progressions stop working, escalate to accumulation/intensification phases or light/heavy training, with light/heavy recommended to try first (p.156).
- Quality > quantity: bodyweight-specific reasoning — technique deteriorates faster under bodyweight loading than under barbell loading because of the skill component, so added volume must be justified by checking (a) week-to-week progress, (b) 24-48h post-workout feeling, (c) sleep/work/family life quality — don't add exercises while under-recovered or sore, especially joint soreness (p.156).
- Training now must specialize by goal rather than staying general: endurance goal → lower strength volume to preserve exercise efficiency plus dedicated endurance work; hypertrophy goal → move from full-body to split routines (cross-ref Ch.5); strength goal → increase frequency as much as possible without overtraining/overuse injury (p.156).

## Intermediate level: ending a cycle (p.156-157)
- [CONVENTION] Expected progression cadence for continuing a cycle: every other workout to weekly, or at minimum every couple weeks — slower progress at levels 8-9 is explicitly normal, not a failure signal, if still pain-free after 8+ weeks (p.156-157).
- [CONVENTION] Termination rule: if literally zero progress after 4 weeks, terminate the mesocycle — reasoning: fatigue masks fitness, so terminating earlier (2-3 weeks) risks stopping before enough accumulated stimulus has been applied for adaptation; the 4-week mark is the floor even with no visible progress, and a stronger showing after the recovery week is expected and should not be scored as a failed cycle (p.157).
- If genuinely no progress occurred, escalate stimulus in the next cycle: add a workout day, add exercises, add sets, or add external load (weight vest) (p.157).
- [CONVENTION] Two consecutive no-progress mesocycles → consult advanced-level programming section for more sophisticated tools (p.157).
- Also terminate early if joints/connective tissue feel sore — same reasoning as beginner level: these are more often the limiting factor than CNS/muscle recovery at this stage; response is a rest week (or two) of prehab focus, downgrading offending exercises to lower-level progressions rather than stopping training entirely (p.157).

## Advanced level (10-13): exercises and recommendations (p.157-158)
- Target skills: iron cross, planche, manna, and combination work across rings/parallettes/floor (p.157).
- [CONVENTION] Consistency alone stops being sufficient here (absent exceptional genetics/training intuition); advanced periodization concepts (from earlier chapters) become necessary (p.157).
- Exercise-selection/frequency principles carry over from lower levels; the lever that changes is manipulating volume/intensity via sets/rep structure across different days (p.157).
- [CONVENTION] DUP-style rep-target math example given: for a target of "10 reps" on an isometric hold, formula = 10 × 2s = 20s hold, i.e. pick a hold you can sustain 20s at your current max. For a high-intensity day such as 5×3 at 3×2s = 6s holds, pick the progression variant (e.g. straddle vs. full planche/front lever) where you can hit 6s holds for all sets (p.158). Reasoning: translates a rep-count periodization scheme onto isometric holds via a fixed "reps × 2s" conversion.
- Modifying difficulty: add ankle weights/weighted vest to increase; use bands/pulley/partner assist to decrease; or drop to an easier positional variant (straddle vs. full) (p.158).
- [CONVENTION] Hybrid sets (chaining a harder variant into an easier one within one set, e.g. 2-3s full planche hold → open to straddle planche for more volume) are recommended specifically for advanced athletes needing extra isometric volume beyond their max hold at the hardest variant. [CONVENTION] Not recommended for beginner/intermediate — reasoning: requires precise tension control while transitioning body positions, which beginners/intermediates typically lack (shoulder/core stabilization) (p.158).
- Isolation exercises: reserved mainly for correcting identified weak links (e.g., biceps curls if lats dominate pulling strength over biceps/pecs) rather than general programming; also useful to offload stress from compound movements onto connective tissues that are getting sore (e.g., swap back-lever volume for biceps curls if elbows ache) while still treating compounds as carrying "the most benefit"; hypertrophy-focused athletes can add isolation on top of compounds (p.158-159).

## Advanced level: weekly scheduling (p.159)
- [CONVENTION] Typical time-to-advanced: 2-4 years consistent training on average, longer (3-5+ years) for higher-bodyweight (175lb+) athletes, depending on genetics/dedication/schedule/recovery (p.159).
- Same "add one workout day per year of training" rule carries over from intermediate level (p.159).
- [CONVENTION] 5-day schedules recommended: M/Tue/W/F/Sat or Tue/W/Thu/Sat/Sun (a 3/1/2/1 rest-day pattern); a straight 5/2 (M-F on, weekend off) is called "slightly less effective" (p.159).
- [CONVENTION] >5 days/week not recommended — easy to burn out. If experimenting with two-a-days: shorten mesocycles to 2-4 weeks (vs. standard 4-8) and/or significantly cut volume per workout (p.159).
- [CONVENTION] Progression path into 6+ days/week: start from a 5-day 3/1/2/1 schedule, adapt to it, then split Saturday's volume into a morning + evening session before working up to two full workouts that day (p.159).
- New concerns entering advanced level: training becomes sport-specific; shoring up weak links via isolation becomes more important; sleep/nutrition/stress management matters more because even 1% improvements move the needle as gains get harder; training-log discipline is called vital for understanding individual response to rest/deload/intensity/volume (p.159-160).

## Advanced level: ending a cycle (p.160)
- [CONVENTION] Progression cadence expected weekly; add reps or progression difficulty at least every 2 weeks — failing that signals a training-factor problem requiring re-examination of periodization approach (p.160).
- Progress may not show until mid-cycle, end-of-cycle, or even after the rest week (especially without DUP) — framed as normal, requiring patience since jumps between advanced levels take substantial time (p.160).
- [CONVENTION] Same 4-week no-progress termination threshold as intermediate, same reasoning (avoid premature termination before enough accumulated stimulus) (p.160).
- Two consecutive no-progress cycles → try light/heavy exercises with high/low reps respectively, or audit sleep/nutrition/stress (p.160).
- Alternative to terminating: adjust the periodization programming mid-cycle if a specific part of the plan is identified (via log review) as ineffective; consider seeking more experienced advice at this point (p.160).
- Same joint/connective-tissue soreness → mandatory termination rule as lower levels, same underlying reasoning (overuse risk vs. simple fatigue) (p.160).

## Inter-mesocycle factors overview (p.160)
- Three factors apply across all ability levels (not tied to beginner/intermediate/advanced): deloading, maximal strength testing, workout restructuring (p.160).

## Deloading (p.160-161)
- [CONVENTION] Goal stated explicitly: supercompensate without losing prior adaptations, by increasing rest — described as requiring experience/coaching to do well because protocols must be varied by individual recovery factors (time, sleep, nutrition) (p.160).
- [CONVENTION] General principle: some exercise during a deload week is more productive than complete rest (p.160-161).
- Deload methods (non-exhaustive, mix-and-match):
  1. **Decrease frequency** — e.g., cut two workout days from the week (p.161).
  2. **Decrease volume** — e.g., keep the schedule but eliminate half the exercises (example given: drop isometrics, keep full-ROM work, since continuing full-ROM work is "more productive" during rest weeks); if a cycle was especially hard on the body, drop all isometrics and focus solely on skill work + prehab (p.161).
  3. **Decrease intensity** — drop all exercise progressions back one level; framed as dual-purpose: deloads joints/connective tissue from intensity while still training them (lighter exercise, prehabilitative) (p.161).
  4. **Other variations** — any combination of frequency/volume/intensity changes: fewer sets, dropped exercises, modified reps, longer rest periods, easier tempo, etc.; deloading is explicitly "not rigid" (p.161).
  5. **Rest weeks with added prehab/stretching** — to reduce scar tissue/adhesions and prepare mobility for the next cycle (p.161).
  6. **Light/novel exercise** — e.g. pickup basketball or a light run, calibrated so you can hold a conversation throughout (keeps intensity low) (p.161).
  7. **"Most common practice"** [CONVENTION] — train only 1-2 days during the recovery week, one exercise per push/pull/legs category, 1-2 sets not-to-failure plus a final set to failure. Reasoning: prevents strength decrements while keeping volume low enough to recover; remaining days go to soft-tissue massage, mobility, prehab, and any needed rehab (p.161).

## Maximal strength testing (p.161-162)
- [CONVENTION] Purpose: find new maxes after a deload week, since the body may supercompensate (gain strength/hypertrophy) once training volume drops and rest increases — testing lets the new mesocycle's targets reflect true new ability rather than undertraining against stale numbers (p.161-162).
- [CONVENTION] Timing: near the end of the deload week, with one rest day before the new cycle starts (example: prior mesocycle ends Sunday, new one starts Monday → test that Saturday) (p.162).
- Rationale for timing: testing after most of the supercompensation has occurred (fatigue dissipated, fitness manifested) gives quality numbers to base the next cycle's progressions/hold times on (p.162).
- [CONVENTION] Testing protocol: (1) typical warm-up; (2) 5-8 reps of a low progression of the movement being tested; (3) a few reps or short holds at the progression under test; (4) rest 3-5 minutes; (5) maximally test the exercise/isometric in question (p.162).
- Note: in advanced training, it's typical NOT to see strength gains until after the supercompensation period completes — i.e., don't expect the test itself mid-fatigue to show gains (p.162).

## Workout restructuring (p.162-163)
- Triggered by achieving goals or re-evaluating priorities; the underlying constraint stated: strength requires progressively repeating movements — frequent, unstructured routine-switching ("workout ADD") caps you at low/intermediate strength and muscle, "high-level strength elusive" (p.162-163).
- On achieving a goal (e.g. full back lever): two options — maintain it with low weekly volume, or drop it and move to the next progressive goal (e.g. front lever); [CONVENTION] push/pull/legs categorization is called out as helping here because of overlap between goals within a category (example: planche pushup strength carries over to handstand pushups and dips), meaning dropped goals are often still retained without practice (p.163).
- [CONVENTION] Maintenance-volume heuristic: if you could previously hold a skill for 30s total volume, add a couple of 5-10s holds after warm-up (before strength work) to maintain it without eating into a full exercise slot or detracting from the main strength session (p.163).
- [CONVENTION] Do not change goals every mesocycle — commit to a goal for at least 2-3 mesocycles before judging it and moving on; if unmet, "put it on hold" and revisit later rather than abandoning outright. Example given: straight-arm press handstand goal may need to be shelved not for lack of strength but for lack of flexibility/compression — work on those prerequisites first (p.163).
- [DISPUTED] A/B routines, push/pull splits, and upper/lower splits are explicitly "not recommended" for this purpose because they reduce per-skill frequency versus full-body routines (specifically flagged for beginners); acceptable only as a deliberate variety trade-off, with the caveat that gains will likely decrease (p.163).

## Elite strength programming (p.163-165)
- [DISPUTED] Elite programming outcomes depend more on the individual (genetics) than on the programming itself — explicit acknowledgment that some athletes (example given: certain women vs. rings-strength skills like the inverted cross) may not reach certain moves even with "decades of perfect training," while historical counter-examples (Lillian Leitzel's reported 27 dynamic one-arm chin-ups in 1918) are cited to argue outcomes are not strictly gender-limited (p.163-164).
- [CONVENTION] Time-to-elite ranges from as little as ~2 years (exceptional genetics) to 10+ years for others — framed as "the luck of the draw" (p.164).
- For athletes expecting a decade-plus timeline: [CONVENTION] recommended tools are DUP and/or heavy/medium/light days, escalating to conjugate or concurrent periodization methods, plus possible supplemental isolation/specific strength work (p.164).
- At this level, sleep and nutrition optimization is framed as a high-leverage remaining lever ("eke out every bit") (p.164).
- The FIG Code of Points contains many rings strength moves beyond this book's charts (maltese, victorian, and Olympic-level combinations) — attainable with sufficient time/dedication (p.164).
- **A Note from Steven Low** (author's personal claim, not sourced to external research) [CONVENTION]: confidence that everyone can reach level 8-9 on the charts regardless of body type, given consistent training commitment; full back lever, full front lever, straddle planche, and "possibly" one-arm chin-ups/iron cross are considered achievable by "everyone" with proper training/nutrition/sleep; the planche may remain elusive for some (p.164). Heavier bodyweight is a disadvantage but not disqualifying — cites Bert Assirati (266 lb) achieving 3 one-arm chin-ups + iron cross, and John Gill (185 lb) achieving maltese cross, inverted cross, iron cross, one-arm chin-ups, one-arm front lever, as evidence the disadvantage is surmountable (p.164-165).
- [CONVENTION] At high-end advanced/elite level, strict adherence to the planned intensity of each day matters more than at lower levels — heavy/intense days must actually be heavy/intense, light days must stay light — reasoning: less margin for programming error as level rises, and recovery factors (nutrition, sleep, stress) must be tightly controlled since "recovery reveals adaptations" (train hard, rest harder) (p.165).
- Bulgarian weightlifting system referenced as an illustrative model: multiple high-intensity sessions per day interspersed with dedicated recovery (mobility, eating, napping, sleeping) — distilled as "Train. Eat. Sleep. Repeat." (p.165).

## Additional programming and mesocycle considerations (p.165-167)
- [CONVENTION] Goal-count heuristic: cap concurrent goals at roughly two pushing, two pulling, two legs (six total) when building a routine; better-conditioned athletes might extend to seven-to-nine exercises rather than the traditional six. Suggested frequency: ~3x/week, adjustable by conditioning/other sporting activity (p.165-166).
- Anecdote (Steven Low, ~5 years prior to writing): 6 straight months focused on handstand pushups + planche (with rest breaks every 6-8 weeks) produced carryover strength — e.g., a subsequent weighted-dip test hit 90lb×5 without direct dip training, and a first-time overhead press attempt succeeded at bodyweight easily — used to argue pushing-exercise overlap means you don't need to train everything simultaneously (p.166).
- [CONVENTION] Recommended cadence: commit to a small goal subset for a full cycle, reassess/change goals only at cycle end (adjustments specifically suggested to happen during the deload week); you may strength-test movements you weren't actively training during the cycle (p.166).
- **Generalized periodization-complexity progression by training age/level** (explicit ordered model, p.166-167):
  1. Linear progression or linear repetition progression — used for most of the beginner phase; add reps/improve progression every workout if possible.
  2. Simple intra- and inter-exercise progressions — used from late-beginner through early-intermediate; increase reps/progression every other workout or so.
  3. Simple periodization (accumulation/intensification phases, or light/heavy rep-alternation) — used mid-to-late intermediate.
  4. Daily undulating periodization (DUP) — used moving into upper-intermediate and advanced phases.
  5. Hybridized light/heavy DUP system — used from mid-advanced level onward; may also introduce concurrent or conjugate periodization if progress stalls.
  6. Concurrent or conjugate system — the expected best fit at the elite level.
- [CONVENTION] Overthinking heuristic: if you catch yourself thinking "maybe I should do X" or "maybe it'd be better if I changed Y" three or four times, that's a signal of over-analysis; consistency, not a "perfect" routine, is the primary driver of progress (p.167).
- [CONVENTION] Change-few-variables principle: altering many program variables at once between cycles destroys your ability to attribute what caused (or failed to cause) progress — whether progress happened or not, you won't know which changes helped/hurt. Keep changes minimal cycle-to-cycle to build accurate self-coaching skill (p.167). Summarized as KISS — "keep it simple, stupid."

## Chapter 12 summary (as stated in book, p.167)
- Different ability levels need different weekly-scheduling and volume (exercise count, intensity, reps, sets) approaches; beginners progress fastest and need the least programming complexity, while intermediate/advanced athletes need more progressive loading or programming complexity.
- Exercise-choice and cycle-termination factors differ by level as detailed above.
- Deloading enables supercompensation and gives a rest-break window for maximal strength testing; workout restructuring lets you pivot focus/goals/exercises; elite strength takes a great deal of time due to both inherent (genetic) and environmental factors.
