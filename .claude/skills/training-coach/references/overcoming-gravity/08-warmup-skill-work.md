# OG2 Ch.8 — Warm-Up and Skill Work

> **Source:** *Overcoming Gravity* 2e, Ch.8, book pp.81-89. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/og2.txt` or PDF pp.81-89.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] established research · [CONVENTION] Low's system · [DISPUTED] contested.

## Goal and structure of a warm-up (p.81)
[CONVENTION] Goal: prime the body into an optimal state for the workout and address movement deficiencies. No universal warm-up exists because every athlete's needs differ; time spent on each component should vary by individual need (p.81).

Three essential components, always in this order (p.81, p.86):
1. Blood Flow
2. Mobility
3. Positional (bodyline/body tension) drills
...followed by Skill Work (a fourth, distinct phase — see below).

**Sample warm-up routine** (~15-20 min total) (p.81):
- Blood Flow: 10-20 Burpees; 60s of Crawling
- Mobility: 10x Wrist Circles; 10x Shoulder Circles; 10x Bodyweight Squats; any other joint/body part needing warm-up; 60s Support Hold Work (parallel bars/rings/chairs/counters); 5x German Hangs/Skin the Cat
- Positional Drills: 30-60s each of Plank, both Side Planks, Reverse Plank, Hollow, Arch Holds
- Skill Work: 5-10min of Handstand Work (against the wall)

## Blood Flow (pp.81-83)
[SCIENCE] Required physiological changes for a good warm-up (p.81-82):
- Raise core temperature → chemical reactions in muscles occur faster → better contractile function and nervous-system activation.
- Elevate heart rate and blood flow to muscles → deliver oxygen/nutrients, export waste.
- Increase blood perfusion to muscles, tendons, connective tissue.
- Prime the brain/nervous system for exercise.
- Saturate joint cartilage with synovial fluid.

[CONVENTION] Recommended exercises: 10-20 Burpees; 60s of Crawling (or crawling a set distance, e.g. 100m) (p.82).
- Burpees: full-body, rapidly raises heart rate, easy to perform, low injury risk. Sequence: plank → pushup → plank → squat → stand → jump → squat → plank, repeated smoothly with no pausing between motions (p.82).
- Crawling: quadruped position, knees lifted a couple inches, contralateral limb pattern (left arm + right leg together, right arm + left leg together), back kept parallel to ground. Activates core musculature and scapular stabilizers; works a wide range of muscles. Rationale for using a fixed distance (e.g. 25m) rather than time: allows measurable improvement over time — 25m is enough to make most beginners lightly perspire (p.82).
- Other acceptable blood-flow options: rolling, squatting, bear walks, crab walks, jumping jacks, jump rope, short jogs, or any low-to-moderate intensity, low-skill, low-injury-risk movement (p.82).

[CONVENTION] Three physiological readiness signs to progress from blood flow → mobility (p.82):
1. Light sweat
2. Moderate increase in heart rate
3. Slight-to-moderate increase in breathing cadence
It's a bonus when the blood-flow exercises themselves segue into mobility work (true of burpees/crawling) (p.82).

## Mobility (pp.82-85)
[CONVENTION] Recap of terms from Ch.4 (p.82-83):
- **Flexibility** – increasing range of motion by stretching muscles via nervous-system relaxation (e.g. working toward the splits).
- **Passive Mobility** – taking a joint through its range of motion without aiming to increase that range (e.g. wrists on floor, body moved over them) — good early warm-up for joints since it doesn't contract the wrist muscles.
- **Active Mobility/Flexibility** – using a *previously gained* range of motion actively (range must first be earned via flexibility training); e.g. actively engaging abs/hip flexors in a pike/straddle stretch, or a standing split/high kick. Book uses "active flexibility" as the common term for this.

Standard mobility warm-up example (p.83):
- 10x Wrist Circles
- 10x Shoulder Circles
- 10x Bodyweight Squats
- any other joint/body part needing warm-up
- 60s Support Hold Work (parallel bars/rings or chairs/counters)
- 5x German Hangs/Skin the Cat

[CONVENTION] Most athletes can skip straight to active mobility (wrist circles, shoulder circles, bodyweight squats). Begin with passive mobility instead if there are particular joint limitations/stiffness, or for older/injured populations — e.g. sore wrists: quadruped position, shift weight passively through the range before progressing to active wrist circles (p.83).

[CONVENTION] Wrist protocol: 15x wrist circles each direction, plus wrist mobility "all ways" on the floor in both flexion and extension (p.83). Shoulders: shoulder dislocates with a band/stick to mobilize scapulae and surrounding musculature (p.83). This mobility segment should take 60-90 seconds (p.83).

[CONVENTION] Elbow/straight-arm warm-up rationale: bodyweight training progressing into advanced straight-arm strength skills makes elbow integrity critical; biceps strength must be built to avoid hyperextension (p.83).

**Straight-arm support-hold protocol** (1-3 sets), target ~60s hold, progressing toward "rings-turned-out" (RTO) — palms forward — which further trains biceps/elbows and warms most shoulder muscles (p.83):
1. Cannot hold straight-arm support at all → start on parallettes/parallel bars.
2. Cannot yet hold straight-arm support on rings → use a theraband to help hold rings together, or get a spotter, until capable (necessary for most beginners).
3. Just started training a non-RTO support hold → jump up straight-armed and accumulate 60-90s total hold time (across sets).
4. Beyond that stage → use as few sets as possible to reach the one-minute mark.
5. Once turning rings out, stay consistent — do not let them drift back in.
6. Once 60-90s RTO achieved with rings in line with the body → widen the rings for further difficulty.

[CONVENTION] Stopping rule: stop when muscles shake significantly or significant bicep pressure appears — this is a warm-up, not a workout; taxing yourself here hinders the workout itself. But including this skill work in the warm-up accelerates strength progress since ring-support proficiency is built through accumulated time on the rings (p.83-84).

[CONVENTION] German hangs / "skin the cat" as dynamic shoulder stretch after ring supports: beginners set rings low, hold 5-10s; more experienced athletes pull out to tuck/pike inverted hang and back. Goal: take shoulders to the edge of range of motion in extension; also stretches chest, lats, anterior shoulder girdle (helps flexion too) (p.84).

[SCIENCE/CONVENTION] Never hold a static stretch >15 seconds during warm-up — longer static holds can decrease strength output during the workout. Three to four short stretches (~1 minute total) is the target (p.84).

**Age-adjusted warm-up duration** (p.84): [CONVENTION] rationale — older or injury-recovering athletes need more warm-up time. Heuristic: age ÷ 4 = extra minutes to add to a 5-10 min base.
| Age | Calculation | Total warm-up |
|---|---|---|
| 20 | 5-10 + (20/4)=5 | 10-15 min |
| 40 | 5-10 + (40/4)=10 | 15-20 min |
| 60 | 5-10 + (60/4)=15 | 20-25 min |
Explicitly a guideline only — modify per individual circumstances/preference (p.84).

[CONVENTION] Warm-ups should evolve as the athlete gets stronger: exercises like dips/pull-ups that become low-intensity/second-nature can migrate into the warm-up itself (p.84).

[CONVENTION] Rule of thumb for warm-up exercise selection relative to current level: use exercises 2-3 levels below current competency on the progression charts. Example: training straddle planche as a primary exercise → warm up with tuck planche pushups (p.84).

## Positional (Bodyline/Body Tension) Drills (pp.85-86)
[CONVENTION] Third critical warm-up component. Most useful drills for bodyweight training: plank, both side planks, reverse plank, arch holds, hollow holds. Sport/discipline-specific alternatives may apply — select exercises that facilitate the required skill work and support proper body positions in strength work (p.85).

[CONVENTION] Definition — **Stability Work**: training focused on neuromuscular re-education — re-teaching the body to move/hold position properly. Examples given: single-leg balance work after an ankle sprain (rebuilds proprioception, activates stabilizing ankle muscles); planks as core stability work to train tension-holding against gravity (p.85).

[CONVENTION] Rationale: positional drills solidify positions needed across many bodyweight exercises and increase core stabilization strength; improving them now improves form/technique later, enabling faster progress and lower injury risk (p.85).

[CONVENTION] Caution on imported warm-up routines: bodyweight training places high demand on connective tissue, so any external warm-up routine should be customized specifically for bodyweight strength training (p.85).

### Stretching placement [DISPUTED — book states an explicit revision from 1st edition] (pp.85-86)
- Static stretching is most beneficial at the **end** of a workout, when the body is warmed up and the nervous system is fatigued (p.85).
- Dynamic stretching (taking muscles through full ROM without holding the end position) belongs in the warm-up; static holds held too long in a warm-up can negatively affect maximal force output during the workout — so pre-workout stretching should be limited unless poor flexibility is inhibiting skill execution (p.85).
- **Change since 1st edition**: mobility work is now recommended *within* the warm-up rather than at the end alongside prehab/flexibility, because mobility exercises prepare the body for high-end skill work and strength development exercises — meaning basic warm-up exercises (support holds, easy pushups/rows) can be intermixed with general mobility (German hangs for shoulders, wrist mobility, bridges for the back) (p.85).

Summary requirement: choose warm-up exercises that increase blood flow, build mobility, and facilitate body awareness (p.85).

## Skill Work: timing, type, qualities (pp.86-88)
[CONVENTION] Skill/technique work always occurs *after* the warm-up — this is the optimal window for the body to learn new skills/movement patterns (p.86). Example: 5-10 min of wall handstand work, resting as much as needed between sets (p.86).

[SCIENCE/CONVENTION] Motor-learning rationale: poor-quality practice gets memorized as incorrect pattern by the body, and is difficult to re-adjust afterward (e.g. loose legs during a handstand). Always prioritize correct form from the start. If fatigue compromises form, stop and rest immediately rather than continuing with degraded technique (p.86).

### What counts as "skill work" vs. strength work (p.86)
[CONVENTION] Definitional distinction, explicitly stated: **the planche, front lever, and back lever are NOT skill work** — despite being straight-arm skills like handstands/L-sits, they belong in the strength portion of a routine because they are trained to build the strength to perform them, not primarily to develop a non-fatiguing attribute like balance.
- **Skill work** = non-fatiguing movements developing specific attributes (chiefly balance). E.g., handstand work is framed as "balancing on your hands" work.
- Handstands, elbow levers, and L-sits are classified as skill work: they demand large time-in-position for balance development, though they also require variable strength; as the athlete gets stronger these become easier.
- **L-sit exception/note**: placed by default in the endurance/core/isolation-work section (most people use it for core work even when paired with handstands) because it is highly fatiguing for beginners, which would degrade capability if placed in the strength-work section. However, if trained explicitly as skill work (e.g., as a stepping stone toward V-sit/manna, where the limiter should not be core strength), it can remain in the skill-work portion (p.86).

### Skill work absorbing former warm-up/strength exercises (pp.86-87)
[CONVENTION] Once skills (handstands) and strength progressions (pull-ups, dips, German hangs) become low-intensity/mastered, they can substitute for a separate warm-up. Example: after mastering a freestanding handstand and moving to freestanding handstand pushups, basic handstand work can be folded into the warm-up to reinforce neural patterns (p.86). Dynamic variants such as handstand shoulder taps (wall handstand, alternately touching a lifted hand to the opposite shoulder) can warm up the body while simultaneously training the skill (p.86-87).

[CONVENTION] General rule: skills/strength progressions 2-3 levels below current competency are appropriate for warm-up or skill work, especially with a significant balance component (handstands, elbow levers) — reference the progression charts. Example: add short tuck front lever holds to warm-up when training the straddle front lever progression as a primary exercise; or substitute a few wall handstand pushups for freestanding handstand pushups to save time while maintaining the movement pattern (p.87).

### Bodyweight progression structure vs. barbell training (p.87)
[CONVENTION/DISPUTED-by-comparison] Contrasted with barbell training, where complex movements (Olympic snatch, clean and jerk) can be learned as a beginner and reach proficiency within months — bodyweight training does not allow this. Bodyweight progression levels are separated by current competency, itself built from prior strength and skill development. Example progression list for the handstand (p.87):
1. Basic static hold (wall → freestanding)
2. Kicking up and holding a basic handstand
3. Handstand walking
4. Developing a proper straight-arm press handstand
5. Obtaining a freestanding bent-arm handstand pushup
6. Obtaining a one-arm handstand
7. Controlling various positions in handstands
8. One-arm handstands
9. One-arm press handstands

[CONVENTION] Progressing in pure bodyweight work is described as extremely difficult without a knowledgeable coach to point to correct progressions/next steps (p.87).

### Skill work vs. strength classification drifts over time (p.87)
[CONVENTION] As an athlete's skill/strength/work capacity improves, exercises once classed as "strength" work may become "skill" work. Example: beginners struggle to hold an assisted handstand 5-10s; as strength increases, the handstand becomes an endurance exercise holdable 60s+ — re-assess goals and exercise classification (skill vs. strength) every 5-6 weeks as training progresses (p.87).

[CONVENTION] Skill level does not necessarily correlate with progression-chart strength level — one can work above or below one's strength level on skill work. With good technique, even relatively weak athletes can work toward one-arm handstands (a skill-dominant feat), though generally greater strength affords greater skill-work potential/ceiling (p.87-88).

### Skill work dosage and fatigue management (pp.87-88)
[CONVENTION] The more skill work is practiced (with adequate recovery), the faster technique improves — but a proper balance must be found; too much practice impairs recovery even for simple skills like handstands (p.87-88).

[CONVENTION] Skill work should be approached like a warm-up: it should not leave the athlete excessively sweaty or tired heading into the strength-training portion. Goal is highest quality of work without inducing fatigue (p.88).
- Beginners: as little as 20 seconds inverted may be appropriate.
- Advanced athletes: 15-20 minutes practicing difficult skills (e.g., one-arm handstand) may be appropriate.
- Heuristic: err toward starting with "too little" skill work and add more only if needed — those who start with too much have difficulty diagnosing what's wrong when progress stalls (p.88).
- Permission to skip: it's acceptable to cut skill work short on a fatigued or "off" day — quality work performed consistently, adapted to daily fatigue/schedule, drives improvement (p.88).
- Closing maxim: "Practice does not make perfect; perfect practice makes perfect." (p.88)

## Chapter 8 Summary (p.89)
A proper warm-up covers three areas:
- **Blood Flow** — burpees, crawling, or other low-to-moderate-intensity, low-skill, low-injury-risk movement (jumping jacks, jump rope, short jogs, rolling, squatting, bear/crab walks).
- **Mobility** — exercises that warm every joint; spend more time on stiff or previously-injured areas. Main recommended long-term mobility exercises: RTO supports and German hangs/skin-the-cats.
- **Positional Drills** — acclimate the body to correct positions and core tension during bodyweight exercises. Work up to 30-60 seconds per drill (plank, both side planks, reverse plank, hollow, arch holds). Can eventually be eliminated as skills improve.

Skill work covers two areas:
- **Sport-specific work**
- **Handstands** (and other skills limited by balance, not strength)

Avoid exercises that are extremely fatiguing in the skill-work portion, since fatigue there degrades the subsequent strength-work portion; otherwise any balance-dominant sport-specific drill is fair game.
