# OG2 Ch.5 — Intro to Programming, Attributes, and the Hierarchy of a Routine

> **Source:** *Overcoming Gravity* 2e, Ch.5, book pp.44-58. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/og2.txt` or PDF pp.44-58.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] established research · [CONVENTION] Low's system · [DISPUTED] contested.

## Overview (p.44)
[CONVENTION] Programming = scheduling: setting order/timing of workouts to progress toward goals. Scales from a single workout, to weekly/biweekly/monthly, up to yearly or quadrennial (Olympic) planning. Long-term planning lets the body constantly progress and lets competitors peak at a targeted time (p.44).

## Intra-Workout Programming and Linear Progression (p.44-46)
- [CONVENTION] The basic unit of programming is the single workout: it must carry enough intensity + volume to stress the body into adapting (strength/hypertrophy), then be followed by adequate rest to recover and supercompensate (p.44).
- Variables available to modify a workout: repetitions, sets, rest, tempo, intensity, volume, frequency, attributes, failure, work capacity, deloading, plateaus. Of these, **intensity and volume are the most important** for structuring a single workout (p.44).
- **Intensity**: modified by increasing exercise difficulty relative to reps performable — via progressing to a harder progression level, adding a weight vest, etc. (p.45).
- **Volume**: modified three ways — (1) reps per set, (2) sets per exercise, (3) total exercises performed (p.45).
- [SCIENCE] Homeostasis: the body maintains tightly regulated internal states; sufficient stress + sufficient recovery → adaptation. Because the body adapts and becomes resistant to a given stress, repeating an identical workout indefinitely stops producing further adaptation — hence progressive overload is required, not repetition (p.45).
- [CONVENTION] Undertraining is "overhyped" — as long as there is step-by-step progressive overload on main exercises it's hard to undertrain; undertraining mainly shows up when there's insufficient intensity/volume, e.g. spreading effort across too few or too scattered exercises (p.45).
- **Worked example — linear progression (Starting Strength, Rippetoe)**: adds 5-10 lb per lift each new workout on core barbell lifts (squat, deadlift, power clean, press, bench), forcing adaptation every session — one of the most effective progression methods for beginners because load itself is infinitely fine-grained (p.45).
  - Monday: Squat 3x5 – 45 lb
  - Wednesday: Squat 3x5 – 55 lb
  - Friday: Squat 3x5 – 65 lb
  - Monday: Squat 3x5 – 75 lb
  - Wednesday: Squat 3x5 – 85 lb
  - Friday: Squat 3x5 – 95 lb
- [CONVENTION] Bodyweight exercises can't add small load increments (barring a weight vest/ankle weights, often inaccessible), so beginners progress mainly via volume, not intensity. Order of preference for volume modification (p.45-46):
  1. Increasing repetitions.
  2. Increasing sets.
  3. Increasing total number of exercises performed.
- Constraint: if using weighted bodyweight progressions, stay in the **5-15 rep range**; don't also change rep speed/tempo or rest between sets — too many simultaneously varying constraints defeats controlled progression (p.46).
- [CONVENTION] Why beginners should *not* add more exercises, three reasons (p.46):
  1. Advanced bodyweight movement patterns are often novel to the body; too many new patterns at once impairs motor learning ("learning ten piano songs at once" analogy) — better to master a few before adding more.
  2. Each added exercise tacks on ~2-3 extra sets; the added volume risks exceeding what the body needs and can cause overuse injury — "for beginners, less is often more."
  3. Spreading across too many goals/exercises causes stagnation, because strength and endurance adaptations sit at different ends of the rep-range spectrum and can't be optimally trained simultaneously — diffuse effort degrades overall quality of improvement.

## Stress, Adaptation, Supercompensation, Fitness, and Fatigue (p.46-48)
- [SCIENCE] Sympathetic ("fight or flight") vs. parasympathetic ("rest and digest") framing: workouts stress the sympathetic system to drive adaptation; recovery activities (sleep, nutrition, massage/sauna/light walking, mobility, meditation, deep breathing, supplements) are parasympathetic and drive the actual repair (p.46).
- [SCIENCE] After a single workout, capacity to perform additional work decreases for roughly the next **24-48 hours**; with adequate rest/nutrition the body then **supercompensates**, rebounding to a level better than baseline (p.46-47).
- [CONVENTION] Training stimulus must clear a minimum threshold to force adaptation (avoid undertraining) but not so much that no supercompensatory rebound occurs at all (avoid excess damage) — "less is often more" (p.47).
- [SCIENCE] A single workout produces both a positive effect (**fitness**) and a negative effect (**fatigue**, i.e. depressed abilities). Fitness from neurological/strength-type adaptation can appear immediately within the same session (comparable to skill gains like balance), but fatigue masks it. Hypertrophy adaptations are slower — protein synthesis driving muscle growth occurs over a **48-72 hour** window post-workout (p.47).
- [CONVENTION] Training again too soon (before fatigue has cleared) means starting the next session below baseline, compounding fatigue further across sessions. This is the **dual-factor theory** (fitness vs. fatigue) as an alternative to the traditional single-factor supercompensation model (p.47-48).
- [CONVENTION] Continuing to accumulate fatigue can still coincide with real strength/muscle progress for a while, but eventually fatigue "catches up" to gains and progress stalls — a plateau is defined as the point where accumulated fatigue equals accumulated gains (p.48).
- Counteracting fatigue = rest: taking full breaks, doing skill work instead, or deloading intensity/reps/sets. For **beginners**, one rest day between workouts is typical (p.48).
- [SCIENCE/CONVENTION] For beginners, **48-72 hours** is cited as the optimal recovery window for a supercompensatory rebound — underlying the common 3x/week beginner schedule (48h between two sessions, 72h after the third) (p.48).
- [SCIENCE] Strength has two components — neurological adaptation and muscular adaptation — and the body builds resistance to both if repeatedly forced to adapt. This is why elite athletes can train nearly daily (sometimes multiple times/day) without excessive soreness once sufficiently adapted; at the highest level, **frequency** becomes the dominant lever for continued strength gains ("strength is a skill" — repeated practice of a movement with progressive overload builds strength, which is also why doing too many different exercises undermines strength gains) (p.48).
- [SCIENCE] Neurological adaptation has the largest capacity for strength gain but adapts *more slowly relative to its potential* than muscle mass does (the "pool" analogy: the nervous system's pool fills slower than the muscle's, even though muscle can be more directly "forced" to grow via heavy lifting + caloric surplus). Example cited: 69 kg Olympic weightlifters snatching 165 kg / clean-and-jerking 198 kg — demonstrating strength is not simply a function of bodyweight/muscle size (p.48).
- [CONVENTION] To overcome this neurological bottleneck, advisable to train **more than three times a week** once past beginner stage — for more advanced athletes some of those extra sessions should be lower intensity rather than pushing maximally every time, trading burnout risk for faster overall improvement. This fitness/fatigue interplay is *why periodization becomes necessary at intermediate/advanced levels* (p.48).

## Basic Periodization and Inter-Workout Structure (p.48-51)
- [CONVENTION] Periodization pioneered by Soviet sports science (1950s-70s) to systematically progress physical capacity by alternating frequency, intensity, volume, reps, etc. over time (p.48).
- Three structural components (classic Soviet model), p.48-49:
  - **Microcycle** — typically ~1 week, organized around a specific attribute focus.
  - **Mesocycle** — a combination of 4-8 microcycles.
  - **Macrocycle** — a combination of mesocycles, often timed so the final mesocycle lands near a competition date, balancing continued hard training against enough dissipated fatigue to peak.
- Classic Soviet microcycle types: preparatory, hypertrophy, strength, power — a typical mesocycle used ~1-2 preparatory + 1 hypertrophy + 1 strength + 1 power microcycle, followed by a deload/rest week → **~6-week mesocycle** (p.49).
- Example 24-week macrocycle structure (figure/table reproduced), showing four mesocycles each ending in a deload week, with emphasis shifting mesocycle-to-mesocycle from preparatory/hypertrophy/strength toward strength/power as the macrocycle progresses (p.49-50):

  | Mesocycle | Microcycle sequence |
  |---|---|
  | 1 | Prep, Prep, Hypertrophy, Hypertrophy, Strength, **Deload** |
  | 2 | Prep, Hypertrophy, Hypertrophy, Strength, Strength, **Deload** |
  | 3 | Prep, Hypertrophy, Strength, Strength, Power, **Deload** |
  | 4 | Prep, Hypertrophy, Strength, Power, Power, **Deload** |

- Attribute/intensity ranges by microcycle phase (Soviet structure), each with its rationale (p.50):
  - **Preparatory phase** — slow ramp-up of volume and intensity coming off a deload/off week; a "ramp in" for serious training.
  - **Hypertrophy phase** — ~60-85% 1RM (~5-12 reps).
  - **Strength phase** — ~80-100% 1RM (~1-8 reps).
  - **Power phase** — lighter, ~40-60% 1RM (~3-6 reps), emphasizing pure acceleration.
  - **Dynamic Effort (DE) / speed work** (Westside Barbell) — ~40-60% 1RM plus accommodating resistance (chains/bands, ~25-30% 1RM additional); trains bar acceleration and addresses sticking points via the variable resistance curve (p.50-51).
- [CONVENTION] Why the weekly microcycle is the practical default: (1) most occupations run on a 7-day week, so weekly scheduling is logistically convenient; (2) microcycles teach that volume must be managed *weekly*, not workout-to-workout — as the athlete gets stronger/more muscular, the body resists stress more, so overload has to be engineered at the weekly-structure level rather than per-session, or long plateaus result (the "gym veteran who looks the same for years" example is attributed to never adopting a structured progressive-overload framework beyond single workouts) (p.51).
- [CONVENTION] As athletes become sufficiently advanced, the planning unit lengthens: biweekly/monthly for advanced, yearly for pro athletes, quadrennial for Olympic athletes — greater programming complexity tracks with training age (p.51-52).
- [CONVENTION] For beginners, mesocycles (not macrocycles) are the operative planning unit, generally **4-8 weeks**, length depending on when plateau/fatigue accumulation catches up; followed by a rest week to dissipate fatigue, let aches/pains resolve, plan prehab/rehab, reassess goals, and plan the next mesocycle (p.52).
- [CONVENTION] Rest weeks are "extremely critical" to progress — the book estimates only ~10% of bodyweight athletes know how to properly use them, implying most people undertrain their recovery structure rather than their work structure (p.52).
- Recommended further reading on periodization theory: *Supertraining* (Mel Siff, described as dense), *Science and Practice of Strength Training* (Vladimir Zatsiorsky), *Periodization: Theory and Methodology of Training* (Tudor Bompa) (p.52).

## Attribute Training (p.51-54)
- [CONVENTION] Core framing questions when constructing a routine (p.52):
  - Can I do this exercise every day?
  - Can I do this exercise on my off days?
  - Can I do this exercise with my workouts?
- [CONVENTION] Every exercise in a routine should serve a purpose, categorizable by the attribute it trains. Example attribute list: strength, hypertrophy, endurance, cardiovascular, mobility, flexibility, stability, skill, endurance, etc. Cites Cawley/Evans (Dynamax) origin of the ten CrossFit-popularized physical attributes: cardiovascular/respiratory endurance, stamina, strength, flexibility, power, speed, coordination, agility, balance, accuracy (p.52).
- [CONVENTION] This book's scope: strength and hypertrophy specifically, via isometrics (planche, front lever, back lever) and full-ROM strength exercises (pushups, dips, handstand pushups, rows, pull-ups) (p.52).
- **The three primary workout attributes**, differentiated by rep intensity per set and matched rest (each with its stated mechanism) (p.52):
  - **Strength** — 1-8 RM, close-to-failure but *not*-to-failure training, ~3-minute rest. (Rest length matched to near-max effort recovery needs, implied by contrast with hypertrophy/endurance ranges.)
  - **Hypertrophy** — 5-15+ RM, failure training, 1-3 minute rest.
  - **Endurance** — 15-20+ RM, failure training, 30-90 second rest.
- [CONVENTION] Cardiovascular training frequency is intensity-dependent — three tiers and how each fits the weekly structure (p.52-53):
  - **Recovery** intensity (light, blood-flow only, ends feeling better than the start) → can be done every day.
  - **Moderate** intensity (general health, not targeting cardio adaptation specifically) → fits on a rest day.
  - **High/workout-focused** intensity (deliberately training cardio capacity for a race/competition) → becomes its own dedicated workout and may displace a strength/hypertrophy/endurance session, because high-intensity work of any kind detracts from recovery and thus impairs other parts of the routine.
- [CONVENTION] Attribute-specific frequency notes (p.53):
  - **Flexibility** — technically trainable daily, but it is an *eccentric* exercise: as muscle elongates, passive tension ramps up (a protective nervous-system response against dangerous ranges), so stretching produces real soreness/fatigue that can interfere with other training — e.g. heavy leg-flexibility work paired with heavy leg-exercise volume can impair performance. Must be weighed against the rest of the routine's demands, not assumed to be "free."
  - **Mobility** — trainable every day; takes a joint actively/passively through range of motion without targeting a specific performance attribute; supports long-term joint health — [SCIENCE] loss of mobility/strength with aging correlates with increased mortality.
  - **Skill work** — frequency depends on intensity: for beginners, some skills (e.g. handstands) are strength-limited and thus belong in the actual workout; once strength is no longer the limiter and balance becomes the priority, skill work can be trained more frequently, even daily, if it's balance-focused. Isometrics like planche/back lever/front lever are *not* balance/skill work — they train strength and are therefore workout-bound, not daily-frequency items.
- [CONVENTION] Three-tier categorization for slotting attributes into a weekly structure (p.53-54):
  - **Workout Factors** — generally 2-4x/week; require significant inter-session recovery due to high intensity/failure training/high volume; higher frequency variation reserved for experienced program designers.
  - **Recovery and Optimization Factors** — 3x/week up to daily; facilitate recovery via movement, soft-tissue work, or low-intensity work that increases blood flow; should leave the athlete feeling better and not impair later sessions (lighter flexibility, recovery jogs/walks, mobility, light skill work).
  - **Rehabilitation Factors** — specific to injury; not always about pure strength gain; typically progress from low-intensity to higher-intensity work over the rehab phase; high reps at low intensity, usually performed after a routine or on off days.
- [CONVENTION] Governing principle for this section: "everything depends" — the routine template chosen constrains only the *main* trainable attributes (strength, hypertrophy, and/or endurance); skill work, flexibility, mobility, prehab, and rehab can be scheduled flexibly on off-days regardless of the main template, giving athletes latitude many feel a fixed routine template denies them (p.54).

## The Basic Hierarchy of a Routine (p.54-58)
- [CONVENTION] Ordering rule for maximizing in-workout training ability and carryover to subsequent sessions — the five-part canonical hierarchy for a single workout, in strict order (p.54):
  1. Warm-Up and Mobility
  2. Skill or Technique Work (handstands, flips, gymnastics tumbling, breakdancing work, etc.)
  3. Power, Isometrics, Eccentrics, Regular Strength Work
  4. Endurance, Metabolic Conditioning, Tabata Method, Interval Training, specific exercises, etc.
  5. Prehabilitation, Flexibility Work, Cool Down
- Note: not every category must appear in a given routine, nor must all categories occur on the same day (p.54).
- [CONVENTION] Strength training gets the most detailed treatment in the book overall, but all categories are discussed for how they interact with strength work and with sport-specific application (p.54).
- **Worked full example routine** (~Level 3-4 on the bodyweight progression charts; credited as informed by Roger Harrell, Blair Lowe, Jim Bathurst, Ido Portal, and the r/bodyweightfitness beginner-routine community — phi, phrakture, SM, m092, iscg, antranik, kayetech, Solfire) (p.54-56):

  **Goals** (stated as arbitrary examples): 10 freestanding handstands; 5 strict muscle-ups; 5 planches; 5 front levers; 10 pistols; 10 vertical V-sits; 5 iron crosses.

  **Warm-Up**
  1. Blood flow: 10-20 burpees, 60s crawling (or ~100m variable-distance crawling).
  2. Mobility: 15x wrist circles, shoulder circles, bodyweight squats (and any other joint needing warm-up); 60s support-hold work (parallel bars/rings/chairs/counters); 5x skin-the-cat / German hangs.
  3. Positional drills: 30-60s each of plank, both side planks, reverse plank, hollow hold, arch hold.
  - Organizing principle stated explicitly: classification of exercise → amount of repetitions → type of exercise.

  **Skill Work**: 5-10 minutes of handstand work against the wall.

  **Strength Work** — all main lifts at 3x5→15 (i.e., work up from 5 toward 15 reps across the progression), 3 minutes rest, 10x0 tempo:
  - Pull-ups
  - Dips
  - Wide ring rows
  - Rings pushups
  - Squats (pistol progression or barbell)
  - Deep step-ups
  - L-sit for 60s total, in as many sets as needed, **not to failure**
  - Compression work, 3x10s
  - Organizing principle stated explicitly: exercise → exercise order → sets x reps with progressive principle → rest time → tempo.

  **Prehabilitation, Isolation Work, Flexibility Work, Cool Down**:
  - 3x1 minute rice-bucket work for the wrists
  - 3x10 biceps curls
  - 3-5x30s splits holds
  - 3-5x30s German hangs
  - 3-5x20s back bridges
  - 1 minute deep breathing (in through nose, out through mouth)

- [CONVENTION] Practical construction checklist for readers building their own routine from goals + progression charts (p.56):
  1. Write down desired skill work and how you'd order exercises per the hierarchy above.
  2. Write down previous injuries / problematic joints, muscles, tendons, and what prehab/rehab has already been done for them.
  3. These factors will later be integrated to avoid contraindicated movements while still working toward goals.
  4. Self-assess soft-tissue structures by hand for tightness/trigger points/restrictions around joints; record findings.
  5. Check and record flexibility/mobility, specifically at minimum: wrists, elbows, shoulders, straddle stretch, pike stretch.
  - Exercise-selection guidance once goals + progression charts are combined:
    - Skill-based exercises being actively practiced should be **at least one or more levels below current competency**, so they can be drilled without significant fatigue interfering.
    - Push/pull goals: generally **1-2 goals** with a total of **2-3 exercises** planned toward them at a time.
    - Selected exercises must avoid aggravating any existing injuries; where working around an injury, include prehab/rehab work so it doesn't linger.
    - Upper-level bodyweight strength skills demand high flexibility, so athletes from non-flexibility athletic backgrounds will likely need substantial hip and shoulder flexibility work.
    - Sequencing: warm-up, skill development, and mobility first; strength/power work in the middle; cool-down, flexibility, and rehab last.
    - Final step: order all selected exercises according to the athlete's specific goals/priority areas.

## Chapter 5 Summary (as stated in book, p.56-58)
- Programming = changing routines via intensity, volume, and frequency to drive progressive overload; beginners typically progress via reps/sets/exercise-count increases (p.56).
- At more advanced levels, understanding weekly-scale stress/adaptation dynamics (fitness vs. fatigue) matters because it enables planning gains through supercompensation rather than working workout-to-workout (p.56).
- Attribute training = understanding how a routine's individual parts function so they can be adjusted on the fly; many attributes (flexibility, mobility, skill work, etc.) needn't live solely inside the workout and can be distributed across the week (p.56).
- Restated basic components of a routine: Goals; Warm-Up (blood flow, mobility, position drills); Skill Development; Strength Work (followed by other work as needed, e.g. endurance/cross-training); Prehabilitation, Isolation Work, Flexibility Work, and Cool Down (p.56).
- Warm-up purpose restated: raise core temperature, bring nervous system and muscles to full operating capacity; light prehab/mobility/stretching can be folded in if it benefits the coming workout (p.56).
- Skill development can be folded into the warm-up when time-constrained, favoring lower-intensity skills that need high practice volume (p.56).
- Strength work restated as comprising power, eccentrics, isometrics, and dynamic movements — the routine's core, potentially followed by other work as necessary (p.56).
- Cool-down restated: rehab/prehab plus isolation work (to address or prevent injury conditions); mobility/flexibility work is better placed here since the body is more responsive once the nervous system and muscles are already fatigued from training (p.56).
