# OG2 Ch.18 — Trained Beginner: Routine Construction and Progression

> **Source:** *Overcoming Gravity* 2e, Ch.18, book pp.231-238. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/og2.txt` or PDF pp.231-238.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] established research · [CONVENTION] Low's system · [DISPUTED] contested.

## Who this stage is for (p.231)
[CONVENTION] "Trained" beginners arrive from weight training, sports, or other organized athletic activity — distinct from "untrained" beginners with no athletic background. They fall anywhere from Level 2-6, but the vast majority land in that range; if entering at Levels 2-4, the book recommends using the untrained-beginner chapter instead because progress will be faster starting there. This section specifically addresses Levels 5-6 (p.231).

## Needs of the trained beginner (p.231)
[CONVENTION] Five priorities, in the book's own framing:
1. **Consistency in training** — discipline is the most important factor in making progress; do not skip workouts except for overuse injury or emergency. "The best program is the one you stick to."
2. **Training that ensures good muscular development and strength**, especially the 5-8 repetition range.
3. **Proper balance of pushing and pulling exercises** in the routine.
4. **Add exercises to maintain structural balance** if imbalances start to develop — typically more horizontal pulling if the athlete trained mostly pushing before coming to bodyweight training.
5. **Allow the body to accommodate to strength training** so connective tissues and underlying structures (joints, bones) can adapt — i.e., tissue adaptation lags behind muscular adaptation and needs dedicated time even for someone already athletic.

[CONVENTION] This stage requires a philosophy shift: since freestanding handstands are nearly mastered, focus shifts toward press-to-handstand, rings, shoulder stands. Skills like forward roll to support and kip to support enter the picture. Rings strength becomes more integrated, and isometric exercises should progress from tuck/advanced-tuck positions toward straddle and layout positions (p.231).

## Goal-setting and routine focus (p.232)
[CONVENTION] At this stage there is a much wider variety of skills to learn, so the book stresses keeping the routine focused: limit concurrent goals to **two or three**. Rationale given — it is better to focus and achieve a single goal than to chase many goals and achieve none. Once a few goals are reached, shift to new goals while maintaining the skills already developed (p.232).

## Routine structure (p.232)
[CONVENTION] A full-body routine still works best at this stage, performed **3-4x/week**. This is the first stage at which a split routine becomes viable (for time constraints or variety). Acceptable splits: push/pull, straight-arm/bent-arm, upper/lower. Explicitly avoid a three-part split like push/pull/legs — instead, integrate legs into a dual push/pull split (squats and pistols count as pushing, deadlifts and deep step-ups count as pulling) (p.232). Split choice depends on schedule and any sport practiced — e.g., an upper/lower split suits someone whose sport is lower-body-dominant.

[CONVENTION] Straight-arm work (handstands, L-sit/V-sit/manna progression) must not be neglected — at this level, this skill work is "critical" (p.232).

### Sample goals used to illustrate routine construction (p.232)
- 10 Freestanding Handstands

- 5s Iron Cross

### Sample static-focused routine (p.232)
- X seconds of Tuck or Advanced Tuck Planche
- X seconds of Straddle or Half Layout Back Lever
- X seconds of Advanced Tuck Front Lever
- 3x5→15 of Strict Muscle-ups, 3 min rest, 10x0 tempo

- 3x5→15 Deep Step-ups (weighted if necessary), 3 min rest, 10x0 tempo
- Straddle L-Sit or RTO L-Sit for 60s total, in as many sets as needed, not to failure

### Sample dynamic-focused routine, three push / three pull (p.232-233)
- 3x3→15 of Tuck and Advanced Tuck FL Pull-ups, 3 min rest, 10x0 tempo
- 3x3→15 of Tuck PL Pushups, 3 min rest, 10x0 tempo
- 3x3→15 of Strict Muscle-ups, 3 min rest, 10x0 tempo
- 3x3→15 of Archer Ring Rows, 3 min rest, 10x0 tempo
- 3x5→15 Squats (pistol progression or barbell), 3 min rest, 10x0 tempo
- 3x5→15 Deep Step-ups (weighted if necessary), 3 min rest, 10x0 tempo
- Straddle L-Sit or RTO L-Sit for 60s total, in as many sets as needed, not to failure

## Worked example: a real athlete's 4x/week push/pull routine (p.233-235)
[CONVENTION] Structure: M/Tue/Thur/F, push on M/Thur, pull on Tue/F.

**Monday (Push):**
- Push Pre-Hab: Wrist Pushups 1x10, Dumbbell Finger Curls 1x10, Straight-Arm Dumbbell Rotation 1x10
- Handstands: 10 minutes of Freestanding Handstands
- 8x5s of Tuck Planche
- 3x5 of Ring Dips
- 3x30s of Planche Leans
- 3x5 of Natural Hamstring Curls
- +Legs (Squat Variation)

**Tuesday (Pull):**
- Pull Pre-Hab: Wrist Pushups 1x10, Dumbbell Finger Curls 1x10, Straight-Arm Dumbbell Rotation 1x10, External Rotations or Cuban Presses, Wall Extensions (facing wall, band on forearms)
- Handstands: same as Monday
- 16x8s of Advanced Tuck Front Lever
- 5x3 of Advanced Tuck Front Lever Pull-ups
- 4x3 of False Grip Pull-ups
- +Legs (Deadlift Variation)

**Thursday (Push):**
- Push Pre-Hab: same as Monday
- Handstands: same as Monday
- 5-6x5s of Advanced Tuck Planche Eccentrics
- 4x2 of HSPU (stomach to wall, on parallettes)
- 3x30s of Planche Leans
- 3x5 of Natural Hamstring Curls
- +Legs (Squat Variation)

**Friday (Pull):**
- Pull Pre-Hab: same as Tuesday
- Handstands: same as Monday
- 5x5s of Front Lever Negatives
- 3x6-8 reps of One-Arm Rows
- 4x3 of False Grip Pull-ups
- +Legs (Deadlift Variation)

[CONVENTION] Athlete's self-report context: 5'8", 215 lbs, ~8% body fat, type-1 diabetic with tight blood-sugar control, strict Paleo diet. He progressively dropped extra statics on push days as the cycle went on, and pulling days progressed from advanced-tuck front lever through single-leg eccentrics to full front lever eccentrics. He rated this "by no means a perfect program" — L-sit work was inconsistent, and exercise selection/rep ranges/volumes varied significantly exercise to exercise — yet still made very good progress. The book's takeaway: "Enough cannot be said about the importance of constructing your own routine over using the examples blindly" — figuring out what does/doesn't work for one's own body, while following general principles of routine construction, matters more than copying a template (p.234-235).

## Adjustments for heavier individuals (p.235)
[CONVENTION] For individuals weighing ~200 lbs, a **4x/week program with two push and two pull workouts** tends to work best; upper/lower and straight-arm/bent-arm splits are also effective. Full-body 3x/week may be **too intense** for heavier athletes, especially when the routine includes exercises like the planche — [SCIENCE-adjacent reasoning implied but not cited numerically] greater body mass raises the absolute load on straight-arm/static positions, so recovery demand rises. A straight-arm/bent-arm split can run M/Tue/Thur/F with straight-arm work M/Thur and bent-arm work Tue/F.

### Straight-Arm day template (p.235)
- X seconds of Tuck or Advanced Tuck Planche
- X seconds of Straddle or Half Layout Back Lever
- X seconds of Advanced Tuck Front Lever
- 3x(3-10) of Straight-Arm Press Eccentrics / Elevated SA Press
- RTO Support Holds
- +Legs

### Bent-Arm day template (p.235)
- 3x(3-10) of Wall HSPUs or Freestanding HSPUs
- 3x(3-10) of Tuck and Advanced Tuck FL Pull-ups
- 3x(3-10) of Tuck PL Pushups
- 3x(3-10) of Strict Muscle-ups
- 3x(3-10) of Archer Ring Rows
- +Legs

[CONVENTION] An alternating straight-arm/bent-arm routine is described as, in a sense, a hybrid of static and dynamic training. Splitting the two modes onto separate days is not mandatory but suits those who prefer to give each mode a more intense, focused session (p.235). Upper/lower splits are called "self-explanatory."

## TRAINED BEGINNER: ROUTINE PROGRESSION (p.235)

### Consistency as the primary lever (p.235-236)
[CONVENTION] The central message: consistency is prized above flawless execution. Explicit example — hitting 6/6 full-body sessions in two weeks beats hitting a "flawless" 5/6; going 12/12 over four weeks beats switching to a better routine mid-cycle and running it for the remainder. Rationale: consistent training builds habits (working out when exhausted/unmotivated, making time around obligations) that compound over the long run, and the discipline itself is framed as a transferable life skill, not just a training input (p.236).

### Repetition ranges (p.236)
[CONVENTION] Bring reps down into the **5-12 range** and hold them there as the default for strength+hypertrophy. Occasional excursions to **15-20 reps** are acceptable specifically for connective-tissue conditioning on progressions that aggravate particular body areas — i.e., higher-rep, lower-intensity work is gentler on tendons/ligaments while still training the pattern. If biasing more toward pure strength within this stage, shift to the **3-8 range** instead (p.236).

### Routine balance / structural balance maintenance (p.236)
[CONVENTION] By this stage posture and technique should already look good; if not, continue correcting imbalances carried over from prior sport/training. Guidance:
- Poor posture (commonly head/shoulders hunched forward) → add an additional horizontal rowing motion, since this is the most frequent imbalance pattern, especially with a desk job. Specific stretching also helps.
- If the athlete's *prior* sport was pulling-dominant (swimming, rock climbing) rather than pushing-dominant, they may instead need an **additional horizontal pushing exercise** — the correction direction depends on the individual's training history, not a fixed rule.
- Diagnostic heuristic: check relative progression-chart levels between pushing and pulling. Pushing is typically naturally a bit stronger than pulling; but if pushing is **more than one full progression ahead** of pulling, or pulling is at a *similar* progression to pushing (i.e., not appropriately behind), that signals an imbalance requiring attention.
- Once posture is corrected, the ongoing maintenance goal is to keep the *count* of pushing and pulling exercises numerically equal in the routine (e.g., 2 upper push ↔ 2 upper pull) — both for health and because doing so surfaces specific weaknesses to address later (p.236).

### Routine length (p.236)
[CONVENTION] Same recommendations as the untrained-beginner chapter apply. At this stage, the athlete should already know how to modify routine length for time constraints — e.g., moving skill work, flexibility work, isolation work, or prehabilitation work to rest days, which works because all strength work is concentrated on full-body training days (p.236).

### Additional skill work (p.236-237)
[CONVENTION] It may be beneficial to add extra skill work on rest days as well as workout days at this point. Contextual note: for untrained beginners, handstands 6-7 days/week can be too much for the wrists; the transition from untrained to trained beginner can take **3-12 months**, by which point the athlete should know their own wrist/shoulder/connective-tissue tolerance — work capacity for skill practice tends to rise as mobility/prehab work continues (p.236-237).

[CONVENTION] Protocol for adding skill work (ordered, exact increments from the text):
1. Add **one extra day per week** of skill work.
2. Hold that for **at least two weeks**.
3. If tolerated, add **another extra day**.
4. Repeat this pattern (one addition, ≥2-week hold, reassess) for further additions.

Rationale given: the two-week buffer lets the body adjust to the new workload before more is added; adding too much too fast risks soreness or real pain. Going slow and steady in two-week increments, by contrast, yields rapid proficiency gains once additional skill work is introduced — described as paying off "immediately" (p.237).

### Strength progression methods (p.237)
[CONVENTION] As athletes move into the trained-beginner range, simple linear progression (e.g., 5-5-5 with 5 lb → 10 lb → 15 lb) or linear repetition progression (5-5-5 → 6-6-6 → 7-7-7) will likely start to fail — i.e., stall as the easy early-stage adaptations are exhausted. Two replacement methods are named as working "very well":
- **Set Addition**: 6-6-6 → 6-6-6-6 → then 7-7-7 (add a set at the current rep target before raising the rep target across all sets).
- **Last Set to Failure**: 5-5-5 → 5-5-6 → 5-5-7 → 6-6-6 (push only the final set toward failure/higher reps first, then roll the gain into all sets).

Other progression methods (repetition addition, tempo changes, density modifications) can be useful, but set addition and last-set-to-failure are called out as the best methods for trained beginners specifically. Once *those* two stop producing progress, the book directs the athlete to shift to manipulating **density** and to **accumulation/intensification** as the new progression methods — framed explicitly as the bridge into the intermediate stage (p.237).

### Bridging progression gaps with hybrid sets (p.237)
[CONVENTION] Simple inter-exercise progressions (moving wholesale to the next-harder exercise variant) are still appropriate at this stage but athletes are likely to stall on them. Weight addition (ankle weights on the current progression) or band-assisted work on the next progression are called the most "intuitive" bridging methods for beginners, but **hybrid sets** are named as the *most effective* bridge for most people. Example protocol (exact numbers from text, ordered):
1. 3 sets of 10 Pull-ups
2. 3 sets of (1 Wide Grip Pull-up, followed by 6 Pull-ups)
3. 3 sets of (2 Wide Grip Pull-ups, followed by 4 Pull-ups)
4. 3 sets of (3 Wide Grip Pull-ups, followed by 2 Pull-ups)
5. 3 sets of (4 Wide Grip Pull-ups, followed by 0 Pull-ups)

Hybrid sets can also be combined with weight addition on the easier exercise, for athletes who want volume on a harder variant while anchored to a familiar movement. Guidance: log which progression method is used and chart results, since different progressions work better for different bodies — no universal winner is claimed (p.237).

### Overuse modifications (p.237-238)
[CONVENTION] Overuse patterns and their fixes are similar to the untrained-beginner stage, but the required spacing between modifications lengthens as the athlete approaches intermediate/advanced exercises. Two standing modifications reiterated:
- Eliminate, reduce volume, or substitute exercises.
- Add prehabilitation, isolation, mobility, or flexibility work for the specific affected area as needed.

The book directs readers with soreness/discomfort/pain in connective tissues to keep prehab/mobility work going, and — if overwhelmed — consult a medical professional (orthopedic sports doctor, physical therapist) or seek input from coaches/advanced athletes/online communities (p.238).

### Avoiding injuries (p.238)
[SCIENCE] Restated verbatim principle from earlier in the book: the number one predictor of an injury is a previous injury. [CONVENTION] Learning to modify a routine based on the body's response — particularly around potential overuse injuries — is called "THE most important thing" an athlete will learn about training, framed as a durable, decades-long skill applicable to both self-training and coaching others (p.238).
