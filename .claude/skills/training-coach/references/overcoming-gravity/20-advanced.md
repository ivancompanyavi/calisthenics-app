# OG2 Ch.20 — Advanced: Routine Construction and Progression

> **Source:** *Overcoming Gravity* 2e, Ch.20, book pp.251-262. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/og2.txt` or PDF pp.251-262.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] established research · [CONVENTION] Low's system · [DISPUTED] contested.

## What changes at the advanced level (p.251)
- Training becomes highly sport/goal-specific rather than general. [CONVENTION]
- Shoring up weak links becomes vastly more important to keep progressing — many athletes have a weak link in the back or quads if posterior-chain dominant; targeted isolation work (e.g., biceps curls if back is much stronger than arms) becomes worthwhile at this stage even though it's less needed earlier (p.251). [CONVENTION]
- Sleep, nutrition, and stress management matter at every level, but at the advanced stage even a 1% improvement compounds because raw strength/mass gains become hard to come by (p.251). [CONVENTION]
- A training log becomes essential: reviewing how the body responded to specific rest periods, deloads, and intensity/volume choices materially eases multi-week planning (p.251). [CONVENTION]
- Programming centers on transitioning to "B-skill" (impressive/advanced) work and requires more complex programming — full implementation of DUP (Daily Undulating Periodization) protocol and push/pull modifications, which the book states is the most effective approach at this point, though traditional periodization can still be used (p.251). [CONVENTION]

## Worked DUP examples (pp.252-254)
The book presents real programs (not templates to copy verbatim, but illustrations of the DUP mechanism).

**Example 1 — Iron cross goal, ~3x/week DUP** (p.252):
- Mon: 3x10 (higher-rep day) across cross pulls, archer rows, straddle FL rows, hanging leg lifts, pistols.
- Wed: 3x7 with added weight (e.g., +15 lb) on the same movements.
- Sat: 3x4 with heavier added weight (e.g., +25 lb) on the same movements.
- Rationale: repetitions are held roughly consistent across a wave (10 RM / 7 RM / 4 RM) while weight is modified — this is the "similar reps, variable weight" DUP variant (p.252-253). [CONVENTION]
- Result cited: cross pulls, archer rows, and straddle FL rows all increased 10-15 lb in added weight after one week at 3x10, comparing first Monday to second Monday — described as "a substantial strength gain" (p.253). [CONVENTION/anecdote]
- Caveat: this particular athlete had poor nutrition at the time and progress devolved after the first two weeks, illustrating that nutrition can cap DUP progress even when the protocol itself works (p.253). [CONVENTION]

**Example 2 — Light/heavy ~2x/week, iron cross goal** (p.254):
- Progression shown: 3x10 → 3x5 (heavier) → 3x8 → mixed rep/weight combos, ending in low-rep near-max attempts (1x8 bodyweight + heavy singles/triples).
- Mechanism: volume and RM target are manipulated across the cycle, alternating 10 RM and 5 RM, then trending toward 8 RM and 4-5 RM (more strength-biased), finishing with 3 RM work by cycle end (p.254). [CONVENTION]
- General principle: instead of holding fixed RM targets (10/7/4) constant, you can run a hypertrophy-biased scheme early and slowly bias toward strength later — the book states this is "extremely beneficial because the modern DUP protocol is better than traditional periodization at maintaining attributes" while shifting emphasis (p.254). [DISPUTED — book's own claim, not sourced to a citation in-chapter]

**Example 3 — Weighted dip single-exercise log** (pp.253-255):
- A day-by-day log of weighted-dip sessions is given (dates, weight x reps x sets notation, "F" = failed rep).
- Over a 3-week ramp-up, +15 lb was added to a weighted dip 1RM starting from a previous 1RM of +155 lb (bodyweight 135 lb), after a layoff of a couple months (p.254-255).
- RM progression across sessions: 5, 5, 4, 3, 3, 4, heavy eccentrics (~1-2 RM), 5, 2, 1, 3, 1 — reps trend down, weight trends up almost every session; the book calls this a way to "add volume, which helps force adaptations," letting you aim for a PR even when already advanced (p.255). [CONVENTION]
- Explicit caveat: the book states this program was "by no means optimal" — ramping to a 1RM this fast is "incredibly taxing" and under-delivers strength-stimulus volume. A better progression: start with more reps and stick to 2-3 RM territory longer — e.g., 8 RM → 6 RM → 4 RM → slowly down to 2-3 RM — rather than crashing toward singles quickly (p.255). [CONVENTION/self-critique]

## Push/pull + light/heavy hybrid system (pp.255-258)
- Rationale: separating push and pull across different days adds volume without compounding fatigue on the same tissue same-day; particularly effective for hypertrophy because of the added volume (p.255). [CONVENTION]
- Example split, 4-5x/week: Mon Heavy Push, Tue Light Pull, Wed Rest, Thu Light Push, Fri Heavy Pull, Sat/Sun Rest (p.255).
- Rep-range mechanism: heavy days ≈ 3-5 reps, light days ≈ 5-8 reps (p.256).
  - Bias toward strength: shift to heavy 1-5 reps / light 3-6 reps.
  - Bias toward hypertrophy: shift to heavy 3-8 reps / light 6-12 reps.
  - Alternating focus week-to-week is "completely acceptable and even encouraged if you hit a plateau" (p.256). [CONVENTION]

**Worked example routine** (p.256):
| Day | Exercises |
|---|---|
| Heavy Push | 3x4 Straddle Planche Pushups; 3x5 Rings Freestanding HSPUs; 4x5 90° RTO Dips; + Legs |
| Light Pull | 3x8-10 Straddle Front Lever Pull-ups; 3x8 Pulley-assisted (20-lb assist) OAC; 3x6 German Hang Pullouts; + Legs |
| Light Push | 3x10 Advanced Tuck Planche Pushups; 2x7 Rings Freestanding HSPUs; 3x7 90° RTO Dips; + Legs |
| Heavy Pull | 3x3 Full Front Lever Pull-ups; 3x4 Pulley-assisted (10-lb assist) OAC; 3x3 German Hang Pullouts + 1-lb ankle weights; + Legs |

- How to make a "light" version of a "heavy" exercise when adding weight is impractical for skills like the planche (p.256-257):
  1. Use added resistance (weight belt, weighted vest, or makeshift item) where feasible.
  2. Use bands to ease the movement instead.
  3. Drop to the previous progression for the light day (e.g., straddle planche → advanced tuck planche) and use longer/higher-rep holds (e.g., 20-second holds) on that easier progression.
  4. If training toward planche on floor/parallettes, move to rings for the light day when dropping a progression — doubles as supplementary work.
- Concrete equipment-driven substitution rules given (p.257):
  - Rings HSPU / RTO dips: heavy→light = decrease sets, increase reps per set.
  - Planche pushups / front lever pull-ups: heavy→light = drop to the lower progression variant.
  - German hang pullouts / OAC work: heavy day = add weight and decrease reps per set.
- These same three levers (progression choice, set/rep inversion, added weight) apply to light/medium/heavy DUP splits generally (p.257). [CONVENTION]
- Upper-advanced expectation: with DUP, gains tend to appear roughly once per week, tapering to once every two weeks as you climb the advanced range; don't be discouraged if an entire cycle yields only a small strength improvement — programming complexity must increase to keep progressing here (p.257). [CONVENTION]
- Auto-regulation: at this level, training becomes "more instinctive." Use the RPE system (referenced from the overreaching/overtraining chapter) to decide whether to add or subtract exercises/sets mid-workout based on how the body feels that day — progress is no longer obvious workout-to-workout, so rely on written notes rather than memory (p.257-258). [CONVENTION]

**DUP/light-heavy taper protocol to a 1RM PR** (p.257):
Available "finally" at this level — a taper starting at 12/6 RM (light/heavy) and stepping down weekly:
1. Week 1: 12/6 RM
2. Week 2: 10/5 RM
3. Week 3: 8/4 RM
4. Week 4: 6/3 RM
5. Week 5: 4/1-2 RM (PR attempt)
- Sets held constant at three, or increased on heavier days if volume can be tolerated (p.257). [CONVENTION]

**3-week hybridized DUP push/pull example, 4x/week** (pp.257-259) — full table across Weeks 1-3:

| Day | Week 1 | Week 2 | Week 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heavy Push | 3x6 Straddle Planche Pushups; 4x6 Rings Freestanding HSPUs; 3x6 90° RTO Leaned-Forward Dips; +Legs | 3x4 Straddle Planche Pushups (+5 lb vest/ankle weights); 4x4 Rings Freestanding HSPUs (+5 lb); 3x4 90° RTO Leaned-Forward Dips (or progress to Dip Straight-Body Press to Handstand); +Legs | 5x3 Straddle Planche Pushups (+10 lb); 5x3 Rings Freestanding HSPUs (+10 lb); 3x3 Dip Straight-Body Press to Handstand; +Legs |
| Light Pull | 3x10 Straddle Front Lever Pull-ups; 3x10 Pulley-assisted (20-lb assist) OAC; 3x10 German Hang Pullouts; +Legs | 3x7 Full Front Lever Pull-ups; 3x7 Pulley-assisted (5-lb assist) OAC; 3x7 German Hang Pullouts; +Legs | 3x5 Full Front Lever Pull-ups (weighted vest); 3x5 Unassisted OAC; 3x5 German Hang Pullouts (ankle weights); +Legs |
| Light Push | 3x10 Rings Advanced Tuck Planche Pushups; 3x10 Rings Freestanding HSPUs; 3x10 90° RTO Dips; +Legs | 3x7 Rings Advanced Tuck Planche Pushups; 3x7 Rings Freestanding HSPUs; 3x7 90° RTO Dips; +Legs | 3x5 Straddle Planche Pushups (+5 lb); 3x5 Rings Freestanding HSPUs (+5 lb); 3x5 90° RTO Leaned-Forward Dips (or progress to Dip SBP-to-HS); +Legs |
| Heavy Pull | 3x6 Full Front Lever Pull-ups; 3x6 Pulley-assisted (5-lb assist) OAC; 3x6 German Hang Pullouts; +Legs | 3x4 Full Front Lever Pull-ups (weighted vest); 3x4 Unassisted OAC; 3x4 German Hang Pullouts (ankle weights); +Legs | 3x3 Full Front Lever Pull-ups (+10 lb weighted vest); 3x3 OAC (+5 lb); 3x3 German Hang Pullouts (ankle weights); +Legs |

- Mechanism note: in Week 2, the "light" workouts closely resemble Week 1's "heavy" workouts — light-day intensity should be dialed slightly harder/easier week to week depending on how well the athlete is progressing (p.258). The stated objective across the block is continued week-over-week progress, mirroring the Week 1→2 gain pattern into Week 2→3 (p.258-259). [CONVENTION]

## Advanced: Routine Progression (p.259)

**Modifying routines.** Because it's hard to know in advance whether a program will work at higher levels, experienced athletes should trust their own "feel" to adjust rep counts up or down mid-cycle. The one inviolable rule for a light/heavy split: keep light days light and heavy days heavy — "that is the most important part of a split light/heavy routine" (p.259). [CONVENTION]
- Warning: as stimulus and training volume both increase to match rising capacity, it becomes very easy to do too much rather than too little; scheduled deload weeks only partially address this — unaddressed fatigue can still accumulate across years of training even while an athlete keeps progressing (p.259-260). [CONVENTION]

**Overreaching and overtraining — extended deloads** (p.259-260):
- After years of consistent training, take an extended deload of a few weeks to a few months; it is not necessary to fully rest the entire time — engaging in other activities/relaxing is fine (p.259).
- Rationale: if training has been consistent for years, the overall decrease in gains upon return will be very small, while accumulated total fatigue dissipates, leaving the athlete fresh (p.259-260). [CONVENTION]
- Case example: an athlete who trained consistently ~4 years, reaching roughly Level 11-12 in most categories, took a 2-month extended break. His body continued changing during the break — hypertrophy increased as he "recovered" more — despite not having felt he needed the break beforehand (p.260). [CONVENTION/anecdote]
- Framing: skyrocketing ability can cause loss of perspective on other important qualities; rest can restore appreciation for training and often positions the athlete for success upon return (p.260).

**Utilizing periodization** (p.260):
- Many periodization/programming schemes tried in early/intermediate/early-advanced stages will be mediocre — this is expected and is how an athlete learns their own individual response. Only with more experience can programming be refined to be more effective (p.260). [CONVENTION]
- For coaches: foundational sport-specific knowledge is necessary but the mark of a great coach is tailoring the fundamentals to each athlete's unique response rather than running a cookie-cutter program — stated as the book's overarching philosophy (p.260).

**Goals** (p.260): at the advanced level, goals must become more specific — maintain previously achieved strength levels while focusing all push and/or all pull work toward one specific goal at a time, which drastically increases the rate of progress toward that goal. [CONVENTION]

**Consider outside factors / marginal gains** (p.260-261):
- Sleep quality, nutrition, and stress levels are cited as prerequisites — "you will not get very far without having these factors in order" (p.260-261).
- Marginal-gains example: cites David Brailsford/British Cycling — 2 gold medals (1908-2002) vs. 58 world-championship golds (2003-2013) and 8 Olympic golds (2008/2012), attributed to targeting 1% improvements across every aspect of training and life (p.261). [external example cited by the book, not book-original research]
- Numeric illustration: ten separate 0.01-second improvements sum to 0.10 seconds — in swimming, track, and cycling, that margin can be the difference between 1st and 4th place (p.261).

**Training log** (p.261): keep one, and record RPE values. At the advanced level "the window for effective volume, frequency, and intensity narrows," so a written log — not memory — is critical for detecting under- or over-training and for informing routine modification. [CONVENTION]

**Weak links** (p.261): advanced progressions make weak links much more pronounced. Check every few weeks (via the training log) for areas needing extra work. If progress stalls on a movement like a one-arm chin-up or iron cross, the cause may be (a) insufficient volume on the prime movers (e.g., back) or (b) a genuinely weak secondary link (e.g., biceps) — isolation work may be needed to bring up the weak link. [CONVENTION]

**Overuse modifications** (p.261-262):
- At this level, difficult bodyweight exercises place large strain on worked areas; higher-intensity exercise has a greater tendency to injure, especially when fatigued. If strain is felt in an area, do not go to failure.
- Repeats the standing modification options: eliminate/reduce volume/substitute the exercise; add prehabilitation, isolation, mobility, or flexibility work for the affected area (p.262). [CONVENTION]

**Avoiding injuries** (p.262): "the number one predictor of an injury is a previous injury." Always back off and modify the routine in response to how the body responds, especially re: potential overuse injury — "an injured athlete always progresses slower than one who stopped short of injury" (p.262). [CONVENTION]

**And beyond** (p.262): beyond Level 12, athlete goals diversify enough that the book does not cover special techniques for shortening workouts (exercise pairing, split-routine variants) in depth; the book directs further questions to the r/overcominggravity community.
