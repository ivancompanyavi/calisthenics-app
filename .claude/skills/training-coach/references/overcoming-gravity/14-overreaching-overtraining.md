# OG2 Ch.14 — Overreaching and Overtraining

> **Source:** *Overcoming Gravity* 2e, Ch.14, book pp.188-194. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/og2.txt` or PDF pp.188-194.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] established research · [CONVENTION] Low's system · [DISPUTED] contested.

## Definitions (p.188)
- **Overreaching** [CONVENTION]: a *temporary* state, reached over multiple workouts, where abilities drop below baseline. A normal rest period or a deload lets the body rebound/supercompensate so capabilities then *increase* — i.e. it is the planned depression half of the fatigue-and-fitness (stimulus→fatigue→supercompensation) model, not a pathology (p.188).
- **Overtraining** [CONVENTION]: a state reached through *many* workouts of continually depressed abilities, producing an overall *regression* of capability that persists even after deloading and resting. Symptoms: regression of capabilities, sleep disruption, decreased appetite, perpetual soreness, increased injury risk (p.188).
- Overreaching, left unmanaged over long periods, can devolve into overtraining (p.188).
- [CONVENTION] Overtraining is genuinely hard to reach, especially as a beginner — overreaching is far more common and is often desirable (p.188-189).

## Overreaching as a planned tool (p.189-191)
- Many athletes deliberately induce short overreaching periods to trigger supercompensation; in sport this is "peaking" or "tapering," usually timed for competition (p.189).
- Mechanism/why [SCIENCE→CONVENTION bridge]: as fitness rises, progress gets harder because the body adapts to the training stimuli (referencing the fatigue-and-fitness model from elsewhere in the book) — so intermediate/advanced lifters must plan in **multi-week mesocycles** rather than expect workout-to-workout gains (p.189).
- **Accumulation vs. intensification** [CONVENTION]: accumulation phase = carefully planned overload with enough volume to deliberately depress capability to a set "quota." Once hit, shift to intensification = lighter volume, higher intensity. Why: lowering volume lets the body supercompensate rapidly even while intensity stays high, which reveals the gains banked during accumulation (p.189).
- This is the core of **dual-factor theory**: combine several workouts (weekly or bi-weekly) to depress capability, then allow progression in the following weeks/period. Elite athletes may run such cycles over months or years (p.189).
- **Beginners** [CONVENTION]: can use a weekly planned-overreaching cycle, but it needs at least a couple of rest/light days per week so the body can recover from total weekly volume. Example within-week schedules: **4/3** (4 days on, 3 off) or **5/2** (5 on, 2 off), or similar (p.189-190).
  - Not fully recommended for beginners moving into intermediate — simpler progression methods (e.g. simple linear progression, light/heavy day structuring) are easier routes. Useful mainly for practical constraints (shift workers, needing weekends free) (p.190).
- **Intermediate/advanced mesocycle example** [CONVENTION]: a within-week decrease in capability is fine provided the week ends with sufficient rest for recovery/supercompensation/adjustment before continuing. An accumulation phase can run ~2 weeks, followed by an intensification week, forming a 3-week "cycle"; two such cycles make a 6-week mesocycle (p.190):

| Week | Phase |
|---|---|
| 1 | Accumulation |
| 2 | Accumulation |
| 3 | Intensification |
| 4 | Accumulation |
| 5 | Accumulation |
| 6 | Intensification |

- It's acceptable to see no progress mid-mesocycle — gains can be masked by fatigue; supercompensation may only reveal itself after the end-of-mesocycle rest/deload (p.190).
- Programming this takes experimentation because individual response varies; a **training log** is essential to see whether ability trended up or down across the mesocycle, and to detect delayed supercompensation after a deload week (p.190).
- **Overreaching vs. DUP** [CONVENTION]: they are distinct concepts — you do *not* need depressed capabilities to progress. Overreaching is one way of applying the fatigue-and-fitness model; DUP and other periodization models let even advanced athletes progress without ever depressing ability. Choice depends on the athlete's goals (p.190).
- Most high-level programs blend some overreaching-type periodization so athletes progress within sessions *and* can supercompensate/peak for competition — but this requires much more planning than the book covers, and requires knowing your own response curve. Logging is repeatedly emphasized as critical here (p.191).

## Overtraining (p.191-193)
- Overtraining = a prolonged state of **under-recovery** where the body cannot properly repair itself; recovery takes weeks to months; capabilities are heavily depressed, sometimes below the athlete's original starting level (p.191).
- Reached only via chronic overworking — specifically, not getting enough rest for **several consecutive months**. [CONVENTION] Deloading properly should prevent ever reaching it (p.191).
- **Beginners' real risk is not overtraining** — it's doing too much volume in a *single workout*. This volume isn't necessarily beyond what the body could eventually handle, but it can cause overuse injuries or blunt optimal progression. "More is not always better" (p.191).
- **Diagnostic heuristic** [CONVENTION]: if capabilities are increasing workout to workout, you are neither overreaching nor overtraining — you may simply be under- or over-dosing volume/intensity/frequency relative to what's optimal; experiment to find the right dose (p.191).
- If plateauing/regressing and you haven't deloaded recently: deload (timing depends on where you are in the mesocycle) and see if rest resolves it. If rest doesn't help, the more likely cause is **undertraining**, not overtraining — also check sleep, diet, and life stress, any one of which could be the actual driver. Take an extra rest day to diagnose. Missing a workout or two beats wasting time in a chronic plateau/regression (p.191).
- The two most obvious overtraining symptoms: **decreased appetite** and **decreased sleep quality**. If these appear after a recent volume/intensity increase, remove extra stressors so the body can adapt (p.191).
- Reiterated: overtraining is not something to worry about if you're progressing toward goals. If not progressing, evaluate training, sleep, diet, and stress; seek a more experienced person's advice for plateau troubleshooting (p.191).

### Two symptom-clusters of overtraining (Lyle McDonald / Body Recomposition) [SCIENCE, cited secondary source] (p.191-192)
Historically labeled parasympathetic-dominant ("addisonic") vs. sympathetic-dominant ("basedowic") overtraining — the old disease-derived names are no longer much used, but the symptom clusters remain a useful conceptual split of autonomic nervous system dysfunction. Causes are multifactorial: training load, caloric intake, vitamin/mineral sufficiency, sleep, stress, and more. Recognizing which cluster is present signals a need to cut training and increase recovery (p.192).

**Addisonic (parasympathetic-dominant) overtraining:**
- Increased diastolic blood pressure (over 100 mm/Hg) during and after physical exertion
- Impaired coordination
- Decreased endurance
- Low resting heart rate
- Persistent muscle aches
- Slight increase in recovery time
- Slight loss in motivation
- Decreased adrenal function / adrenal fatigue

**Basedowic (sympathetic-dominant) overtraining:**
- Slight increase in blood pressure
- Impaired coordination with increased reaction time
- Decreased endurance with tendency to fatigue easily
- Increased sleep requirements
- Elevated resting heart rate
- Increased appetite
- Increased metabolism and sweating
- Increased rate of breathing under stress
- Increased headaches and colds; decreased immune function
- Increased nervousness, unease, decreased motivation, eventual depression
- Increased sympathetic nervous system activity (fight-or-flight)

## Rate of Perceived Exertion (RPE) and training logs (p.192-193)
- RPE scale: 0-10, where 0 = rest, 10 = maximal exertion; rate each exercise's felt intensity on a given day. Other scales exist (Borg, Visual Analog, Likert) but RPE is simplest and most widely used (p.192).
- Why it matters: RPE quickly flags problems with a specific exercise/set. Example: 3x5 tuck planche push-ups at RPE 8 → room to push harder next session. Same volume at RPE 10 → jumping straight to 3x6 next session will likely fail; better to progress via **linear repetition addition**: 5-5-5 → 6-5-5 → 6-6-5 → 6-6-6 (p.192).
- RPE was deliberately excluded from the strength-progression chapter because its real use is *managing/avoiding* overreaching and overtraining, not driving progression itself (p.192).
- Diagnostic example [CONVENTION]: one-arm chin-ups at 5-5-5 with RPE 10, then next session only 5-5-4 / 5-4-3 with RPE still pinned at 10 → strength is *decreasing* while perceived effort stays maxed — a serious warning sign of overreaching/overtraining. The fix is a **deload, not more volume** (p.192).
- Training logs let you review past workouts to see when/how you progressed, plan future progressions, and notice good/bad patterns (p.192).
- For coaches: individual logs per athlete are important because what works for one athlete may impair another's progress; logs are critical to remembering weekly specifics across multiple athletes (p.192).
- A training log lets an athlete request help meaningfully — a coach needs the routine, sets/reps, tempo, rest times, overall volume, frequency, intensity, nutrition, sleep, stress levels, and any injuries to make an educated diagnosis of a stalled routine. Without this detail, troubleshooting is "throwing dirt against a wall"; with it, it's "a laser-guided missile" (p.193).
- Athletes who log become more invested, ask better questions, and give better feedback; the practice also opens a pathway to broader mentorship beyond the sport itself (p.193).

## Chapter summary (p.193, restated)
- Beginners: overreaching shows up as a visible workout-to-workout stall; remedy is to deload until constant progression resumes.
- Intermediate/advanced (progress measured weekly, not per-workout): planned overreaching can and should be used to apply adequate stress to force strength/mass adaptation.
- Overtraining is not a concern unless abilities are *constantly* regressing — if so, take significant time off; know the signs/symptoms. Recovery keys: sleep, nutrition, stress reduction. Seek a medical professional if needed.
- Best practice: track RPE alongside reps/sets/volume/intensity/frequency in a training log, even though it's subjective.
