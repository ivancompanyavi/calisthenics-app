# OG2 Ch.3 — Progression Charts and Goal Setting

> **Source:** *Overcoming Gravity* 2e, Ch.3, book pp.21-34. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/og2.txt` or PDF pp.21-34.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] established research · [CONVENTION] Low's system · [DISPUTED] contested.

## Progression charts and how to use them (p.21-22)

[CONVENTION] The book provides "skill and strength progression charts" to solve a specific gap: bodyweight training info is scattered online with "virtually no way to ascertain levels of skill and strength progression" (p.21). Framed explicitly as an RPG skill-tree analogy — train to earn "skill points," spend them to unlock new progressions; skills overlap (handstand/handstand-pushup training carries over to other pressing movements like pushups/dips) (p.21).

Chart basis: many elements are calibrated against the Federation Internationale de Gymnastique (FIG) Code of Points (COP) difficulty scale, A (easiest) to G (hardest), which FIG uses to standardize difficulty of swinging/strength/dismount elements across apparatus (p.21). The book borrows this difficulty ranking for bodyweight strength/skill progressions but does not cover swinging elements or dismounts (p.21).

Chart structure: four skill categories — **Basic Skills, A-level Skills, B-level Skills, C-level Skills** — each with four subcategories of increasing difficulty (p.22). (figure — see PDF pp.30-33, progression charts themselves are non-extractable tables/diagrams.)

[CONVENTION] Practical use: copy the charts (rather than flipping back to the book's exercise section repeatedly); mark goals and cross off completed exercises; charts also available on the Eat.Move.Improve website (p.22). Abbreviation list is in Chapter 23 (p.22).

## The level system (p.22-23)

[CONVENTION] 16 levels total, grouped into 4 quartiles of 4 levels each, mapped to the four skill categories (Basic/A/B/C) (p.22):

| Population | Levels |
|---|---|
| Beginner | 1-5 |
| Intermediate | 6-9 |
| Advanced | 10-13 |
| Elite | 14-16 |

[DISPUTED/caveat] Some movements classed "advanced" here (iron cross, full planche, other isometrics) are considered only *intermediate* in competitive gymnastics — the underlying strength-attainment concepts are the same, but gymnasts have far higher skill-work volume and typically 10-15+ years of training, so gymnastics coaches push athletes past these charts' ceiling (p.23).

## Classification of differences in the level system (p.23-25)

[CONVENTION] Beginner/intermediate/advanced/elite is **not** defined by time trained — "length of time spent training plays almost no consideration" because people can train for years while stagnating (p.23). It is defined by **ability level** (can you do a solid handstand? a muscle-up/back lever with good technique? squat 2x bodyweight?) because ability is a measurable proxy for CNS proficiency at exerting force under control, and correlates with the physiological adaptations needed to handle a given training load (p.23-24).

[SCIENCE-adjacent, stated as fact by book] Rate of progress is inversely related to strength level: "the body will progress much faster at lower levels than it will if you already have higher levels of strength" (p.24) — this is why programming complexity must scale up with level.

[CONVENTION] Programming complexity must increase with level because higher strength requires progressively greater stress to drive the same adaptation; this is achieved by manipulating intensity, volume, reps, and frequency as level rises (p.24). Individual factors — modifiable (stress, sleep, nutrition) and unmodifiable (genetics, limb length) — mean two athletes at the same nominal level may still need individually tailored programs (p.24).

[CONVENTION] Transition between levels doesn't automatically require program changes, but **plateauing is the trigger** to increase programming complexity (p.24-25).

Volume/frequency contrast across the spectrum, given as illustration of the beginner→elite range (p.25):
- Beginner programs: full-body, ~3x/week.
- Olympic weightlifter: gym 3-5x in a *single day*, ≤1 rest day/week.
- Olympic-track gymnast: up to 40 hrs/week of movement practice, *separate from* strength training.
[CONVENTION] Throwing a beginner into elite-athlete volume risks injury within weeks (p.25).

### Action steps for untrained beginners (p.25)
- Introduce fundamental exercises and build proficiency.
- Use higher reps: [CONVENTION] rationale given is to solidify movement patterns and build connective-tissue strength (not muscular strength per se) (p.25).
- Focus on individual weaknesses — e.g., desk-job posture/alignment deficits, sedentary-lifestyle mobility/flexibility deficits — because these raise injury risk if unaddressed (p.25).
- Start generalized/balanced, high-rep, and only later transition to traditional strength training.

### Action steps for trained beginners (p.25)
- Consistency/discipline over any other variable — "the best program is the one you stick to"; only skip sessions for overuse injury or emergency (p.25).
- Emphasize the **5-15 rep range**: [CONVENTION] stated rationale is "to ensure good muscular development and strength" (p.25) — no deeper mechanism given in this chapter.
- Keep push/pull balanced; add corrective volume (typically more horizontal pulling) if imbalance emerges — book flags this as a *recurring* pattern for those who did mostly pushing before starting bodyweight training; conversely pull-dominant sports (e.g., climbing) may need added pushing work (p.25).
- Allow connective tissue, bone, and joint structures time to adapt to the new loading (p.25).

### Action steps for intermediate (p.25-26)
- Training must diversify and specialize by goal — full-body routines lose effectiveness as needs narrow (skill work, sport-specific skill, flexibility, mobility, pre/rehab) (p.25).
- Decide your primary training direction (strength / endurance / hypertrophy) and tailor accordingly:
  - **Strength** — [CONVENTION] increase frequency "as much as possible without overtraining or developing overuse injuries" (p.25) — no numeric prescription given, framed as a ceiling-seeking heuristic.
  - **Endurance** — work strength at *lower* volume to preserve exercise efficiency, and train endurance-specific work at high reps separately (p.25).
  - **Hypertrophy** — split away from full-body routines; detailed in Ch.5 (p.25).
- Must adapt frequency/volume/intensity to sustain progress at this level, and adopt more complex programming techniques (p.25).

### Action steps for advanced (p.25-26)
- Training becomes maximally specific to sport/primary goal (p.25).
- Shoring up weak links becomes far more important for continued progress. [CONVENTION] Example given: one-arm-pull-up training tends to leave the back much stronger than the arms (because gymnastics-style training emphasizes straight-arm work), so targeted bicep isolation work may be needed to fix the weak link and improve overall strength; the deadlift is cited as a barbell-lift analogue where posterior-chain dominance can leave a weak link in back or quads needing isolation work (p.25-26).
- Dialing in sleep/nutrition/stress-reduction — always useful, but at this level "even small improvements will add up" once progress becomes difficult to earn (p.26).
- Keep a training log at this stage specifically to observe how the individual responds to rest, deloads, and given intensity/volume — this personalized data simplifies future weekly-routine planning (p.26).

[CONVENTION] Overall framing: know what matters vs. what's minutiae — especially relevant for coaches managing multiple athletes; the goal is always maximizing improvement while staying injury-free and on track toward goals (p.26).

## Setting and achieving goals (p.25-26)

[SCIENCE-framed-as-fact] The rep continuum has strength at one end and endurance at the other; the book states "you cannot optimally train strength and endurance at the same time" (p.25-26) — the whole book assumes a strength-training goal.

[CONVENTION] Endurance training = maximize reps or density/volume of work in a fixed time. Training both strength and endurance simultaneously is not recommended in general, but is sometimes necessary for sport/competition demands, in which case set separate strength goals and endurance goals rather than blending them (p.26). Strength goals ⇒ high load/difficulty, low reps; endurance goals ⇒ low load, high reps producing a "burning" sensation (p.26).

## Selecting goals (p.26-27)

[CONVENTION] High-quality goals are tangible and numerically measurable. Book's own examples of high-quality goals (p.26):
1. Perform 10 dips on parallel bars with good form.
2. Run 400 meters in 60 seconds.
3. Reduce body fat to 15%.
4. Put on 10 pounds of muscle.
5. Lose 10 pounds of fat.

Contrasted low-quality goals (ill-defined, not numeric) (p.26):
1. Improve on dips.
2. Run without getting winded.
3. Lose weight.
4. Gain muscle mass.
5. Get fit.

[CONVENTION] Routines are built around the high-quality version — e.g., a 10-dips goal implies a literal ladder: get 1 dip, then 2, then 3, etc. (p.26).

**SMART model** (p.26-27):
- Specific
- Measurable
- Action-Oriented
- Realistic
- Time-Bound

[CONVENTION] Goals must align with the athlete's *overarching* objective, illustrated with two worked (generic, hypothetical) examples in the source text: a goal framed as endurance (many pushups in a set) does not serve an underlying strength objective, and a goal framed as an unrelated pulling feat does not serve an underlying skill objective like the handstand — the fix in both cases is to decompose the overarching objective into SMART sub-goals that are mechanistically related to it (e.g., decomposing a handstand goal into a timed wall-hold goal and a separate freestanding-hold time goal) (p.27).

[SCIENCE-framed] High-rep bodyweight work (e.g., many reps of an easy movement) demonstrates endurance, not strength — the book is explicit that endurance gains are easier to obtain once already strong/powerful, i.e. strength should generally precede endurance-focused work if both are goals (p.27).

[SCIENCE-framed] "A lack of strength will always limit you in other domains — technique, endurance, skill, balance, flexibility (active and passive), agility, coordination" — strength is claimed as a prerequisite/enabler for the other domains, and the book states the converse relationship does not typically hold (p.27).

[CONVENTION] Caution against unrealistic goals: a single pull-up is realistic for most people with training; ten strict planche pushups is not (planche sits in the advanced-strength range), by analogy to wanting ten reps of a 500 lb bench press. Achieving basic competency in any advanced/elite movement plausibly takes years of work. Rationale: unrealistic goals that are never achieved become a net psychological detractor from training, whereas achievable, achieved goals build momentum (p.27).

## Goals and exercise selection (p.27-28)

[CONVENTION] Cap simultaneous goals at **one to two goals per category**, across six categories: skill, pushing, pulling, legs, core, flexibility (p.27). Rationale: more than that cannot be trained optimally in parallel.

Category scope as defined in the chapter (p.27-28):
- Skill: primarily handstands (aside from sport-specific technique), the only skill goal category the book covers in depth.
- Pushing: pushup variations, dip variations, handstand pushup variations, planche.
- Pulling: back lever, front lever, iron cross, pull-up variations, row variations.
- Legs, core, flexibility: treated as self-explanatory.

[CONVENTION] Ordering/sequencing constraint for connective-tissue safety: work back lever before front lever, and work both levers before attempting one-arm chin-ups or the iron cross (p.28). This ordering exists specifically to build connective-tissue tolerance progressively rather than jumping straight to a very difficult isometric and risking injury; the book explicitly notes athletes "may decide to bypass these recommendations at your own risk" (p.28).

[CONVENTION] Exercise-selection algorithm once goals are chosen (p.28):
1. Find your goal movement on the progression chart.
2. Select the highest rung of that specific progression you can currently perform, and put it in your routine.
3. If you cannot perform *any* rung of the target progression, substitute a **similar exercise in the same movement-plane category** (e.g., planche pushup goal but can't do even the tuck-planche-progression pushup → fall back to the standard pushup progression, since both are horizontal pushing and strength carries over) until strong enough to switch onto the target progression.
4. General category-substitution rule: for pushing, choose vertical- or horizontal-pushing as the stand-in category; for pulling, vertical- or horizontal-pulling — matching movement plane is what preserves carryover.

[CONVENTION] Deliberately anti-overthinking: "Overthinking it only leads to more confusion" (p.28) — goal-directed chart lookup plus plane-matched substitution is sufficient; goals/exercises chosen here get consolidated into an actual routine in subsequent chapters (p.28).

## Commitment to achievement (p.28-29)

[SCIENCE] Writing goals down is "well-proven through psychological research" to increase follow-through, and is a technique long used by sales teams (p.28).

[CONVENTION, cited anecdote] Harvard Business School Class of 1963 story (via contributor Artie Buerk): of a graduating class, 3% had written goals, 13% had unwritten goals, 84% had no goals; at the 10-year reunion the 3%-written-goals group had achieved roughly 10x the outcomes of the rest, and the 13%-unwritten-goals group had achieved roughly 2x the outcomes of the 84%-no-goals group (p.28-29).

[CONVENTION] Practical prescriptions: keep goals in a training journal (physical or digital); place it somewhere seen regularly (by the fridge, near the computer, on the desktop) so it prompts action and prevents missed sessions; checking items off a physical list functions as positive reinforcement that builds training drive; "success is built through discipline, and discipline is built through tangible work, and tangible work starts with writing down your goals on paper" (p.29).

## "Stop reading. Take action." — practical protocol (p.29-30)

Ordered self-application steps given verbatim in structure (p.29-30):
1. Grab four or five sheets of blank paper.
2. Write down all goals you want to accomplish in bodyweight training (consult the charts/exercise section if unsure what's achievable/available).
3. Refine the goals into SMART goals.
4. Print the progression charts (middle of the chapter) — two copies recommended.
5. Locate each goal on the chart; note progressions grouped near it (chart-adjacent skills have carryover and can serve as training supplements). Highlight target level in one color and current ability in another color across all charts.
6. This baseline (current vs. target) is the gap the next chapters' exercise selection will bridge.

## Chapter 3 Summary (p.33) — Knowledge Base

[CONVENTION] Restated benefits of the progression charts (p.33):
- Show relationships between balanced levels of skill work and strength.
- Give progressions/prerequisites toward goals.
- Illustrate breadth of skill/strength training beyond the common headline goals (back lever, front lever, planche, iron cross).

[CONVENTION] Programming needs differ by population (beginner/intermediate/advanced/elite); this distinction matters both for initial program construction and for diagnosing a plateau (p.33).

[CONVENTION] Goals should follow a progression, be both quantitative and qualitative, and ultimately serve the overarching aim of strength development. Commit to achieving them; write them down and keep them visible; maintain a workout log to track progress and learn what does/doesn't work for the individual (p.33).

SMART recap (p.33): Specific, Measurable, Action-Oriented, Realistic, Time-Bound.
