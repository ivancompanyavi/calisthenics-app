# OG2 Ch.13 — Endurance, Cardio, Cross Training, Hybrid Templates, and Routines

> **Source:** *Overcoming Gravity* 2e, Ch.13, book pp.171-187. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/og2.txt` or PDF pp.171-187.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] established research · [CONVENTION] Low's system · [DISPUTED] contested.

## Terminology and Energy Systems (p.171)

- **Endurance** is a catch-all term for high repetitions of any activity; distinct in principle from metabolic conditioning and circuit training but the terms are often used interchangeably (p.171).
- **Cardiovascular endurance activities** = cycling, long-distance running/swimming, and other sustained 10-20+ minute efforts (p.171).
- **Metabolic conditioning** trains the body's energy-producing metabolic systems via varied activities performed in a sustained manner, usually lasting single-digit minutes (sometimes longer) (p.171).
- **Circuit training** typically moves between weight-lifting exercises; can include disparate activities but practitioners usually stick to weights (p.171).
- [SCIENCE] Three energy pathways by duration: phosphocreatine/creatine phosphate dominates 0-10s efforts; the glycolytic pathway dominates 10-75s; oxidative phosphorylation dominates 75s+ efforts. Phosphocreatine and glycolytic are termed **anaerobic**; oxidative is **aerobic** (p.171, citing Gastin's review table on energy system interaction/relative contribution during maximal exercise — figure, see PDF p.171-172).
- [SCIENCE] Elite track data confirm the duration-dependent anaerobic/aerobic split: 400m (world record ~43s) is ~60/40 anaerobic:aerobic; 800m (~100s) is ~40/60; 1600m (~223s) is ~20/80 (p.172).
- [CONVENTION] Implication: to train cardiovascular endurance *capacity* specifically, sessions need to run well past four minutes because that's the timescale needed to work the oxidative system properly; research/programs recommend sub-anaerobic-threshold running for 20 minutes to an hour or more (p.172).
- [CONVENTION] To train the anaerobic system maximally, work in the 30-75s range — long enough to minimize both phosphocreatine and aerobic contribution. This is also roughly when metabolic acidosis ("the burn") is felt, which the book notes coincides with the research (p.172).
- CrossFit-style short workouts (2-5 min) are primarily fueled by the aerobic system even though they feel anaerobic; CrossFit programming has to be efficient across pathways because of this overlap (p.172).
- [SCIENCE] Endurance and metabolic conditioning share a biological basis: both require high neural adaptation to technique plus high energy-pathway efficiency. Metabolic conditioning leans slightly less on CNS adaptation and more on energy-pathway efficiency (more exercises to cycle through), making it comparatively lighter on the CNS (p.172).
- Both endurance and metabolic conditioning increase **overall work capacity** — meaning muscles/CNS can handle more total work in a session, which lets you push hypertrophy stimulus further without overtraining, even when adding strength work on top (p.172).
- [CONVENTION] Going to failure is acceptable/encouraged for most/all endurance and (especially) metabolic-conditioning sets, because the high energy-system demand of these modalities stresses energy systems relatively more than the nervous system — so it won't burn out the CNS while still driving adaptation (p.172-173). Pushing through the burn raises the lactic-acid (glycolytic) threshold.
- [CONVENTION] Alternative: stopping short of failure in metabolic conditioning increases work capacity for the *next* bout within the same session — more work in less time, higher total power output. This makes not-to-failure metabolic conditioning behave more like a strength stimulus (total-body-distributed intensity) than a pure endurance stimulus (p.173).

## To-Failure Training (p.173)

- [CONVENTION] Failure is possible but not encouraged on the first set, because failing early reduces the achievable reps on subsequent sets and lowers total session volume (p.173).
- Worked example: max 20 dips, goal 50 dips. Options: 3×18 (54 total) vs. 5×15 (75 total, last set to failure). 5×15 wins because **total reps performed is the primary lever for endurance gain**, so pick the rep target that maximizes total volume across sets, even though it means fewer reps per set than a near-max attempt (p.173).
- [SCIENCE] Individual variation: athletes with more slow-twitch fiber proportion (noted as generally true of women vs. men) can hold closer to their endurance-RM for more sets — program according to individual response, not a fixed template (p.173).

## Grease the Groove (p.173-174)

- [CONVENTION] **Strength version**: 6-10 submaximal sets spread through the day, each at ~60-80% of max reps, performed every 1-2 hours. Rationale: staying submaximal avoids overworking the body while giving high total practice volume/frequency on the movement pattern — driving rapid neural (motor learning) adaptation without accumulating the fatigue of near-max daily training (p.173).
  - Worked example: 4-rep max dips → 2-3 reps/set, 6-10x/day → up to 30 reps/day, vs. only ~4-5 sets of 3 (12-15 reps) if confined to one workout session (p.173).
- [CONVENTION] **Endurance modification**: use a high-rep exercise; work at 60-80% of max reps across the day (higher intensity than strength GTG allowed — 75-90% of max — because "endurance work is less intense than strength work" so the athlete can tolerate higher relative intensity per set) (p.174).
  - Worked example: 20-rep max pushups → 60-80% = sets of 12-16 reps, 6-10x/day; can push to 75-90% for sets of 15-18 reps.
  - Going to-failure or near-failure is stated as *needed* to maximize endurance gains in this context (p.174) — differs from the strength version, which stays deliberately submaximal.

## Ladders and Pyramids (p.174)

- **Ladder protocol**: start a timer (commonly 1 min, arbitrary); do 1 rep, rest until the interval elapses; do 2 reps, rest; continue climbing (by 1s, or by any increment, e.g. 5-10-15) until you cannot complete the rung (p.174).
- **Pyramid protocol**: climb the ladder to failure, then climb back down symmetrically (e.g., up to 7 pull-ups fails on the next rung, so descend 6-5-4-3-2-1) (p.174).
- [CONVENTION] Rationale for both: they drastically increase total volume within a single session relative to straight-set schemes. Worked comparison: 20-rep-max dips done as 5×15 = 75 total reps, vs. a pyramid working up to a 12-rep max (1+2+...+12+11+...+1 = 144 total reps) — nearly double the volume — because **volume is the primary driver of endurance adaptation**, much like it is for hypertrophy (p.174).

## Metabolic Conditioning and Circuits (p.174-175)

- [CONVENTION] Construction heuristic: choose 2-5 exercises normally performed for 10+ reps; arrange in any order; set a rep target per exercise at ~25-75% of max ability; choose 3-5 rounds; perform as fast as possible (or use AMRAP — as many rounds as possible in a fixed time) (p.174-175).
- [CONVENTION] Programming metabolic conditioning is described as "an art" requiring a specific goal going in — specifically whether the intent is to train oxidative or glycolytic capacity (aerobic vs. anaerobic pathway), and calibration to the individual's ability level. Ending a workout so fatigued that only single reps can be ground out is called an ineffective (non-optimal) target (p.175).
- [DISPUTED] The "any asshole" criticism (that anyone can throw together random exercises into a workout) is addressed directly: while such workouts can improve capacity somewhat, they are not optimal and can overshoot into overtraining — "more is not always better." Effective metabolic-conditioning programming should show a measurable, trackable increase in capacity from workout to workout (p.175).

## High-Intensity Interval Training (HIIT) (p.175)

- **HIIT** and similar techniques (e.g. fartlek) are described as good for depleting all energy systems quickly; typical structure is all-out effort 15-30s followed by rest (jog/walk) 30-45s. Builds aerobic and anaerobic pathways rapidly — good for metabolic conditioning and cardiovascular health (p.175).
- [CONVENTION] Training anaerobic vs. aerobic capacity is a matter of intensity + rest, not just work duration: anaerobic qualities (like strength) are best trained at maximal effort with **long rest** (e.g., 10s sprint, 2-3 min rest) so the muscle is fully phosphagen-replenished and each effort is closer to true max — same rationale as strength rest periods (p.175).
- **Tabata protocol**: 8 rounds of 20s all-out effort / 10s rest, originated by cycling research; framed as similar to metabolic conditioning/HIIT but can be applied to non-running exercises; used to rapidly increase anaerobic capacity (p.175).
- [CONVENTION] Tabata can be modified: e.g. 30s intervals (20s on/10s off) or 60s intervals (45s on/15s off), for 5+ rounds — variations of a metabolic-conditioning workout hitting all energy systems rapidly (p.175).

## Cardio — Generalized Aerobic Capacity (p.175-177)

- [DISPUTED] Low-intensity steady state (LISS) training is "demonized by many" but the book asserts it remains the most effective way to increase aerobic capacity, citing that every intermediate/long-distance runner uses it (p.175).
- [CONVENTION] LISS is performed at or slightly below (5-10% below) anaerobic threshold — the "can carry on a conversation" heuristic — for 30-60+ minutes, depending on sport/goal. Rationale: anaerobic pathways are spared while maximal aerobic-system stress is applied, isolating the intended adaptation (p.175-176).
- The primary reason to include LISS in a strength-focused routine is that it **improves recovery** for strength workouts when performed at lower intensities (p.176).
- Definitions used to decide whether/how to add extra cardio:
  - **Passive Rest** — no exercise/activity on a rest day; usually the day after a workout.
  - **Active Rest** — moving without heavy exertion, staying below anaerobic threshold (e.g. recreational sports).
  - **Active Recovery** — a mindful, much-lower-intensity session (e.g. handstand technique work or sport-specific technique at low intensity) (p.176).
- [CONVENTION] Common failure mode in young/competitive athletes: active rest/active recovery drifts into a full workout. If an athlete cannot keep intensity genuinely low, the book says they should do neither and stick to passive rest, because "your body requires rest!" — rest is what allows adaptation before the next intense session (p.176).
- If an athlete *can* maintain low intensity, light work benefits training, especially as strength/work-capacity increase. Cited examples: Chinese gymnastics team and top powerlifters use low-intensity cardio; fighters/multi-round athletes improve their aerobic "wind" via LISS (p.176).
- [SCIENCE] Physiological rationale for light activity generally: increases parasympathetic nervous system stimulation (aids digestion/nutrient shuttling for repair), increases cerebral blood flow (mood, neural recovery), can improve posture, lowers resting heart rate, and increases work capacity during strength workouts via more efficient energy recovery (p.176).
- [CONVENTION] Cardio's training value is level-dependent: beginners focused on strength/hypertrophy do not need to train cardio at all and can progress without it — rest days should be passive unless cardio is specifically desired for its extra benefits. As athletes move into intermediate/advanced, some low-level activity on rest days becomes useful for increasing work capacity and speeding recovery, and grows more important for recovery as strength increases (p.176).
- Circus-arts athletes model built-in active rest via daily non-strength technique training (3-4+ hours), enabling 5-7 training days/week at 6-8 hours/day with breaks — offered as an existence proof that heavy volume is sustainable when paired with low-intensity activity for recovery (p.176).
- Elite-level pattern: activity generally benefits recovery/strength as long as it stays low-intensity outside dedicated workouts. Elite *endurance* athletes show the inverse — training LISS 60-80% of workouts but still building strength/power/anaerobic capacity (especially near competition), because strength/power/anaerobic capacity can decide races in a final sprint (p.176-177).
- [CONVENTION] **Pareto/80-20 rule for balancing power, strength, and endurance**: strength/power athletes should devote ~80% of training to strength/power and ~20% to walking/LISS cardio for recovery; endurance athletes should invert this ratio (p.177).
- [CONVENTION] Overriding heuristic: "listen to your body" — the 80/20 split is a starting point, not a fixed rule. If fatigued, cut strength/power work and increase recovery; if cardio addition is hurting training, scale it back toward strength/power. Individual variance means the ratio must be adjusted to the athlete's actual response (p.177).
- [SCIENCE] Passive rest taken to the extreme (desk-job-level inactivity) correlates with higher mortality even among people who otherwise exercise regularly — a walk or light jog is stated as superior to prolonged sitting from a health standpoint (p.177).

## Cross Training (p.177-181)

- [CONVENTION] Core caution: combining a bodyweight program with another sport's full strength-and-conditioning program is not advised for non-beginners — splitting time across systems causes gains to lag in both and raises overtraining risk. Athletes with serious aspirations in another sport should evaluate with their coach whether bodyweight training will help or hurt that sport specifically (p.177).
- Coach dynamics matter: some coaches are inflexible ("my way or the highway") and should be deferred to if the sport is a priority; others are flexible and may incorporate bodyweight work given good information; some don't care, leaving discretion to the athlete (p.177-178).
- Recommended practice when integrating with a coach: keep them informed, consider a trial period for substituting bodyweight work, continue if results are good, and be ready to bring supporting research if the coach's methods seem outdated (p.178).
- [CONVENTION] Priority rule: sport-specific training should be done fresh, not after energy is depleted by lesser priorities; if bodyweight training (e.g., handstands) interferes with sport-specific work, it should be the one eliminated, not vice versa (p.178).
- [CONVENTION] Volume rule: training/practicing the primary sport 5+ times/week generally means it isn't prudent to add extensive bodyweight strength-and-conditioning — though skill-based work (e.g. handstand practice) may still fit. If the sport has no dedicated strength-and-conditioning component, bodyweight training may usefully fill that gap, but the sport coach should be consulted first (p.178).
- "More is not always better" — default to erring on the side of eliminating extracurricular workouts if unsure, and integrate bodyweight training only when time and recovery allow. Judge by actual sport performance, not by feel alone (p.178).
- Gymnastics/parkour/climbing framing: each discipline has three components — psychological (fear/pressure management), skill-based, and physical preparation (strength and conditioning to increase ability toward the discipline). Bodyweight strength training is classified as physical preparation, consistent with how organized athletics train (gymnasts, track athletes, swimmers all supplement skill practice with dedicated S&C) (p.178-179).
- [DISPUTED] "Fallacy of the recreational athlete": believing structured S&C is unnecessary and only practicing the favorite activity. The book asserts physical preparation should always be separate but integrated (p.179).
- [SCIENCE/DISPUTED] Second fallacy addressed: that strength/conditioning work should mimic sport movements. The book states studies disprove this — e.g., throwing heavier balls doesn't improve throwing speed for baseball, swinging a heavier bat doesn't improve bat speed/power, weighted single-leg half squats don't necessarily improve sprint speed. Conclusion: apply general strength-and-conditioning principles / address weak links, rather than mimicking sport-specific movement, because overall strength transfers to the sport-specific skill (p.179).
- [CONVENTION] **80/20 rule for beginner/intermediate cross-training athletes** (distinct from the elite 80/20 above): 80% of training time on sport-specific skill work, 20% on strength and conditioning/mobility/injury-proofing. Combined with the earlier elite ratio this yields a nested breakdown: 80% sport-specific skill, 16% (=80% of the 20%) strength/conditioning for the sport's primary attributes, 4% (=20% of the 20%) cross-training attributes (p.179-180).
- Sample weekly split for an emerging discipline like parkour (citing Rafe Kelley/Parkour Visions and Ryan Ford/Apex Movement's recommended 3-4 days skill : 2 days S&C):

  | Day | Activity |
  |---|---|
  | Mon | Parkour Skill Work |
  | Tue | Strength and Conditioning |
  | Wed | Rest Day |
  | Thu | Parkour Skill Work |
  | Fri | Strength and Conditioning |
  | Sat | Parkour Skill Work |
  | Sun | Rest Day |

  Rationale: sessions run 2-4 hrs (skill) vs. 1-2 hrs (S&C), so total hours land near the 80:20 ratio even though session *counts* look closer to even (p.179-180).

- Alternative structure biasing skill work to weekends, for beginner/intermediate athletes of any sport (preferred default: full-body routine 2-3x/week with two full rest days):

  | Day | Activity |
  |---|---|
  | Mon | Full-Body Strength Work |
  | Tue | Rest Day |
  | Wed | Parkour Skill Work |
  | Thu | Full-Body Strength Work |
  | Fri | Rest Day |
  | Sat | Parkour Skill Work |
  | Sun | Parkour Skill Work |

  (p.180)

- Gymnastics/climbing pattern: S&C tacked onto the end of skill practice (gymnastics practices run 4-5 hrs, 4-5x/week: 2-3 hrs skill, remainder flexibility/mobility plus S&C at the end). Climbing: 3-4 gym sessions/week with ~1 hr S&C after each. Both land near the same 4:1 sport:S&C ratio, assuming 2-4 hr skill sessions (p.180):

  | Day | Activity |
  |---|---|
  | Mon | Climbing + Strength and Conditioning |
  | Tue | Rest Day |
  | Wed | Climbing + Optional Strength and Conditioning |
  | Thu | Rest Day |
  | Fri | Climbing + Strength and Conditioning |
  | Sat | Flex Day (climbing, recovery, or rest) |
  | Sun | Rest Day |

- [CONVENTION] Split-structure exception for climbing: while most sports pair well with an upper/lower split (bias leg-intensive sport work to one day, lower-body S&C to another), climbing is the opposite case — example weekly template (p.180-181):

  | Day | Activity |
  |---|---|
  | Mon | Climbing + Lower-Body Strength and Conditioning |
  | Tue | Upper-Body Strength and Conditioning |
  | Wed | Rest Day |
  | Thu | Climbing |
  | Fri | Upper-Body Strength and Conditioning |
  | Sat | Climbing + Lower-Body Strength and Conditioning |
  | Sun | Rest Day |

- Prehab/mobility/flexibility/isolation work can be slotted into rest days, training days, warm-ups, or post-workout — flexible placement. Example (climbers doing yoga post-session, S&C and mobility on off days) (p.181):

  | Day | Activity |
  |---|---|
  | Mon | Climbing + Yoga |
  | Tue | Strength and Conditioning |
  | Wed | Climbing + Mobility Work |
  | Thu | Rest Day |
  | Fri | Climbing + Yoga |
  | Sat | Strength and Conditioning |
  | Sun | Rest Day |

- [CONVENTION] Weak-point-driven customization principle: identify specific weaknesses and adjust the routine to them rather than following a fixed template — e.g. add flexibility/yoga for a specific technical weakness (heel hooks in climbing), add pull-up/dip/muscle-up strength work for climb-up weaknesses in parkour, or add 2-3 hangboard/campus-board/system-board sessions for hand-strength weaknesses in climbing (p.181).
- [CONVENTION] Strength as the foundation of athletic development: developing strength first is claimed to optimize development of many other attributes — cardiovascular endurance, stamina, flexibility, power, speed, agility, coordination, balance, accuracy — making it the top priority attribute for beginner/intermediate athletes across sports, including endurance sports (p.181).
- Supporting example: elite marathoners running mile splits of ~4.5 min for 26 consecutive miles requires substantial strength endurance; mile/1500m world-record holder Hicham El Guerrouj trained strength/power 3-4x/week, at up to nine reps of his target pace stretch depending on training phase (p.181-182).
- Weight training and bodyweight strength training are both presented as valid means of physical preparation, with bodyweight training highlighted as "an excellent alternative," especially in certain sports/disciplines (p.182).

## Hybrid Templates (p.181-185)

- Hybrid templates mix weights and bodyweight training; the book frames both fanatical positions — barbell-only critics dismissing bodyweight as too easy/high-rep, and bodyweight-only advocates dismissing weights — as short-sighted, since the two complement each other well and many high-level athletes across sports use both (p.181-182).
- Three integration methods addressed: **substitution, complementing, supplementing** (p.182).

### Substitution (p.182)
- [CONVENTION] Standard full-body exercise-selection template: 2 upper-body push, 2 upper-body pull, 1-2 leg exercises, keeping the body balanced for strength/mass progression. The two upper exercises per plane are further split into one horizontal-, one vertical-movement exercise to maintain structural balance (p.182).
- Example barbell routine → bodyweight substitution mapping (p.182):

  | Category | Barbell | Bodyweight equivalent |
  |---|---|---|
  | Upper Push | Bench Press + Press or Dips | Pushup/Planche Progression + Dips or Handstand Pushup Variation |
  | Upper Pull | Pull-ups + Bent-Over Rows | Pull-ups + Inverted Rows or Front Lever Pull-up |
  | Legs | Squat + Deadlift | Squats or Pistols + Glute Ham Raise/Hamstring Curl |

- [CONVENTION] For strength+hypertrophy goals, these substituted exercises should be performed in the 5-12 rep range (difficult exercises); progress barbell lifts by adding weight, progress bodyweight by adding reps until moving to the next progression (p.182).

### Complementing (p.183)
- [CONVENTION] Complementing = training a barbell lift and its bodyweight analog concurrently for different benefits (e.g., bench press for brute pressing strength/hypertrophy, planche for upper-body control/strength simultaneously) — presented as a valid alternative to picking just one exercise exclusively, dependent on the athlete's specific goal (p.183).
- [CONVENTION/SCIENCE] Structural-balance caveat: eliminating vertical pushing/pulling entirely to specialize risks shoulder imbalance over time, because vertical pulling and most pushing exercises both emphasize shoulder internal rotation, which — especially combined with desk-job/school posture — encourages a hunched-forward posture. Horizontal pulling is the typical corrective, since it biases scapular retractors and posterior shoulder much more than vertical pulling (p.183).
- [CONVENTION] Risk-management rule: if imbalance is developing from a complementing/specialized structure, back off toward a more structured all-around routine until corrected, then resume specialization (p.183).

### Supplementing (p.183-184)
- [CONVENTION] Supplementing = one primary lift (bodyweight or barbell) plus assistance work targeting weak links, referencing Jim Wendler's 5/3/1 as the barbell exemplar (main lift: squat/deadlift/bench/OHP; supplemental work addresses weaknesses — e.g. good mornings for weak glutes, straight-leg deadlifts/glute-ham-raise for weak hamstrings, reverse hyperextensions for a weak back; dips or other pressing for bench weaknesses) (p.183).
- The same logic applies with a bodyweight main lift supplemented by weights targeting weaknesses, hypertrophy, or other goals — at an advanced level this can effectively drive both neural adaptation and hypertrophy-relevant strength development. Weighted substitution is also suggested as an injury-management tool: swap in weighted exercises for bodyweight ones that aggravate an injury, allowing it to heal while training continues (p.184).
- Worked planche example: smith-machine planche pushups (undergrip/supinated grip, hips under the bar) let the athlete load brute pressing strength/hypertrophy without reinforcing the back-arching compensation common in bodyweight planche pushups, and without needing to self-correct pushup angle (machine constrains the bar path); loading also gives a trackable progressive-overload metric. Past a certain point, weights are noted as potentially a *better hypertrophy stimulus* than bodyweight exercise, particularly for shoulders/chest (p.184).
- Dumbbell planche pushups (hands above hips) are offered as an alternative to smith-machine work; dumbbells also enable training more advanced skills like maltese/inverted cross. An elbow-conditioning/strength-hypertrophy drill using dumbbells: hold planche 2s → move to maltese, hold 2s → back to planche, repeat 3-5x (p.184).
- Rings remain preferable to dumbbells when accessible, but dumbbells are a workable substitute (p.184).
- [CONVENTION] Additional supplementing benefit: weight can be tuned to a level just heavy enough to build connective tissue without injury — particularly useful for elbow-taxing straight-arm skills (back lever, planche, maltese, inverted cross) (p.184).
- [CONVENTION] Practical gym-access constraint: athletes limited to globo gyms (no bodyweight-friendly setup) or limited home time should design around their actual access from the outset, potentially maintaining two separate routines. Repeated barbell-vs-bodyweight mapping given for this scenario (identical to the substitution table above) (p.184-185).
- Ground-based bodyweight moves (e.g. planche) can be trained at a globo gym in place of a barbell lift on gym days; gym equipment (smith machine, dumbbells) can also be repurposed for bodyweight-goal-specific work (p.185).
- Non-exhaustive mix-and-match exercise lists by category, for building custom hybrid routines (p.185):

  **Barbell Pushing** — Upper: Bench Press, Dumbbell Presses, Decline/Incline Bench, Military Press, Behind-the-Neck Press, Jerks, Push Press. Lower: Goblet Squats, Front Squats, High-Bar Back Squats, Low-Bar Back Squats, Overhead Squats, Hack Squats, Lunges. (Note: Upright Rows excluded from the list due to shoulder impingement risk.)

  **Barbell Pulling** — Upper: One-Arm DB Rows, Seated Rows, Bent-Over Rows, Pendlay Rows, Face Pulls. Lower: Deadlifts, Snatch, Clean/Power Clean, any Olympic lift variant (ground-to-shoulder or hang-to-shoulder).

  **Bodyweight Pushing** — Upper: Handstand Pushups, Handstand Presses, Planche, Pushups, Clapping Pushups, Dips, Maltese, Inverted Cross. Lower: Bodyweight Squats, Pistols/Single-Leg Squat, Shrimp Squats.

  **Bodyweight Pulling** — Upper: Pull-ups (two-arm, uneven, one-arm, etc.), Clapping Pull-ups, Inverted Rows, Inverted Pull-ups, Back Lever, Front Lever, Iron Cross. Lower: Leg Curls, King Deadlifts, Glute Ham Raises.

  **Miscellaneous** — Combined Upper: Muscle-ups, Inverted Muscle-ups, any bar/ring transitional movement (above-to-below or below-to-above). Combined Lower: Sprinting, Stairs, Vertical Leaping, Broad Jumping, and more.

  Exrx.net referenced as a source of animated exercise demonstrations (p.185).
- Leg-exercise classification is noted as inherently fuzzy since most leg exercises hit every leg muscle; the primary distinctions used are push vs. pull, and knee-dominant vs. hip-dominant/hip-hinge. General instruction: balance the routine and address weak points regardless of category fuzziness (p.185).

## Routines (Sequences/Combinations) (p.185-187)

- [CONVENTION] Definition: routines/sequences/combinations string multiple exercises/skills together, forcing transitions between movements the athlete would not otherwise train in typical repetition-based work; transitions themselves are described as very difficult and beneficial for gaining concentric-range-of-motion strength (p.185).
- [CONVENTION] Routines are best suited to **advanced-level** strength, not beginners. Rationale: strength is mostly neurological adaptation, which is best built by training movement patterns in isolation via typical repetition exercises first; stringing many exercises/transitions together doesn't allow the same focus on individual movement patterns — analogous to a barbell complex/quick circuit working better once the athlete is already fairly strong (p.185).
- Routines/combinations are usually performed on rings or parallettes; commonly drawn from FIG Code of Points (COP) progression charts, plus other combinations like handstand → elbow lever → handstand. A sequence of 5-6 skills performed 5 times through is offered as a quick, decent training session alternative to repetitive standard training ("the grind") (p.185).
- Routines are also framed as a way to keep mastered skills fresh by combining them with currently-in-progress movements, producing training stimuli distinct from standard methods — described as one of the primary ways advanced/elite-level strength is developed, where focus shifts from achieving isometric holds to moving *between* them (p.185-186).
- Worked example of transition difficulty: front lever (A-rated skill, per FIG-style difficulty rating) to iron cross (B-rated skill) transition is called a "Pineda," rated D-level — i.e., harder than either static hold alone. Many high-level gymnasts eventually stop doing dedicated strength work and rely on strength-skill routines alone for sufficient stimulus (p.186).
- [CONVENTION] Caveat: routines/sequences should not fully replace dedicated strength work — there is a real trade-off between working movements in a dedicated fashion for strength gain vs. working routines for variety, paralleling the trade-off between full-ROM concentrics and isometrics. The book frames this as an acceptable trade-off only if made deliberately for variety/goals (p.186).
- Gymkana "beginner routine" example (decades-old standard from this exhibitional gymnastics group), given as an ordered sequence (p.186):
  1. Begin from Hang
  2. Muscle-up to Support
  3. L-Sit
  4. Shoulder Stand
  5. Come down to Support
  6. Immediately roll back into Inverted Hang
  7. Back Lever
  8. German Hang Pullout
  9. Swing to Flyaway Dismount (or move back to hang and drop off)

## Chapter Summary (p.187, as stated in book)

- Endurance trains both aerobic endurance and local muscular endurance, with multiple construction techniques available (to-failure, GTG, ladders/pyramids, metabolic conditioning, HIIT/Tabata, LISS).
- Cross training is highly variable; the ideal routine depends on sport, coach, and existing strength/conditioning program — consult a coach before adding bodyweight progressions/skills, and err toward less rather than more.
- Strength and conditioning are fundamental to sport performance and injury prevention.
- Hybrid templates mix barbell and bodyweight exercises into a single routine via substitution, complementing, or supplementing.
- Routines are a legitimate alternate training method for performance, working movements in succession, or enjoyment — best reserved for advanced athletes, and not a full replacement for dedicated strength work.
