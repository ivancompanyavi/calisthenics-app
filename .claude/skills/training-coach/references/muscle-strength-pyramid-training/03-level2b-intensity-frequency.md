# MSP Training — Level 2 (part 2) — Intensity & Frequency

> **Source:** *The Muscle & Strength Pyramid: Training* 3e (Helms/Valdez/Morgan), book pp.46-82. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/msp-training.txt` or PDF pp.46-82.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] research-established · [CONVENTION] the authors' specific evidence-based recommendation · [DISPUTED] contested / authors flag uncertainty.

## What Intensity Means (p.45-46)

- [CONVENTION] Intensity is not "how hard/sore/hardcore a session feels." It is precisely two factors: **Load** (% of 1RM) and **Proximity to failure** (RPE in powerlifting parlance, RIR in bodybuilding parlance) (p.46).
- [SCIENCE] For hypertrophy, both volume and proximity to failure (RIR) matter — you must train close enough to failure for a set to provide a meaningful growth stimulus, especially at lower loads (p.46).
- [SCIENCE] For strength, 1RM is not influenced by how close to failure a set is taken. Specificity — the load used, and (per Ch.4) movement specificity — is the driving factor (p.46).

## Specificity (p.47)

- [SCIENCE] The Principle of Specificity / SAID principle (Specific Adaptations to Imposed Demands): to achieve a particular outcome you must train specifically for that outcome (p.47).
- [SCIENCE] Strength is a specific *performance* adaptation requiring specific neuromuscular adaptation expressed through a specific skilled lift — maximizing strength requires practicing the target movement with similar loads and ranges of motion (p.47).
- [SCIENCE] Hypertrophy is a *physiological* adaptation, not a performance — muscle growth can be achieved across a wide range of exercises/loading schemes provided sufficient volume and proximity to failure are met (p.47).

## Measuring Intensity (p.47-53)

### Percentage of 1RM
- [CONVENTION] Estimate 1RM from a **2-5RM performance** (lower reps in the test = more accurate 1RM estimate) (p.47).
- [SCIENCE] Table 2.3, Repetitions Allowed Table (p.47-48) — reps possible at a given %1RM, deliberately broadened to cover machine exercises and less-experienced lifters (trained lifters on free-weight exercises above 85% should expect the *low* end):

  | % of 1RM | Reps allowed |
  |---|---|
  | 100% | 1 |
  | 95% | 2-4 |
  | 90% | 3-6 |
  | 85% | 5-9 |
  | 80% | 8-12 |
  | 75% | 9-15 |
  | 70% | 11-18 |

- [DISPUTED] Percentage-of-1RM prescription has major practical limitations (p.48-49):
  - Not all exercises suit 1RM testing (e.g., lateral raises — ambiguous end-range).
  - Day-to-day fatigue/readiness swings mean a fixed 80% load might *feel* like 75% or 85% on a given day.
  - If strength has changed since the last 1RM test, programmed % becomes stale (too light if you've improved, too heavy if you've regressed) — and there's no clear answer for how often to retest.
  - Rep variation at a given %1RM is larger than commonly assumed: a study [21] found weightlifters averaged a 12RM at 80% of 1RM while runners averaged a 20RM (gap widened further at 70%: 20 vs 40 reps). Even among trained lifters of similar background, a study of 58 men/women (≥2y experience, ≥1.5x/1x BW squat) found 6-26 reps performed at 70% 1RM (p.48-49).
  - **Book's conclusion**: because of this variability, the authors do **not** use %1RM for load prescription in their programs, though it retains value as a running 1RM estimate to gauge progress for competitive powerlifters/experienced strength lifters (p.49).

### RPE and RIR
- [SCIENCE] RPE (Rating of Perceived Exertion) originated with Borg (1970) for aerobic training and was later adapted (imperfectly) for resistance training (p.49).
- [SCIENCE] Traditional qualitative RPE ("hard"/"very hard") is unreliable for rating an individual set's difficulty, especially near failure — perceived exertion at a true failure point varies by training background, exercise, rep range, and psychology (p.49-50) [24].
- [CONVENTION] Mike Tuchscherer's RIR-based RPE scale is now the dominant practical tool; RPE and RIR are described as "essentially interchangeable" (p.50). Table 2.4, RIR and RIR-based RPE:

  | RPE | Description | RIR |
  |---|---|---|
  | 10 | Max lift, couldn't do more | 0 |
  | 9.5 | No more reps, could do slightly more load | 0 |
  | 9 | Could do 1 more rep | 1 |
  | 8.5 | Could do 1 more rep, possibly 2 | 1-2 |
  | 8 | Could do 2 more reps | 2 |
  | 7.5 | Could do 2, possibly 3 more reps | 2-3 |
  | 7 | Could do 3 more reps | 3 |
  | 5-6 | Could do 4-6 more reps | 4-6 |

- [DISPUTED] Pure RIR lacks fidelity for powerlifting's near-max singles (no equivalent of a 9.5 RPE) (p.50).
- [SCIENCE] RIR accuracy: a 2022 meta-analysis (Halperin et al. [25], 414 participants) found an average **underestimation of ~1 repetition** — RIR is on average quite accurate, but it is a trainable *skill* with individual variance (p.51).
  - Ratings closer to failure (1-2 RIR) are more accurate than ratings further out (3-4 RIR).
  - Accuracy degrades as reps-per-set rise, becoming an issue above **12 reps/set** — likely because high-rep sets to failure produce more discomfort/negative affect than moderate-rep sets, confounding RIR self-rating (p.51-52) [26].
- [CONVENTION] Five strategies to improve RIR/RPE rating skill (p.51-52): (1) record and review sets on video for objective bar-speed/rep-quality assessment; (2) run a 3-6 week low-volume block training frequently to *momentary* failure (with spotters) to calibrate true 0 RIR; (3) compare ratings with an experienced training partner before discussing; (4) get a coach who uses RPE/RIR to review lifting video; (5) test predictions — pick a weight you think allows 8-12 reps, push past your predicted 2 RIR point and see the actual gap.
- [CONVENTION] For lifters new to RIR who don't know where to start loading: use the Repetitions Allowed Table to pick set-1 load from an existing 1RM estimate, then autoregulate off RIR/RPE for subsequent sets (p.52).
  - If actual RIR ≈ target: keep load the same.
  - If only 1 rep in reserve when 2-4 were targeted: too heavy — reduce load.
  - If 5+ reps in reserve when 2-4 targeted: too light — increase load.
  - [CONVENTION] Rule of thumb: adjust load ~**4% per rep off target** [27] — e.g., 2 RIR off target ⇒ adjust load by ~8% on the next set.

### Failure
- [SCIENCE] Two definitions distinguished (p.52-53): **volitional failure** (stopping at your last perceived-completable rep, i.e. 0 RIR/10 RPE by self-report) vs. **momentary failure** (continuing until an actual missed concentric rep, i.e. attempting one more rep after 0 RIR/10 RPE and failing it) [28] — momentary failure is the more objective reference point.
- [SCIENCE] 2024 meta-analysis (~3000 participants for strength, ~1600 for hypertrophy) [7]: proximity to failure affects strength and hypertrophy **differently**:
  - **Strength**: RIR has a **negligible** effect independent of load — a 10-rep-capable set at 80% 1RM produces a similar strength stimulus whether done as 3 reps @7RIR, 5 reps @5RIR, or 8 reps @2RIR, because load is matched (p.53).
  - **Hypertrophy**: a **linear dose-response** exists between proximity to failure and stimulus — a set ended at 5 RIR is less stimulative than one ended at 2 RIR, which is (hypothetically) less stimulative than one taken to momentary failure (p.53).

## Intensity Considerations for Strength (p.53-59)

Three primary contributing factors to strength, in order discussed:

1. **Muscle mass and other structural adaptations** (p.53-54)
   - [SCIENCE] Larger muscles with more contractile tissue can produce more force (p.53) [14]; body-composition/hypertrophy indices in powerlifters correlate strongly with 1RM strength [29-33].
   - [SCIENCE] Other structural contributors: non-contractile elements (titin, extracellular matrix, costameres); connective tissue (tendons, fascia, ligaments); bone mineral content/density; muscle architecture (fascicle length affecting force-length behavior; pennation angle allowing more fibers to pack into a given muscle volume) (p.54).
   - [SCIENCE] Even short-term hypertrophy matters: muscle-size increases in the 12 weeks before a powerlifting meet strongly predict 1RM gains [33] (p.54).

2. **Neuromuscular adaptations** (p.54-55)
   - [SCIENCE] Increased voluntary motor-unit recruitment/firing rate occurs predominantly in **early-stage** strength development and drives initial gains more than hypertrophy does [34-35] (p.54-55). In well-trained lifters, muscle size becomes the dominant explanatory factor for greater unskilled-contraction force output vs. untrained lifters [36-37].

3. **Motor patterns / skill** (p.55-56)
   - [SCIENCE] Real-world strength is expressed through coordinated movement — strength is itself a skill; movement efficiency differentiates elite from novice weightlifters/powerlifters [38-40].
   - [DISPUTED] Frequent maxing-out "works" [41] and entire training systems are built on it, but the book frames strength as skill **plus** structural factors (muscle mass, limb lengths) — frequent maxing alone is not endorsed as sufficient, and movement specificity (Ch.4) adds further nuance beyond simply maxing often (p.55-56).

### The Relationship of Load and Proximity to Failure With Strength (p.55-59)
- [SCIENCE] The 2024 meta-analysis [7] finding that RIR has negligible independent impact on strength gains controlled for load — it does **not** mean 70/80/90% 1RM sets are equivalent, only that RIR-at-a-given-load doesn't matter much (p.55-56).
- [SCIENCE] Two 2022 meta-analyses independently support load (not RIR/volume) as the primary strength driver (p.56):
  - Lopez et al. [9] (failure-trained studies), load bands: Low <60%, Moderate 60-79%, High ≥80% 1RM.
  - Carvalho et al. [42] (volume-load-matched studies), load bands: Very low <30%, Low 30-59%, Moderate 60-79%, High ≥80%.
  - Both found a **linear dose-response**: higher load → greater strength gain, with the largest jump moving moderate→high, and effect size scaling with the size of the load disparity.
- [SCIENCE] Given that volume's effect on strength "maxes out" at a surprisingly low weekly set number [8] and RIR's independent effect is negligible, **load** — not reps or proximity to failure — is the primary strength stimulus (p.56).
- [DISPUTED] Loads ≥90% 1RM: the meta-analyses only classified "high" as ≥80% (8-12RM range); whether ≥90% is meaningfully better is not conclusively answered, though anecdotal/powerlifting evidence and one RCT support it having "notable positive impacts" (p.57).
  - Androulakis-Korakakis et al. [15], 6-week powerlifter study (p.57-59):
    - **Group 1 (Daily Max Only)**: daily single @9-9.5 RPE — squat 2x/wk, bench 3x/wk, deadlift 1x/wk, no back-off work. ≈3 bench / 2.5 squat / 2 deadlift sets/week. Increased total, but gains were small; deadlift barely moved (likely due to its very low volume).
    - **Group 2 (Daily Max + Back-Offs)**: same daily heavy singles **plus** 2×3 @80% of the daily max (avg. RIR 3-5) after each single. ≈9 bench / 7.5 squat / 6 deadlift sets/week. All three lifts improved similarly and by more than Group 1 — total increase exceeded what would be considered "meaningful" over 6 weeks.
    - [DISPUTED] Caveats: short duration, small sample, and unclear whether either group was starting from a true baseline (peaking/detraining state).
    - **Conclusion drawn**: high/very-high-load singles are powerful for short-term strength, but volume still matters (6-9 sets beat 2-3 sets); the *added* volume, however, needn't be huge to capture most of the benefit of specific high-load training (p.59).
  - [CONVENTION] Bottom line: a reasonable volume and frequency of specific, high-load training is the cornerstone of a strength program; additional volume beyond that point is better invested in **hypertrophy work** to support strength long-term (p.59).

## Intensity Considerations for Hypertrophy (p.59-63)

- [SCIENCE] For hypertrophy, absolute bar load matters far less than for strength; proximity to failure (RIR) is critical, with meaningful interactions vs. both high and low loads (p.59).
- [DISPUTED] The "must train to failure" claim is characterized as a **myth** in its black-and-white form — compelling due to bodybuilder anecdote and "no pain no gain" culture [43], but only partially supported by evidence (p.59-60).
- [SCIENCE] 2024 meta-analysis (~1600 participants) [7]: dose-response relationship — as RIR decreases from 10→0, per-set hypertrophy stimulus increases, but there is real uncertainty in the model; RIR is just one of many contributing factors (p.60). Figure 2.7 shows study clustering at 0 vs. 8 RIR, with highest hypertrophy ~+20% at 0 RIR vs. ~+10% at 8 RIR (figure — see PDF p.60).
- [SCIENCE] Load in isolation (~30-90% 1RM) does **not** meaningfully affect hypertrophy when either trained to failure [9] or volume-matched [42] — but there is an **interaction between load and RIR** (p.60-61).
- [DISPUTED] Exploratory sub-analysis (smaller study groups, less statistically robust): as load increases, RIR's influence on hypertrophy **diminishes**. At ~90% 1RM, RIR no longer meaningfully affects hypertrophy — 0-7 RIR produce similar growth at that load (p.61). Figure 2.8 depicts this flattened relationship at 90% 1RM (figure — see PDF p.61-62).
- [SCIENCE] Corroborating support: Refalo et al. [44] found equivalent quadriceps hypertrophy in highly trained lifters whether trained to failure or to 1-2 RIR, on sets of 8-10 (leg press) and 10-12 (leg extension) reps (p.62).
- [SCIENCE] For a heavy-load set to "count" on equal footing with higher-rep, lower-load sets for hypertrophy, it must last **at least ~4-5 repetitions** [4, 11-13] (p.62).
- [CONVENTION] Practical implication: in the ~4-6 rep range you can likely train at **3-4 RIR** and still produce a comparable hypertrophy stimulus to training closer to or at failure (p.62).
- [SCIENCE] As load decreases, RIR must decrease too (train closer to failure) in a roughly linear fashion to maintain stimulus — because lower loads recruit fewer fibers per rep, so higher-load sets reach a sufficient hypertrophy stimulus further from failure than light-load sets [45] (p.62).
- [DISPUTED] Very high-rep sets (25-30RM) to failure produce markedly worse discomfort/perceived exertion/negative affect than moderate-rep failure sets (8-12RM), especially for multi-joint compound lifts (hack squat, bench, lat pulldown) — this may cause RIR *overestimation of proximity* (i.e., you think you're further from failure than you are), reducing the real-world stimulus of very high-rep training (p.62) [26].
  - Lasevicius et al. [46]: volume-equated training to momentary failure produced greater biceps/quad hypertrophy at 80% vs 20% 1RM, but **no difference** between 60% and 40% 1RM (p.62).
- [SCIENCE] Injury-rate evidence for load selection: a volume-load-matched study comparing 7×2-4RM vs. 3×8-12RM (full battery of upper/lower, free-weight and machine, multi-joint and isolation) found a **20% dropout rate from joint pain** in the high-load group vs. **0%** in the moderate-load group [47] (p.62).
- [CONVENTION] Nuanced, exercise-type-specific RIR/load guidance rather than one-size-fits-all (p.62-63):
  - Multi-joint, especially **lower-body**, exercises (which spike general fatigue when taken to failure at high reps): use higher load + moderate RIR.
  - Machine-based, upper-body multi-joint exercises: moderate-to-high reps, moderate-to-low RIR.
  - Single-joint (isolation) exercises: higher reps, low RIR, closer to failure — a good option when joint discomfort limits heavy loading elsewhere.

## Intensity Recommendations (p.63-65)

- [CONVENTION] Hypertrophy: load doesn't need to be heavy, just "heavy enough" (**above ~30% 1RM**); RIR should scale **inversely with load**. In the 4-6 rep range you can stay several reps shy of failure — particularly useful for lower-body free-weight lifts (squat/hinge), which recruit large muscle mass, are technically demanding, and carry higher risk/fatigue cost if pushed to failure at high reps with no clear upside (p.63-64).
- [CONVENTION] Example hypertrophy leg-day set-up illustrating the load/RIR gradient across an exercise sequence (p.64):
  - Start: 4-6 rep sets @3-4 RIR, or 6-8 reps @2-3 RIR, on squats/deadlifts.
  - Follow with leg press at 6-8 reps @2-3 RIR, or 8-12 reps @1-2 RIR.
  - Finish with isolation work at 8-12+ reps @0-2 RIR, optionally to failure on final sets.
- [CONVENTION] Strength: specificity is key, so emphasis is on heavier loads — but *not* maximal-every-set. Because the volume needed to maximize short-term strength is relatively low (see volume unit), once that low volume of specific, high-load work is done, additional volume is better spent on hypertrophy work supporting the involved muscles long-term (p.64).
- [SCIENCE] Table 2.5, Intensity Recommendations Table (p.65) — described by the authors as *appropriate average target ranges*, not fixed prescriptions:

  | | Hypertrophy | Strength |
  |---|---|---|
  | Load | ~4-30RM (~30-90% 1RM) | 1-8RM (~80%+ 1RM) |
  | RIR | 4-0 RIR and failure, per load/rep combo:<br>• 4-6 reps/set → 4-0 RIR<br>• 6-8 reps/set → 3 RIR to failure<br>• 8-12 reps/set → 2 RIR to failure<br>• >12 reps/set → 1 RIR to failure | ~7-0 RIR per load/rep combo. Load, not RIR, dictates strength — RIR is a *function* of load (higher loads inherently allow fewer reps and sit closer to failure). |

  Table footnote: high load is effective for hypertrophy even at high RIR, but sets must be ≥4 reps; RIR underestimation is more likely at low loads (p.65).

## Frequency (p.65-66)

- [CONVENTION] Frequency = how weekly training dose is distributed across sessions. Increasing frequency is not about doing more total training — it's a distribution tool that can (a) enhance skill development and (b) improve the relationship between training stress and recovery so no single session becomes overly taxing (p.65).

## How Frequency Can Impact Training (p.65-67)

### Skill retention
- [SCIENCE] Educational research: spacing study sessions over multiple days improves long-term retention vs. cramming into one session, even though cramming can maximize next-day recall [48]. Some evidence this generalizes to motor-learning tasks [49] (p.66).
- [CONVENTION] Strength is a skill developed through practice; cramming all sets into one day may degrade attentional focus/movement quality across that session — spreading volume across sessions may aid skill acquisition, partly via sleep/rest-mediated memory consolidation between sessions [49] (p.66).
- [DISPUTED] Counterpoint (untested but plausible): lifters need to *notice* technical errors to correct them, and error-amplification coaching outperforms simply pointing errors out [50]. Spreading training too thin (e.g., one set/session across 6 days/week) may not give enough reps-per-session to notice and correct errors, vs. a top set + 2 back-off sets allowing in-session correction. The book notes this specific ultra-low-per-session structure is rarely seen in practice or research, so it remains theoretical (p.66).
- [SCIENCE] Despite sparse direct motor-learning-for-lifting data, higher frequencies show independently beneficial associations with strength gains, with diminishing returns [8] (p.66).

### Training quality and recovery
- [DISPUTED] Anecdotal claims run both directions — some say low frequency (more rest days) is needed for recovery from hard training; others say higher frequency spreads stress so no single session is overly demanding, improving recovery and weekly training quality. The authors' coaching experience: **ideal frequency is highly individual**, depending on individual recovery speed (sometimes independent of volume, if intensity is high), exercise-specific lingering discomfort (e.g., many powerlifters report prolonged lumbar discomfort from deadlifts), and individual ability to sustain focus across longer vs. shorter/more frequent sessions (p.66-67).
- [SCIENCE] When training dose is held equal, frequency's **independent effect** is negligible for hypertrophy [8]; frequency has a positive effect on strength, but most of the benefit is realized at a relatively low number of weekly occurrences (p.67).

## The Relationship of Frequency With Hypertrophy and Strength (p.67-70)

- [SCIENCE] The 2024 meta-analysis [8] used **fractional counting** for frequency, mirroring the fractional set-counting method used for volume — e.g., two weekly bench sessions = frequency 2 for chest, 1 for triceps (bench counted as a half-day for triceps); two bench sessions + one OHP session/week = frequency 2.5 for bench, 2 for OHP (p.67).

### Frequency and strength
- [SCIENCE] >2000 participants: a dose-response relationship between frequency and strength gain rate — notable increase from 1→2x/week, continuing to increase (with diminishing returns) through 3, 4, 5, and 6x/week (the highest frequency analyzed) (p.68).
- [SCIENCE] The most meaningful single jump is **1→2x/week per lift**, raising rate of strength gain from **~13% to ~17%**. To achieve a similarly-sized further increase requires going all the way up to **6x/week** — sharply diminishing returns beyond 2x/week (p.68).
- [CONVENTION] This validates the modern raw powerlifting/weightlifting practice of training competition lifts multiple times per week (or per day, for weightlifting) (p.68).

### Frequency and hypertrophy
- [SCIENCE] >1000 participants: a positive but **much smaller** relationship between frequency and hypertrophy rate — roughly **one-tenth** the magnitude of frequency's effect on strength. Arguably the independent effect of frequency on hypertrophy is negligible (p.69).
- [SCIENCE] No meaningful relationship was found between hypertrophy and weekly frequency **per muscle group** specifically — frequencies of 1+ times/week were similarly effective, all else equal (p.69-70).
- [CONVENTION] Real-world confound: higher-frequency training usually correlates with higher-volume training (bodybuilders train more often *to* accumulate more volume) — it's the volume that drives the benefit, not the frequency itself. A limitation flagged: average weekly volumes in this analysis were relatively low (8 sets for strength, 13 for hypertrophy), so the volume-frequency relationship could differ at higher volumes (p.69).
- [SCIENCE] Remmert et al. 2025 meta-regression (~3 dozen studies, >1000 participants) [51] assessed volume **per muscle group per session** (not just per week) and found a diminishing-returns relationship at the session level: after **~11 sets per muscle group per session**, additional benefit becomes uncertain (descriptive, not strictly prescriptive) (p.70).
- [CONVENTION] Practical guideline derived from the above: increase weekly frequency per muscle group once single-session volume for that muscle would exceed **~11 sets**, to preserve per-set training efficiency by spreading volume across more days (p.70).
- [CONVENTION] Table 2.6, Frequency Recommendations by Volume (p.70):

  | Weekly fractional sets per muscle | Weekly frequency per muscle |
  |---|---|
  | 4-10 | 1-2 |
  | 11-20 | 2-3 |
  | 21-30 | 3+ |

## Frequency Recommendations (p.71-72)

- [CONVENTION] No single ideal frequency — it depends on total training volume and individual schedule (time-per-session availability vs. days-per-week availability) (p.71).
- [CONVENTION] **~3-6 sessions/week is appropriate for most goals**; even 2x/week can work well for time-pressed novices (p.71).
- [CONVENTION] Three-step process to determine ideal frequency (p.71-72):
  1. **Determine weekly volume** (exercises, sets, rest periods, warm-ups) needed for the goal; estimate time required; consider what frequency lets you break volume into focused, manageable chunks.
  2. **Apply the theory**:
     a. Hypertrophy: don't exceed **~11 fractional sets per muscle group in a session** — increase frequency if needed to stay within this range, weighing mental focus, recovery, and personal preference for session length/pace.
     b. Strength: prioritize high-quality practice — give at least **2 weekly "exposures" per movement**, even if one is an easy exposure (low RIR and volume, e.g., 3×1×80% 1RM), since this can still meaningfully help gains.
  3. **Test and adjust**:
     a. If a single day's volume is too much to perform well, redistribute across the week or add a session (schedule permitting).
     b. If a day is "easy" (completed with minimal fatigue), consider adding volume there so other sessions carry less fatigue burden.
- [CONVENTION] Table 2.7, Frequency Recommendations (p.72) — average values for a training block, expected to change over time in a periodized plan (covered in Ch.3):

  | Goal | Frequency Guideline |
  |---|---|
  | Hypertrophy | ≥1x/week per muscle group, based on volume. If a session exceeds ~11 fractional sets for a muscle, increase frequency to distribute volume better. |
  | Strength | 2-6x/week per lift, depending on volume. Spread sets across as many sessions as possible, with 1-2 direct sets per lift per session. |

  Footnote: high volume is best distributed with higher frequency for hypertrophy; increased frequency benefits strength via enhanced skill practice and movement quality (p.72).

## Volume and Intensity Interact (p.73-77)

- [CONVENTION] Both volume and RIR/proximity-to-failure independently show **diminishing returns** for hypertrophy — but they interact, and naive "more of both is always better" reasoning is flawed (p.73).
- [CONVENTION] Anecdotal failure mode: as lifters chase more volume, they unconsciously drift further from failure (pacing to survive the workload), eroding the very stimulus quality the added volume was meant to provide (p.73).
- [CONVENTION] Illustrative interaction: going from **5→15 sets/muscle/week** produces roughly **twice** the expected gain of going from **15→25 sets** (both are +10-set increases, but the first captures far more of the volume-response curve). Similarly, the hypertrophic difference between training at 3 RIR, 2 RIR, 1 RIR, or to failure exists but is relatively small (p.73-74).
  - Practical implication: 5→15 sets (low→moderate volume) buys a substantial gain without meaningfully compromising RIR. But 15→25 sets (moderate→high volume) buys only a small gain — and if pushing that extra volume costs you even ~2 RIR further from failure on average (to survive the workload), that small volume benefit can be **entirely negated**, because the moderate→high volume increment was *also* a small effect. Net result: nearly double the volume, more fatigue and frustration, no better outcome (p.74).

### What the data can't tell you (p.74-76)
- [DISPUTED] The authors explicitly warn against treating research as an infallible "guru" — studies estimate relationships, they don't hand down fixed rules; findings evolve as science self-corrects (p.74).
- [DISPUTED] No studies exist on multi-year training plans or on manipulating many programming variables simultaneously; sport science "cannot, and was never meant to, tell you what to do in the gym on Tuesday" (p.75).
- [CONVENTION] Fractional set-counting is a *useful, wrong* model: on average it fits the data across dozens of studies/thousands of individuals, but individual exercises within a "half-set" category are not literally equally effective (a bench-press set is not literally half as good for triceps as a triceps pushdown) — exercise-specific and individual factors (limb lengths, injury history, prior training) shift real-world transfer (p.75-76).

### These guidelines are dynamic (p.76-78)
- [CONVENTION] Guidelines represent what works best *on average* for most people, all else equal — some individuals will do better deviating from them (p.76).
- [CONVENTION] Guidelines are best read as **appropriate average values over the course of a training block**, not fixed weekly targets — training phases will emphasize higher or lower intensity/volume/frequency at different points (detailed in the next Pyramid level) (p.76-77). Best starting point for most beginner-to-intermediate lifters; understanding the underlying relationships matters more than the specific numbers for diagnosing plateaus later (p.77).
- Figure 2.11, "Assessing and Responding to Plateaus" flowchart (p.77) — full decision logic preserved:
  1. **Are you plateaued?** (not making gym progress appropriate to training status)
     - **No** → don't change anything.
     - **Yes** → check foundational factors: sleeping 7+ hours? Not in an energy deficit? Protein ≥0.7g/lb (1.6g/kg)+? RPE neither over- nor under-estimated? Training muscles/lifts min. 2x/week (for strength goals)? Exercise selection/ROM/tempo appropriate and consistent?
       - If **yes** to any deficiency → fix that issue, run another mesocycle, reassess.
       - If **no** to all (i.e., all foundations are already solid) → ask: **are you recovering?** (dreading the gym? sleep quality worse than normal? load/reps actually decreasing? life stress worse than normal? aches/pains worse than normal?)
         - If **no** to all, or all-but-1 of these recovery red flags → likely recovering fine → **it may be time to increase training dose**.
         - If **yes** to 2+ red flags → likely not recovering → **temporarily reduce volume / take a light week**. If that restores performance, good. If you quickly re-fatigue and stall again, that signals a need for more than a deload/taper — consider a genuine reduction in volume or reorganizing it to better manage fatigue.
- [CONVENTION] Closing emphasis: the guidelines target the **practical effective dose**, not the maximum effective dose — and Adherence remains the base of the Pyramid underlying all of this (p.77).
