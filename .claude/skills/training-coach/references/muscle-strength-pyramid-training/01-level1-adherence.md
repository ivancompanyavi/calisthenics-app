# MSP Training — Level 1 — Adherence

> **Source:** *The Muscle & Strength Pyramid: Training* 3e (Helms/Valdez/Morgan), book pp.9-24. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/msp-training.txt` or PDF pp.9-24.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] research-established · [CONVENTION] the authors' specific evidence-based recommendation · [DISPUTED] contested / authors flag uncertainty.

## Why Adherence is the pyramid's foundation (p.9-10)
- The pyramid places Adherence as Level 1 — the base layer everything else stands on — because **a program only succeeds if it is followed**: "even the most 'optimal' plan won't work if you can't stick to it" (p.9). [CONVENTION] — this is the book's foundational framing/ordering principle, not a single research finding.
- Corollary prescription: build the plan around what you *can* do, not what you wish you could do in an ideal world (p.10).
- Long-term consistency > short-term perfection: "A slightly less-than-optimal plan you can stick to will outperform the perfect plan you can't" (p.11). [CONVENTION]

## The three conditions for training adherence (p.10)
For any training program to be sustainable, it must meet three conditions, forming the acronym **REF**:
1. **R**ealistic
2. **E**njoyable
3. **F**lexible

These conditions are described as general to achieving most goals, not specific to strength training (p.10). [CONVENTION] — the authors' organizing framework; not itself a cited empirical claim, though each pillar below is backed by evidence or mechanism.

The book explicitly rejects a "grit it out" mindset: training doesn't need to be hard for hard's sake, and setting up an unsustainable program impresses no one (p.10).

## Realistic: is your training schedule sustainable and practical? (p.10-11)
Two components to check for realism:
- **Time frame** — align the training approach with your actual real-life time horizon (e.g., an 8-week meet prep, a 24-week bodybuilding show, or an informal "look good by summer" goal). Forcing a mismatched cookie-cutter program's timeline (e.g., an 8-week program into a 6-week window) causes problems (p.10). [CONVENTION]
- **Weekly/daily schedule** — start from what you can actually do (available days/hours), *then* fit training to that, rather than picking an "optimal" schedule (e.g., 6 days/week, 2 hr/session) that doesn't fit your life and abandoning it. "Begin with what you can do before thinking about what you should do" (p.11). [CONVENTION]

## Enjoyable: can you train this way for a long time? (p.12-13)
- Mechanism given: if you enjoy training, you put in more effort even on a technically suboptimal plan, and that extra effort compounds over time more than plan optimality does (p.12). [CONVENTION]
- Enjoyment-effort-results is framed as a **positive feedback loop**: enjoyment drives effort, effort drives results, results reinforce desire to continue (p.12).
- CrossFit is used as an illustrative case: gains attributed less to programming superiority and more to community/structure/energy sustaining adherence (p.12).
- Figure 1.1 ("REF Yourself: Training Should Be Realistic, Enjoyable, and Flexible") depicts enjoyment, results, and training difficulty on interacting axes (figure — see PDF p.12; text elements only partially extractable).
- **[SCIENCE] Process focus vs. goal focus.** Cited study: Fishbach & Choi (2012), "When thinking about goals undermines goal pursuit," multi-experiment study (p.12, ref [1]). Key experiment: two groups ran on a treadmill; one group was told to focus on the end goal, the other on the process of running itself; both told to run as long as they wanted and predict their duration. Result: the goal-focused group ran for a *shorter* time than predicted; the process-focused group *exceeded* their prediction. Takeaway: enjoying the process (not fixating on the end goal) sustains greater effort/duration (p.12-13).
- Caveat: even an in-the-moment enjoyable program will stop being enjoyable — and stop producing results — if it causes family stress, poor sleep, or mounting anxiety (p.13). Enjoyment must be assessed in the context of the person's whole life, not just the training session itself.
- **An Aside: Is there such a thing as "optimal"? (p.13)** [DISPUTED framing by the authors themselves] "Optimal" is described as a concept/aspiration, not a fixed, knowable destination — science can show approach A tends to beat approach B in a given context, but can't certify absolute optimality because of the number of variables and because science itself keeps evolving. The book warns that chasing a "perfect" plan is a trap that makes lifters vulnerable to marketing promising secret shortcuts or "revolutionary" systems. Recommended focus instead: consistent application of sound principles, tailored to sustainability and to the individual's life (p.13).

## Flexible: do you have flexibility to accommodate the unexpected? (p.14-15)
- Flexibility is called a *necessity*, not a nice-to-have — it supports and reinforces both the realistic and enjoyable pillars (p.14).
- Purpose: over a training career measured in years, roadblocks (work, sick kids, travel, mental rough patches) are inevitable; what matters is the *ability to adapt*, not perfect plan-following (p.14).
- Flexibility prevents the **all-or-nothing trap**, where one missed workout spirals into abandoning a whole week or the whole plan (p.14).
- Flexibility is grounded in understanding training *principles* (the "why"), which lets you make confident smart adjustments instead of feeling like you've failed when deviating from the letter of the plan (p.14-15).

### Flexibility when stress is high (p.15)
- **[SCIENCE]** Individuals experiencing higher levels of perceived negative life stress during a training program tend to adapt less effectively (p.15, ref [2]: Bartholomew et al. 2008, *J Strength Cond Res* — strength gains after resistance training and the effect of stressful, negative life events).
- Mechanism/implication: trying to control every variable to force progress can itself become an added stressor. Because unpredictable disruptions (work stress, conflict, financial concern, illness, family emergencies) are inevitable, the book recommends **auto-regulating training to match current recovery capacity** rather than forcing progress through willpower (p.15). [CONVENTION] — auto-regulation methods are deferred to later chapters, not detailed here.
- Core principle stated directly: "your plan needs room to bend so it doesn't break" (p.15).

### Flexibility to adjust based on energy levels (p.15-16)
- **[SCIENCE]** Study 1 (ref [3]: McNamara & Stearne 2010, *J Strength Cond Res*, "Flexible Nonlinear Periodization in a Beginner College Weight Training Class"): two groups did the same workouts over 12 weeks with matched total volume. One followed a fixed session order; the other chose an easy/moderate/hard session each day based on how they felt. Both groups completed all sessions, but **the flexible group made greater strength gains** (p.15).
- **[SCIENCE]** Study 2 (ref [4]: Colquhoun et al. 2017, *J Strength Cond Res*, "Comparison of powerlifting performance in trained men using traditional and flexible daily undulating periodization"): trained lifters split into a fixed structured weekly plan (strength/hypertrophy/power sessions in set order) vs. a group that chose session type week-to-week based on readiness. **The autonomy group stuck to their programs more consistently** (p.15-16).
- Takeaway/prescription: once a schedule is built, follow it when possible; but on a drained day, prefer **swapping in the lightest already-planned session of the week** rather than forcing the hard day as scheduled (p.16). [CONVENTION]
- Analogy drawn: flexible programming for training adherence parallels flexible dieting typically outperforming rigid dieting (p.15-16). [CONVENTION] — analogy, not a cited study within this unit.

## "Life doesn't stop for lifting" — applying flexibility to real disruptions (p.16-21)

### Missed workouts (p.16-17)
- Default rule: **pick up where you left off** — e.g., if Monday/Wednesday/Friday training and Wednesday is missed, just do that session on Friday; there's "nothing sacred" about the day-of-week structure, and finishing a plan a few days late is not meaningfully "falling behind" (p.16-17). [CONVENTION]
- Explicit caution against **cramming missed sessions into a shorter window**: doubling up on intense sessions raises fatigue and can compromise quality or delay recovery — net worse than simply sliding the schedule (p.17).
- Exception: **competition prep with a fixed non-negotiable deadline** — in that case, pushing everything back may not be feasible; must instead skip or reschedule to fit without compromising future sessions (p.17).
- Low-stress sessions (accessory day, technique-focused power day, active-recovery-oriented session) can simply be **skipped** without much harm, or slotted into a spare day (Thursday/Sunday) later in the week (p.17).
- **[SCIENCE]** Even when back-to-back hard sessions are unavoidable, several studies show training three consecutive days can yield strength/hypertrophy gains similar to the same workouts spaced with rest days between (p.17, refs [5-7]: Hunter 1985; Carvalho & Rodrigues 2018; Yang et al. 2018). Recommendation when this happens: **auto-regulate loads** to the intended effort/RPE for that day rather than rigidly hitting the prescribed load on the spreadsheet, to get through the volume without overreaching (p.17). [CONVENTION]

### Sports, outdoor, and fitness activities outside of lifting (p.17-19)
- Strictly maximizing hypertrophy/strength might argue for minimizing outside activity, but the book frames "being a well-rounded, happy person" as the better definition of optimal (p.17-18).
- **[SCIENCE]** Being outdoors, socially connected, and part of a community reduces stress and boosts mood (p.18, refs [8-10]: Richards et al. 2015; Yorks et al. 2017; Tillmann et al. 2018) — these benefits can outweigh the downside of "non-optimal" extra activity.
- Key principle for dual-activity lifters: **you can't treat lifting and the other sport as isolated efforts — one must take priority**, and periodization must reconcile the competing stressors (p.18). [CONVENTION]
  - If the sport is primary: lifting becomes an add-on to enhance sport performance — avoid high-risk exercises, avoid training to failure, use minimal volume/intensity sufficient to help performance, avoid excess soreness (p.18).
  - If lifting (strength/physique) is primary: don't chase an "optimal" separate conditioning program for the recreational sport either — same subordination principle applies in reverse (p.18-19).
- **[SCIENCE] The interference effect.** Endurance and strength adaptations can physiologically compete (efficient low-force-long-duration output vs. high-force-short-burst output) (p.19). The interference effect does **not** meaningfully blunt hypertrophy but **can** slow strength/power gains (p.19, refs [11,12]: Schumann et al. 2022 meta-analysis; Huiberts et al. 2024 meta-analysis).
- **[SCIENCE] Mitigating interference via sequencing/timing:**
  - Doing cardio *before* lifting is more problematic than doing it *after* (p.19, ref [12,13]).
  - Separating cardio and lifting by **at least 3 hours** is better than doing them back-to-back (p.19).
  - Putting cardio and lifting on **different days entirely** is best of all (p.19, refs [12,13]: Robineau et al. 2016).
- **[CONVENTION] Cardio modality/duration guidance:** where possible and goal-appropriate, prefer low-impact, low-eccentric-component modalities (elliptical, cycling, swimming, rowing) to limit joint-stress/muscle-damage fatigue; keep higher-intensity cardio sessions to **20-30 minutes** in most cases; if shorter high-intensity sessions don't fit the schedule, keep sessions **under an hour** (p.19, ref [14]: Wilson et al. 2012 meta-analysis on concurrent training interference).

### Injury (p.19-21)
- Framing: serious resistance training carries injury risk, but the health benefits of a lifting-inclusive life are argued to outweigh it; a sedentary lifestyle carries its own injury/health-complication risk (p.19-20).
- **[SCIENCE] Table 1.1 — Strength sport injury rates (injuries per 1,000 training hours)**, sourced from Keogh & Winwood [15] and Tung et al. [16] (p.19):

  | Sport | Injury rate (per 1000 hrs) |
  |---|---|
  | Bodybuilding | 0.24–1.0 [15] |
  | Powerlifting | 1.0–4.4 [16] |
  | Weightlifting | 2.4–3.3 [16] |
  | CrossFit | 3.1 [15] |
  | Strongman | 4.5–6.1 [15] |
  | Highland Games | 7.5 [15] |

  For comparison, basketball injury rates are reported at **8.5-11.1 per 1,000 hours** (p.20, ref [17]: Cumps et al. 2007) — i.e., resistance training disciplines (bodybuilding especially) carry markedly lower injury rates than mainstream non-contact team sports.
- Explicit non-medical disclaimer: the authors are not physical therapists or medical doctors; significant injuries should go to a physical therapist or sports injury doctor, ideally one experienced with lifters — social media (or a training book) is not the place for diagnosis/treatment (p.20).
- Distinction drawn between **significant injury** (needs professional care) and **minor aches/pains/strains/niggles** ("part of the serious lifter's experience") that don't warrant stopping training altogether (p.20).
- Red flags requiring caution / not pushing through: pain that is **persistent, intense, radiating, accompanied by visible bruising, dizziness, nausea, or other unusual symptoms** (p.20).
- Counter-caution against catastrophizing: cited example of contest-prep athletes with back issues who substituted squats/deadlifts with hip thrusts, leg extensions, and curls, and swapped free-weight upper body work for machines — and still competed successfully (p.20).
- **[SCIENCE] Pain ≠ reliable injury/damage indicator.** Pain perception is shaped by emotional state, fatigue, past experience, and sleep quality; imaging findings often don't correlate with reported pain (some people have disc damage with no pain, others report pain with no clear mechanical cause) (p.20, ref [18]: Hall et al. 2021, BMJ — "Do not routinely offer imaging for uncomplicated low back pain"). Framed metaphor: pain is "a radar system with adjustable sensitivity" — can flag real issues but also throws false positives and false negatives (p.20).
- **[CONVENTION] Practical middle ground:** if a specific exercise/load/ROM/modality causes **consistent, reproducible pain**, see a specialist, who can help modify ROM, reduce load, or substitute a pain-free movement (p.21).
- **[SCIENCE] Blood flow restriction (BFR) as a load-reduction tool**, especially for single-joint exercises: BFR allows dropping load to as low as **20% of 1RM** while still providing a solid hypertrophy stimulus (p.21, ref [19]: Hughes et al. 2017 systematic review/meta-analysis, BFR in clinical musculoskeletal rehabilitation).
- Escalation rule: if the issue can't be worked around easily, or pain lingers **more than a few weeks**, seek professional help (p.21). [CONVENTION]

## Summary (p.21)
- Mnemonic to self-audit periodically: **REF yourself** — Realistic, Enjoyable, Flexible — check in regularly that the plan still aligns with current circumstances and preferences (p.21).
- Restated thesis: "optimal" on paper rarely survives contact with real-life stress; the program must be tailored to what the individual can sustain, enjoy, and adapt — doing so raises long-term success odds (p.21). [CONVENTION]
