# MSP Training — Introduction & The Pyramid Model

> **Source:** *The Muscle & Strength Pyramid: Training* 3e (Helms/Valdez/Morgan), book pp.1-8. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/msp-training.txt` or PDF pp.1-8.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] research-established · [CONVENTION] the authors' specific evidence-based recommendation · [DISPUTED] contested / authors flag uncertainty.

## Purpose of the book (p.1)
- Goal: build a strong, adaptable foundation for long-term progress via a step-by-step, evidence-based, goal-oriented training plan that supports both strength and hypertrophy while aligning with lifestyle (p.1).
- Before covering the six pyramid levels, the book establishes two "core skills" that underlie every training decision: (1) prioritizing what matters most, (2) adjusting training variables in response to life's inevitable changes (p.1).

## 1. Prioritizing what matters most (p.2)
- [CONVENTION] Core claim: many training factors influence each other and jointly cause adaptation; combining a century of gym experience with several decades of research lets the authors establish "a fairly clear order of importance" for what will and won't yield the most from training effort (p.2).
- When faced with seemingly conflicting advice (exercise choice, load, set count, training to failure, explosive vs. slow "feel the burn" lifting, etc.), the prescribed method is to judge how important each factor is *relative to your goals* and how it affects other training aspects — viewing variables through a hierarchy ("pyramid of importance") to avoid confusion (p.2).
- Analogy given: "fill your cup to the brim" — place big rocks (highest-priority variables) before pebbles (mid-priority), and pebbles before sand (lowest-priority, finishing-touch variables) (p.2).

## 2. Learning to adjust (p.3)
- [CONVENTION] Second core skill for long-term progress: knowing how and when to adjust the plan over time without losing sight of what matters most — requires critical thinking, not a "rigid, black-and-white mindset" (p.3).
- The book explicitly critiques absolutist framing common in commercial training content. Examples of the wrong kind of question (context-free, binary):
  - Are squats the best exercise?
  - Is the leg press inferior to the squat?
  - Is 5x5 better than 3x8?
  - Is more volume better?
  - Is training twice a week enough?
  - Will training every day cause me to overtrain? (p.3)
- These are flawed because they ignore context. The book's reframed, context-sensitive versions:
  - What is unique about the barbell squat that makes it worthy of inclusion in a training program? What are its limitations?
  - When is the leg press appropriate?
  - What set/rep patterns are appropriate for what exercises, for *me*, and *when*?
  - What impact will adding more sets have on my recovery and results?
  - What is the best way to split appropriate training volume over the training week? (p.3)
- [CONVENTION] Thesis: this style of question-asking leads to better decisions and results, not just for one program but across an entire lifting career (p.3).

## The Levels of the Pyramid (p.3)
- Structure: an organizational hierarchy of training priorities with **six levels**. Most important elements sit at the **bottom** and occupy the **largest area** — they form the foundation of a training program (p.3).
- Ordering rationale [CONVENTION]: it is likely that ~80% of training progress comes from focusing on the bottom four levels (Adherence, Volume & Intensity, Progression, Exercise Selection); the top two levels (Rest Periods, Tempo) contribute only "very small finishing touches." Discussion of the top levels is framed mostly around *how not to do something wrong*, rather than optimization upside (p.3-4).
- Explicit disclaimer: unlike a nutrition pyramid, the training pyramid's layers are "less clear-cut" because training variables are highly interdependent — but the construct still helps achieve goals more efficiently and gives a deeper contextual understanding of the principles. Intended audience use-cases: trainers building client plans, students converting theory into action (p.4).

## Level 1: Adherence (p.4)
- [CONVENTION] Framing claim: strength training is a long game — meaningful results require time, consistency, and a plan that can actually be followed; even the most "optimal" plan on paper is worthless if it can't be sustained (p.4).
- Prescribed priority order: build the plan around what you *can* do, not what you wish you could do in a perfect world — first priority is fitting the plan to your life, not the reverse (p.4).
- Chapter scope stated: the factors that influence adherence, and how to build a plan that can be maintained "week after week, long enough to see real progress" (p.4).

## Level 2: Volume and Intensity (p.5)
- [CONVENTION] Volume and intensity together form the foundation of programs: they constitute the training "dose," and are described as interrelated and inseparable — one affects the other, and this interaction is context-dependent (p.5).
- [DISPUTED/framing] Depending on perspective, *either* volume or intensity could be considered the single most important variable; because of this ambiguity the book places them in the same pyramid layer rather than ranking one above the other (p.5).
- Optimal volume/intensity combination is stated to vary by: training age, goals, preferences, schedule, and current stage of one's lifting career. Frequency modifications can also make the same training dose more or less efficient (p.5).
- Flagged as the book's longest section and covering its most important concepts (p.5).

## Level 3: Progression (p.5)
- [SCIENCE] Named principle: **Progressive Overload** — the training stimulus must be increased gradually to keep producing gains in size/strength (p.5).
- [CONVENTION] Important nuance: with an *appropriate* training dose (per Level 2), overload occurs somewhat automatically — so the progressive-overload principle is described as "primarily addressed in Level 2," not a fully independent lever (p.5).
- Training-status-dependent claim: for novice/early-intermediate lifters, simply setting up a plan with appropriate volume, intensity, and frequency for one's goals produces gains "without much additional thought or effort" — even reusing the same set/rep schemes week to week will still drive gradual strength/size increases at this stage. A deliberate progression *plan* only becomes necessary "at a certain stage of development" to sustain continued progress (p.5).
- Chapter scope: detailed progression theory for both hypertrophy and strength (p.5).

## Level 4: Exercise Selection (p.5-6)
- [CONVENTION] Importance of exercise selection is goal-dependent, not universal:
  - For **competitive strength athletes**, exercise selection is critical because sport performance is judged directly by specific competition movements — training those lifts is a given. The relevant question becomes how much time to allocate to competition lifts vs. assistance lifts, and how assistance lifts transfer (directly or indirectly) to competition-lift performance (p.5-6).
  - For **hypertrophy**, a wide variety of exercises can stimulate muscle growth, but exercise biomechanics and individual anthropometry (body measurements/proportions) mean some exercises suit some people/contexts better than others (p.6).
- Individual-structure example given: a powerlifter who is not well-built to squat (per their limb/torso proportions) may be better served by more assistance work than a powerlifter with more favorable proportions for squatting, even though the sport requires the squat (p.6).
- Physique athletes must similarly factor individual structure and goals — exercise choice shaped by how well it isolates the intended muscle, how it fits the athlete's body, and how it contributes to symmetry or addresses weak points (p.6).
- Bottom line framing: exercise selection is ultimately about training goal (maximal load lifted vs. maximal muscle built), matched against individual structure (p.6).

## Level 5: Rest Periods (p.6)
- [CONVENTION] Rest between sets affects both training quality and session duration. The book flags persistent confusion about ideal rest length, "especially for hypertrophy," attributing this partly to outdated theories that persisted after the research moved on (p.6).
- Chapter scope: current evidence on rest-period guidelines, plus time-efficient techniques (supersets, rest-pause sets, drop sets), aimed at preserving the intended training stimulus while managing time in the gym for both strength and hypertrophy goals (p.6).

## Level 6: Lifting Tempo (p.7)
- Definition: tempo = the speed at which you lift (p.7).
- [DISPUTED] The book flags this topic as "fraught with misunderstanding," specifically calling out overemphasis on "time under tension" that isn't grounded in a true understanding of tempo's actual impact on muscular tension (p.7).
- Chapter scope: how manipulating tempo impacts training outcomes, and which aspects of tempo actually deserve applied attention (p.7).
