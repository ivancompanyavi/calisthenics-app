# MSP Training — Level 5 — Rest Periods

> **Source:** *The Muscle & Strength Pyramid: Training* 3e (Helms/Valdez/Morgan), book pp.187-204. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/msp-training.txt` or PDF pp.187-204.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] research-established · [CONVENTION] the authors' specific evidence-based recommendation · [DISPUTED] contested / authors flag uncertainty.

## Framing (p.187)
- Rest periods affect fatigue accumulation within a session, which affects the loads/reps you can perform and thus your ability to adapt (p.187). [CONVENTION]
- With straight sets (same load, set → rest → repeat), the main rule is simply "don't cut rest too short" (p.187).
- For time-limited lifters (and advanced/high-volume lifters/competitors), time — not effort — is often the binding constraint, making time-saving techniques (antagonist-paired supersets, peripheral-paired supersets, drop sets, rest-pause sets) valuable (p.187).

## How Long Should You Rest Between Sets? (p.187-188)
- **General principle**: with unlimited time, rest long enough to maintain performance; longer rest (up to a point) → greater inter-set recovery → more reps per set for experienced lifters (p.187). [SCIENCE]
- Senna et al.: 15 trained men, 5 sets to failure at 10RM (bench, leg press, machine flyes, leg extension), resting 1, 3, or 5 min between sets — resting 3-5 min better maintained rep performance across all 5 sets vs. 1 min rest (p.187, ref 1). [SCIENCE]
- **Strength recommendation**: Grgic et al. 2018 systematic review (23 studies, ~500 participants) concluded **at least 2 minutes** rest between sets is necessary to maximize strength gains in trained lifters (p.188, ref 2). [SCIENCE] → For main lifts trained for strength, rest as long as needed to perform at your best; for most this means ≥2 minutes (p.188).

## Rest Periods for Hypertrophy (p.188-189)
- For hypertrophy, load matters less than each set delivering a strong growth stimulus — some drop in reps/load between sets is acceptable provided RIR and total volume are maintained (p.188). [CONVENTION]
- Tradeoff logic: shorter rest reduces per-set stimulus but frees time for more sets in a fixed session length, which can offset the loss ("more stimulus per unit of time") — cites Longo et al. 2022 (ref 3) (p.188). [SCIENCE]
- **Key hypertrophy threshold**: Singer et al. 2024 meta-analysis found hypertrophy differences were **negligible once rest exceeded ~90 seconds**; at ~90s, volume load was no longer meaningfully reduced (p.188, ref 4). [SCIENCE] → **Recommendation: rest at least 90 seconds between sets on average for hypertrophy** (p.200-201). [CONVENTION]
- **Context Matters (p.188-189)**: the 90-second figure is an *average* across studies varying in strength level, rep range, and lift. Modifiers, all [SCIENCE]/mechanistic reasoning given by the book:
  - More muscle mass used → more energy expended → more recovery needed (e.g., 10 squats vs. 10 lateral raises) (p.188).
  - Energy expenditure scales with volume load even within the same exercise (ref 5) — higher-rep sets (lower %1RM) produce a higher volume load and thus need more recovery. Example: 100 kg 1RM, ~25 reps @ 60% (60 kg) vs. ~10 reps @ 80% (80 kg) → nearly 2× difference in volume load (p.188-189).
  - As you get stronger, the same reps/%1RM combo produces a higher absolute volume load (e.g., 80% 1RM rising from 100 kg → 120 kg = 20% higher volume load for the "same" set) (p.189).
  - **Net rule**: 90s may be plenty for single-joint lifts / lighter loads; stronger individuals or heavy multi-joint lower-body lifts will likely need more (p.189). [CONVENTION]
- Core takeaway restated: if no time constraints, rest as much as needed; if time-constrained and unable to hit target volume, consider cutting rest short — the loss can be offset by fitting in more sets (p.189).
- All of the above (90s threshold, tradeoffs) applies to **straight sets only** — other structures (below) can save time while preserving, or only slightly reducing, per-set stimulus (p.189).

## Time-Saving Techniques (p.190-200)

### Antagonist-Paired and Peripheral-Paired Supersets (p.190-193)
- **Superset** = perform one exercise, immediately follow with a different exercise, rest only after both (p.190).
- **Agonist-paired supersets** (same muscle group, e.g., bench press → incline DB press): reduce force output vs. straight sets to failure → fewer total reps (ref 5); better classified as a time-efficiency technique, not an "intensity technique" (p.190). [SCIENCE]
- **Antagonist-paired supersets** (opposing muscle groups, e.g., shoulder press + lat pulldown, leg extension + leg curl, bench press + row, curl + tricep extension) and **peripheral-paired supersets** (unrelated but non-opposing muscle groups, e.g., squat + bench) result in no loss, or much less loss, of force output (ref 6) (p.190). [SCIENCE]
  - Mechanism: while one muscle group works, the other rests — total rest time is shorter, but *local* muscle rest can be as long or longer than in straight sets (p.190-191). Several studies report slightly *more* volume load completed during antagonist-paired supersets of bench/row (refs 7,8) and leg extension/leg curl (ref 9) vs. straight sets (p.191).
  - **Longitudinal evidence**: Burke et al. 2024 and Iverson et al. 2024 (both 2024, longitudinal) found similar hypertrophy between antagonist-paired-superset training and straight-set training, but supersets completed in **half (Iverson) to two-thirds (Burke) the time** (p.191, refs 10, 11). [SCIENCE]
- **The Trade-Off (p.191)**:
  - Cardiorespiratory system gets less rest (higher work-to-rest ratio) → higher perceived exertion. Burke's participants rated a full superset session ~1 point higher on a 10-point RPE scale than a straight-set session (p.191, ref 10). [SCIENCE]
  - Exercise pairing matters: in Iverson's study, pairing lat pulldown with a highly fatiguing lift (leg press) and having the row sometimes come last resulted in slightly less volume and smaller strength gains for those lifts vs. straight sets (p.191-192, ref 11). [SCIENCE]
  - Squat volume can suffer when paired in peripheral supersets with upper-body compounds, per short-term studies (refs 6, 12) (p.192). [SCIENCE]
  - **Rule**: be in good cardiorespiratory shape to use antagonist/peripheral supersets; **lower-body compound exercises should not be supersetted with other compound lifts or large-muscle-group exercises in most cases** — pair them with calf raises, arm training, delt training, etc. (p.192). [CONVENTION]
- **Implementing (p.192)**:
  - Ensure rest between supersets is long enough that cumulative fatigue doesn't hurt performance. Research protocol: superset (both exercises back-to-back) then rest ~2 minutes (p.192).
  - **Practical rule**: alternate sets on each exercise, completing a set of each within a **3-5 minute period**; can add rest between the two exercises within a superset if out of breath, especially early on or with multi-joint lifts (p.192). [CONVENTION]
  - With isolation exercises (e.g., tricep ext + bicep curl, leg ext + leg curl), cardiorespiratory fatigue is lower → rest between supersets can drop to **~1-2 minutes** (p.192). [CONVENTION] No fixed rule — ensure readiness and that reps aren't sharply dropping set-to-set.
  - **Table 5.1** example upper-body antagonist-paired superset session (p.192, figure/table — reproduced): 3 rounds of {Bench Press + Chest-Supported Row} ~2 min rest, {Overhead Press + Lat Pulldown} ~2 min rest, {Tricep Press-downs + Bicep Curls} ~1 min rest.
  - **Crowded gym workaround**: bring dumbbells to a single station — e.g., DB shoulder press seated backward on the lat pulldown machine, or DB rows on the bench press (p.192-193).
- **Role of Agonist-Paired Supersets (p.193)**: despite lower net force output/volume vs. equal straight sets (ref 5), they save time and have two strategic uses:
  1. **Drop-set alternative** — reduce per-set stimulus but save time, potentially offsetting the loss with more total work.
  2. **"Insurance policy" for target muscles** — ensure a specific muscle reaches full fatigue after a compound lift (example: superset incline flyes/cable crossovers after incline press to fully fatigue the clavicular pec rather than letting anterior delts/triceps/sternal pec take over).

### Drop Sets and Rest-Pause Sets (p.193-200)
- Both are "intensity"/"advanced" bodybuilding-labeled techniques the book reframes primarily as **time-saving** techniques (p.193).

**Drop Sets (p.193-194, 198)**
- Definition: reach failure at a given load, drop the load (**typically ~20% in research**), go to failure again, repeat for intended number of drops — extends the set "past failure" (p.193).
- Time savings: an equivalent number of drop sets usually takes **less than half the time** of the same number of straight sets, since rest occurs only between exercises, not between drops (p.193).
- Tradeoff vs. supersets: drop sets **reduce per-set stimulus** (unlike antagonist/peripheral supersets) because: lower load → less force production (can hurt strength gains); smaller pool of active fibers as fatigue builds → slightly less hypertrophy stimulus per set (p.193). [SCIENCE]
- **Quantitative equivalence**: Sødal et al. 2023 systematic review (6 studies, ~140 participants) + meta-analysis of 5 studies: drop-set groups needed **~13 sets on average** to match the hypertrophy of straight-set groups doing **10 sets** (p.194, ref 13). [SCIENCE]
- **Rule of thumb**: initial set + subsequent drop sets count as **75% as many straight sets** — e.g., 3 planned straight sets ≈ 1 initial set + 3 drops (p.194). [CONVENTION] (Note: later restated on p.200-201 as "four drop sets, including the initial set, equivalent to three straight sets" — same ~75% ratio.)
- **Implementing (p.197-198)**: preserve the *program's intended RIR*, not literal to-failure training (a key evidence-based-practice distinction — apply findings conceptually, not literally) (p.197):
  1. Perform the top set exactly as programmed (load, reps, target RIR).
  2. Immediately (no rest) drop load ~20% and go again; repeat for the planned number of drops.
  3. Target roughly the RIR you'd have reached on that straight-set number due to cumulative fatigue (e.g., 2nd/3rd straight set RIR), finishing the final drop at ~1 RIR to maximize the last drop without exceeding the RIR prescription.
  4. Don't over-fixate on exact reps/RIR per drop — exercise, RIR prescription, training history, and individual traits determine this.
  - **Table 5.2** example, converting 3×10 @1-3 RIR straight sets to drop sets (p.198):

    | Straight Sets | Drop Sets |
    |---|---|
    | Set 1: 100 kg × 10 @ 3 RIR, 2 min rest | Top Set: 100 kg × 10 @ 3 RIR, no rest |
    | Set 2: 100 kg × 10 @ 2 RIR, 2 min rest | Drop 1: 80 kg × 10 @ 2 RIR, no rest |
    | Set 3: 100 kg × 10 @ 1 RIR, 2 min rest | Drop 2: 65 kg × 8 @ 1 RIR, no rest |
    | Next Exercise | Drop 3: 52.5 kg × 9 @ 1 RIR, 2 min rest, then Next Exercise |

**Rest-Pause Sets (p.194-195, 198-200)**
- Definition: like drop sets but load stays the same; take very short "mini rests" (**often ~20 seconds**) before more reps at the same weight (p.194).
- Typical research protocol (p.194):
  1. Choose a load.
  2. Train to failure.
  3. Rest ~20 seconds.
  4. Do more reps to failure.
  5. Repeat until a target total rep count or number of rest-pause sets is hit.
- Evidence base is thinner than for drop sets, and fair RCT comparisons are methodologically harder (often failure-vs-non-failure or differing loads) (p.194). [DISPUTED — limited research]
- Available evidence: rest-pause sets promote **similar strength and hypertrophy as straight sets when load, proximity to failure, and volume load are matched** (p.194). [SCIENCE]
- **Practicality limit**: impractical for exercises requiring racking/unracking heavy loads every ~20s (e.g., heavy squats) — even though load is preserved (good on paper for strength), the logistics make it impractical for many lifts, especially for strong lifters (p.194). [CONVENTION] → best suited to upper-body multi-joint lifts, machine exercises, and single-joint exercises (p.199-200).
- **Implementing (p.198-199)**: maintain load, rest 20s, perform more reps stopping in the 1-3 RIR range each rest-pause set, until reaching a target total rep count (e.g., 30 reps for a 3×10 straight-set target).
  - **Table 5.3** example, converting 3×10 @1-3 RIR straight sets to rest-pause sets (p.199):

    | Straight Sets | Rest-Pause Sets |
    |---|---|
    | Set 1: 100 kg × 10 @ 3 RIR, 2 min rest | Top Set: 100 kg × 10 @ 3 RIR (10/30 reps), 20s rest |
    | Set 2: 100 kg × 10 @ 2 RIR, 2 min rest | RP1: 100 kg × 6 @ 2 RIR (16/30), 20s rest |
    | Set 3: 100 kg × 10 @ 1 RIR, 2 min rest | RP2: 100 kg × 5 @ 2 RIR (21/30), 20s rest |
    | Next Exercise | RP3: 100 kg × 4 @ 1 RIR (25/30), 20s rest; RP4: 100 kg × 3 @ 1 RIR (28/30), 20s rest; RP5: 100 kg × 2 @ 1 RIR (30/30), 2 min rest, then Next Exercise |
  - Observations (p.199-200): total rest across the rest-pause series = 100s (vs. 6 min for straight sets) — less time-efficient than drop sets but more efficient than straight sets. Average RIR per rep is much lower with rest-pause: straight sets produced 6 reps in the 1-3 RIR range vs. 13 reps in that range for rest-pause sets. **For the biggest time savings, choose drop sets over rest-pause sets**; expect rest-pause to feel harder and produce more fatigue/soreness, especially unacclimated (p.200). [CONVENTION]
  - Open question: whether being closer to failure on all reps with rest-pause could produce a *greater* stimulus is unresolved by research — "it may, but it probably isn't a major difference if there is one" (p.200). [DISPUTED]

**Comparing Drop Sets, Rest-Pause, and Straight Sets — Example Study (p.194-196)**
- Enes et al. 2021: three matched groups, all achieving similar volume, proximity/past failure, and hypertrophy (light spotting used to ensure completion) (p.195-196, ref 14):
  1. Straight Sets: 4×12 @ 70% 1RM, 2 min inter-set rest.
  2. Rest-Pause Sets: 3×10 + 6×75% 1RM (after each set of 10, a rest-pause set of 6; 20s rest before rest-pause sets, 2 min inter-set rest).
  3. Drop Sets: 3×10×75% 1RM + 6×55% 1RM (drop to 55% with no rest after each set of 10 for 6 reps; 2 min inter-set rest).
- All three groups (with equal inter-set rest by design, unlike real-world use) achieved similar hypertrophy, but **strength gains were highest in the rest-pause group**, which did the most reps at the heaviest load (75% 1RM) (p.196). [SCIENCE]
- Conclusion drawn: "nothing magical" about either technique — matching volume load and proximity to failure yields similar hypertrophy to straight sets; using a higher load may yield more strength (p.196).

**The Role of Training Dose (p.196-197)**
- Longo et al. 2022 single-leg leg press study, 80% 1RM, four conditions across paired legs (p.196-197, ref 3):
  1. 3 sets to failure, 3 min inter-set rest (leg A).
  2. As many sets to failure as needed on the paired leg to match leg A's volume, 1 min inter-set rest.
  3. 3 sets to failure, 1 min inter-set rest (leg B).
  4. As many sets to failure as needed on the paired leg to match leg B's volume, 3 min inter-set rest.
- Hypertrophy was equal within condition-1-vs-2 pairs and within condition-3-vs-4 pairs (matched volume + matched failure training → matched hypertrophy, regardless of rest structure) (p.197). [SCIENCE]
- But conditions 1&2 completed **~40% more volume load** than conditions 3&4 because the 1-minute rest in condition 3 constrained volume — and conditions 1&2 achieved **twice the quadriceps hypertrophy** of conditions 3&4 (p.197). [SCIENCE]
- **Governing principle**: training dose dictates adaptation; whenever rest interval or set structure changes the *training dose* (volume actually achieved), adaptation changes accordingly (p.197).

**Practical Considerations for Drop/Rest-Pause Sets (p.196-197)**
- **Experience matters**: not truly "advanced" techniques, but require accurate proximity-to-failure judgment, especially at high reps; novices tend to underestimate closeness to failure, blunting the effect (p.196). [CONVENTION]
- **Load selection**: research often uses moderate-to-heavy loads (~80% 1RM), where full failure isn't required for the set to "count" toward hypertrophy (ref 15), though sets likely need **≥4-5 reps** (refs 16-18) (p.196). Perceived exertion/discomfort is higher for high-rep (>12) failure sets than moderate-rep sets (ref 19), which is likely why judging proximity-to-failure is harder at high reps (ref 20) (p.196-197). [SCIENCE]
- **Fatigue management**: these techniques raise cardiorespiratory fatigue, especially combined with supersets, making it harder to reach the needed effort level (p.197).
- **Motivation helps**: research settings use verbal motivation from researchers (absent in real-world training), making the intended proximity-to-failure harder to reach when extending "past failure" with drop/rest-pause sets; experienced lifters can likely push through, but novices may inadvertently train further from failure than intended — a training partner's motivation can help (p.197). [CONVENTION]

## Rest Period Recommendations (p.200-201)
- **Strength, main lifts**: rest until ready to perform your best — **for most, at least 2 minutes**, often longer if the lift uses a lot of muscle mass, involves high absolute loads, or cardiorespiratory conditioning is limiting (p.200). [CONVENTION]
  - Signal for insufficient rest: a large drop-off in load or reps per set. Even with 3-5+ minutes rest, some drop-off is normal; anecdotally, **reps or RIR should only decrease ~1-2 set-to-set** when training at 1-3 RIR in moderate-to-low rep ranges with multi-joint lifts (p.200). [CONVENTION, anecdotal per book]
- **Hypertrophy**: larger reductions in reps/RIR/load across sets are acceptable (goal is muscle stimulation, not maximal load) — but there's a point of insufficient recovery to recruit/stimulate enough fibers to make the next set worthwhile. **Rest at least 90 seconds between sets on average**; strong lifters and lower-body multi-joint lifts often need more (p.200). [CONVENTION]
- **If time-pressed or using high volumes**, use time-saving techniques (p.200):
  - **Antagonist/peripheral-paired supersets**: cut training time by roughly a third or more; rest at least 2 minutes between supersets that include a multi-joint lift; only pair lower-body multi-joint lifts with isolation work.
  - **Drop sets**: most time-efficient; count **four drop sets (including the initial set) as equivalent to three straight sets**.
  - **Rest-pause sets**: sit between supersets and drop sets for time savings; impractical for heavy squats/demanding multi-joint lifts, especially for strong lifters; expect higher fatigue than straight sets from performing more reps close to failure.
