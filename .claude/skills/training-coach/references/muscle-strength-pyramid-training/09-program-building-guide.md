# MSP Training — Guide to Program Building

> **Source:** *The Muscle & Strength Pyramid: Training* 3e (Helms/Valdez/Morgan), book pp.223-254. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/msp-training.txt` or PDF pp.223-254.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] research-established · [CONVENTION] the authors' specific evidence-based recommendation · [DISPUTED] contested / authors flag uncertainty.

## Overview (p.223)
This chapter is the practical synthesis of all prior Pyramid levels into a 6-step program-building sequence: (1) adherence/split, (2) volume-intensity-frequency, (3) progression, (4) exercise selection, (5-6) rest periods and tempo — followed by warm-up guidance and dual-athlete (strength + physique) programming. [CONVENTION] The authors explicitly frame their companion Sample Programs spreadsheet as illustrative blueprints, not "the" prescribed program — customization is expected (p.223).

## Step 1: Adherence — choosing training days and split (p.223-229)

**Decision order:**
1. Choose a realistic weekly training-day count that fits life/schedule — anywhere from 2+ days/week [CONVENTION] (p.223).
2. Decide fewer-longer vs. more-frequent-shorter sessions.
3. [SCIENCE] Training experience interacts with this: past a certain point, progress (not maintenance) is difficult without training a muscle/lift at least 3x/week; for advanced lifters a 2-day/week schedule is rarely practical because required loads/volumes make each session an ordeal (p.223).
4. Pick a split from the frequency matrices below.

### Frequency splits for powerlifting (p.223-225)
[CONVENTION] Rule: any movement that trains the same muscles as a main lift but isn't the lift itself adds 0.5 to that lift's weekly frequency (e.g., a squat session also contributes 0.5 to weekly deadlift frequency, and vice versa) (p.223).
[SCIENCE] Ideal is training each main lift ≥2x/week; if only 2 days/week are available, both days must include all three main lifts, which many find infeasible — hence the matrix also offers 1.5-2x/week options (p.223-224).
[CONVENTION] Anecdotally squats create less next-day soreness/discomfort than deadlifts, so the sample splits lean toward higher squat frequency (reversible per individual) (p.224).

### Frequency splits for bodybuilding (p.225-227)
[SCIENCE] Unlike strength, muscle-group frequency has a very small impact on hypertrophy outcomes — so hypertrophy splits have far more design freedom (p.225).
[CONVENTION] Split-design goal: avoid sessions that are logistically impractical, overly fatiguing, demotivating, or that exceed ~11 sets/muscle group/session (p.225).
[CONVENTION] The authors' own Sample Programs use a non-traditional split (Chest & Back w/ Quads & Calves; Arms & Middle/Rear Delts w/ Glutes & Hamstrings) rather than classic Upper/Lower, Full Body, or Body Part splits — offered as *additional* options, not because traditional splits are inferior (p.225). [DISPUTED/opinion] In the authors' experience, traditional splits (body-part splits, lower/push/pull) are often not ideal for balancing recovery and stimulus at higher volumes, so more creative arrangements matter more as volume/training status increases (p.225).
[SCIENCE] Because of indirect (fractional) work, even "train each muscle once/week" classical splits rarely produce a true frequency of exactly 1 (e.g., bench indirectly adds 0.5 triceps frequency) (p.225).

**Session-type descriptions (p.227-229):**
- **Muscle Group Sessions** — 1-2 muscle groups/session; allows high per-session muscle volume without excessive length; needs longer recovery → lower weekly frequency; best for low-moderate weekly volumes (~4-12 sets); less effective for higher volumes unless combined with other session types (p.227).
- **Upper Push/Pull Sessions** — the "Legs/Push/Pull" split's upper days; train all pushing (chest, triceps, anterior delts) or pulling (back, biceps, rear delts) muscles, with one day dedicated to middle delts; supports low-moderate volumes at 1x/week or moderate-high (~12-24 sets) at higher frequency/combined with other types (p.227).
- **Upper or Lower Sessions** — entire upper or lower body together; moderate volume without excess length; low-moderate at 1x/week, moderate-moderately-high (~12-18 sets) at 2x/week (e.g., 4-day Upper/Lower); higher volumes possible at 6x/week or combined with other types. Note: lower-body sessions are disproportionately taxing due to load/muscle-group count — non-traditional approaches may suit very high lower-body volume seekers better (p.227-228).
- **Full Body Sessions** — train the whole body to some degree each session; strict full-body limits per-session muscle volume unless sessions get very long; works for low-moderate volume at 2-3x/week, moderate-moderately-high (~12-18 sets) at 3+x/week, higher at 5+ sessions/week with careful programming (p.228). Because per-session muscle volume is low, consecutive-day training of the same muscle can occur without hurting recovery if RIR, muscle length, and exercise selection manage overlap/fatigue (p.228).
- **Full Push or Pull Sessions** — combine an upper push/pull session with lower-body work (push days add quads/calves; pull days add glutes/hamstrings); a non-strict "full body" interpretation, avoiding a full leg day so sessions don't become too long/demanding; good for those training consecutive days or sensitive to prior-day arm-training bleed into chest/back performance. Supports: low volume at 2x/week with shorter sessions; moderately low-moderately high at 3-4x/week; very high at 5+x/week with moderate session length (p.228).

**Key programming note (p.228-229):** at equal total weekly volume, the split determines per-session work and thus session length — 6 full-body days → fewer exercises/sets per muscle per session, shorter sessions; 2 full-body days → more exercises/sets per muscle, longer sessions. In the sample programs, higher weekly volumes pair with higher frequencies (p.229).

[SCIENCE] For strength, the frequency matrix organizes around bench-press frequency and squat+deadlift combined frequency (due to their stimulus/stress overlap); very high or very low frequencies are both problematic — overload sessions cause performance drop-off, while excessive frequency may cause joint/connective-tissue stress regardless of total volume (p.229).

[CONVENTION] For ~90% of lifters: train 3-5 days/week with a muscle/movement frequency of 2-4x/week — this typically best balances stimulus and recovery (p.229).

## Step 2: Volume, intensity, frequency (p.230-241)

**Table 7.3 — Volume/Intensity/Frequency starting recommendations** (p.230):

| | Hypertrophy | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| **Minimum effective dose** | 4 sets/muscle/wk, increases w/ training status [SCIENCE: lowest volume producing measurable gains in 2-3 months; for trained lifters requires near/at-failure RIR; produces progress in novice-intermediate, maintenance in intermediate-advanced] | 1 set/lift/wk, increases w/ training status [same logic; requires high specificity — high load, on the specific lift] |
| **Maximum effective dose** | ~30 sets/muscle/wk [SCIENCE: threshold where diminishing returns plateau] | ~5 sets/lift/wk (short-term) |
| **Practical effective dose** | 10-20 sets/muscle/wk; higher with specialization [CONVENTION: real-world/long-term range; individual differences dictate placement; near-max volumes only advised for advanced lifters via specialization cycles] | 3-5 sets/lift/wk (short-term), 5-10 sets/muscle/wk (long-term) [CONVENTION: long-term strength gains require hypertrophy, so some hypertrophy-range volume on main-lift muscles is recommended] |
| **Load** | ~4-30RM (~30-90% 1RM) | 1-8RM (~80+% 1RM) |
| **RIR** | 4-0 RIR and failure per load/rep combo | ~7-0 RIR per load/rep combo [load, not RIR, dictates strength; RIR is a function of load — higher loads inherently allow fewer reps and land closer to failure] |
| **Rep/RIR detail (hypertrophy)** | 4-6 reps: 4-0 RIR; 6-8 reps: 3 RIR-to-failure; 8-12 reps: 2 RIR-to-failure; >12 reps: 1 RIR-to-failure | — |
| **Frequency** | ≥1x/muscle/wk based on volume; if >11 weekly sets/muscle-group, raise frequency so no session exceeds 11 fractional sets/muscle | 2-6x/lift/wk based on volume; higher frequency provides some benefit; spread sets over as many days as possible, 1-2 direct sets/main lift/session |

Footnoted caveats (p.230): fractional-set counting rule (hypertrophy: primary=1 set, secondary=0.5; strength: tested lift=1, other lifts sharing muscles=0.5). [SCIENCE] RIR underestimation is more likely at low loads; high loads can be effective for hypertrophy at high RIR but sets must be ≥4 reps. [SCIENCE] High volume is best distributed with high frequency for hypertrophy; increased frequency benefits strength via enhanced skill practice/movement quality.

### Determining volume (p.231-232)
"Optimal" volume is goal- and circumstance-dependent [CONVENTION]:
- Minimal-time hypertrophy goal → optimal may mean lowest volume still producing good returns.
- Competitive-bodybuilder goal → optimal may mean maximum achievable volume for fastest gain rate.
- Use personal training-log history of progress-rate-by-volume if available; otherwise start tracking now and adjust in future cycles (p.231).

**Table 7.4 — Hypertrophy volume tiers by time commitment, per-set stimulus, and total stimulus** (p.232):

| Weekly sets/muscle | Time commitment | Avg. stimulus/set | Total stimulus |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4-8 | Minimal (~1-2.5h) | Highest | Lowest (~25-45% of highest tier) |
| 9-12 | Low (~3-4.5h) | High | Modest (~45-60%) |
| 13-16 | Medium (~5-6.5h) | Medium | Medium (~60-70%) |
| 17-20 | High (~7-8.5h) | Low | High (~70-85%) |
| 21-30 | Very High (~9+h) | Lowest | Highest (~85-100% if sustainable) |

[CONVENTION] 21-30 sets/muscle/wk is often best accomplished via specialization cycles, with other muscle groups held in a lower-volume tier. [SCIENCE/caveat] At the top tier, if RIR increases, technical execution degrades, effective exercises get dropped, or deloads become frequent (net volume falls), overall realized stimulus may not actually be highest (p.232).

### Determining frequency (p.231-233)
[SCIENCE] Higher weekly volume → more sense to spread over more sessions: prevents excessively long/fatiguing sessions, maintains session quality, increases skill practice for strength goals (p.231).

**Table 7.5 — Frequency recommendations** (p.232): Hypertrophy: ≥1x/wk/muscle group based on volume — if session exceeds ~11 fractional sets for a muscle, increase frequency to redistribute. Strength: 2-6x/wk/lift depending on volume, spreading sets across as many sessions as possible, 1-2 direct sets/lift/session.

**Table 7.6 — Frequency recommendations by volume** (p.232):

| Weekly fractional sets/muscle | Weekly frequency/muscle |
|---|---|
| 4-10 | 1-2 |
| 11-20 | 2-3 |
| 21-30 | 3+ |

[CONVENTION] If volume doesn't fit the Step-1 split, either adjust total volume or adjust split choice (p.232).

**Hypertrophy volume distribution worked example (p.233):** Goal 13-15 sets/muscle/wk, 4-day split (Lower / Upper Push / Upper Pull / Full Body), 2x/wk muscle frequency → ~4-6 sets/muscle on the Full Body day, leaving 9-11 sets spread across Legs/Push/Pull days.

**Strength distribution considerations (p.233):** Secondary work follows the same volume/frequency logic as hypertrophy. Main lifts carry lower total volume but need relatively high frequency to drive faster gains via frequent practice. [CONVENTION] Avoid interference: don't schedule heavy secondary work for a muscle group the day immediately before that muscle's main-lift training (e.g., avoid heavy quad accessory work before squat day, heavy pressing accessory work before bench day) (p.233).

### Determining intensity (p.234-235)
[CONVENTION] The chart below (Table 7.7) adds specificity beyond Table 7.3 by considering exercise selection — e.g., 0 RIR can suit low-rep/high-load hypertrophy work generally but is not ideal for free-weight compound lower-body/upper-pressing exercises (safety) or compound machine exercises (fatigue management) (p.234). Similarly, 4-6 rep hypertrophy work is usable but the resistance profile of most back exercises and many isolation exercises makes form-maintenance hard at that load — better reserved for compounds, especially for those prone to joint discomfort/prior injury (p.234).

**Table 7.7 — Rep and RPE/RIR range combinations for hypertrophy by exercise type** (p.234):

| Exercise type | Rep range | RPE/RIR range |
|---|---|---|
| Lower free-weight compound (squat, deadlift, RDL) | 4-8 | 6-8 RPE / 4-2 RIR |
| Lower machine compound or upper free-weight pressing (leg press, hack squat, OHP, bench, incline DB) | 4-12 | 6-9 RPE / 4-1 RIR |
| Upper machine pressing or pulling compound (machine chest/shoulder press, lat pulldown, BB row, cable row) | 6-15 | 7-10 RPE / 3-0 RIR and failure |
| Isolation (curls, tricep pushdown, calf raise, leg extension, lateral raise) | 8-20 | 8-10 RPE / 2-0 RIR and failure |

[SCIENCE] Rationale: for hypertrophy/secondary strength work, the higher the fatigue generated and technical demand, the greater the safety risk — so it makes sense to train further from failure with higher load to preserve full per-set stimulus, avoid fatigue bleed into the rest of the session, possibly reduce injury risk, and keep RPE ratings accurate (RPE is harder to rate accurately on high-rep, fatiguing movements). Compound movements are the better vehicle for lower-rep, farther-from-failure (heavier) volume; isolation/machine exercises are the better vehicle for higher-rep, lower-load, closer-to-failure volume (p.235).

[DISPUTED/no-fixed-recipe] For main strength lifts, load is the driving adaptation force, so set/rep/load/RPE combinations depend entirely on the goal for that lift on that day and training phase — there is no fixed "cheat sheet" (e.g., a single at 5 RPE/5 RIR 16 weeks from a meet to maintain skill with minimal fatigue vs. the same during a taper to hold peak strength while dissipating fatigue). Repetition/RPE combos for main lifts are ultimately dictated by specificity and proximity to peaking, with load dictating RPE/RIR (p.235).

## Step 3: Progression (p.235-241)

### Implementing rep-range variation and specialization phases (p.235-237)
[SCIENCE/CONVENTION] For hypertrophy, three needs: (1) variation in rep ranges, (2) a plan to manage unwieldy high volumes for advanced lifters offsetting the natural slowdown in hypertrophy rate, (3) a progression system keeping pace with adaptation to maintain each set's effectiveness (p.235).

**Step order:** decide how rep-range variation will be incorporated — options are within-session, between-session, between-week, or between-block (p.235-236).

**Table 7.8 — Hypertrophy training structures to achieve rep-range variation** (p.236), 4-day Lower A/Upper A/Lower B/Upper B example:
- **Within-Session Variation:** each day runs 4-8, 6-10, 10-15, 15-20 rep ranges varying lift to lift.
- **Between-Session Variation:** Lower A / Upper A use 4-6, 6-10, 8-12 on all lifts; Lower B / Upper B use 8-12, 10-15, 15-20 on all lifts.
- **Alternating Week Variation:** Week A uses 4-6/6-10/8-12 on all days; Week B uses 8-12/10-15/15-20 on all days.
- **Alternating Mesocycle Variation:** Meso A uses 4-6/6-10/8-12 all weeks; Meso B uses 8-12/10-15/15-20 all weeks.

**Table 7.9 — Muscle group specialization phase mesocycles** (p.236) [CONVENTION, advanced lifters only]: Balanced Phase (8-12 wks, 10-20 sets/wk all muscle groups) → Specialization Phase (8-12 wks, 20-30 sets/wk on 1-2 muscle groups, 5-15 sets/wk on remaining) → Balanced Phase (8-12 wks, 10-20 sets/wk all) → Specialization Phase (8-12 wks, 20-30 on 1-2, 5-15 on remaining) → Balanced Phase (8-12 wks, 10-20 sets/wk all).

### Autoregulated double progression and phasic approaches (p.237-239)

**Table 7.10 — Example autoregulated double progression, intermediate lifter, 3×10-15 @ 0-2 RIR** (p.237):

| Session | Load | Reps | First-set RIR |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 40 lbs | 12, 12, 11 | 2 |
| 2 | 40 lbs | 13, 13, 12 | 2 |
| 3 | 40 lbs | 14, 14, 12 | 2 |
| 4 | 40 lbs | 15, 14, 14 | 1 |
| 5 | 40 lbs | 15, 14, 14 | 2 |
| 6 | 45 lbs | 11, 10, 9 | 2 |

[CONVENTION] Rule embedded in the example: increase load once the top of the rep target is reached (or reps plateau/RIR tightens at target reps), then reps drop back down at the new load and the cycle repeats.

[CONVENTION] For strength programs, a **phasic approach** is recommended: alternate volume and load phases, add peak phases as needed pre-test/competition, since strength programs must both manage fatigue/specificity to peak short-term *and* build hypertrophy long-term to raise strength ceiling. Secondary lifts use autoregulated double progression exactly as for hypertrophy; main lifts' rep range, RIR/RPE, volume, and frequency are phase-dependent (p.237-238).

**Table 7.11 — Volume phase summary** (p.238):
- Goal: build muscle; maintain or build strength.
- Main lifts: 1-3 singles/lift/wk; progress from 5 to 8 RPE (5 to 2 RIR).
- Secondary lifts: 10-20 sets/wk per main-lift muscle group; mostly 6-20 rep range at 0-3 RIR; autoregulated double progression; primarily non-specific, low-demand exercises.
- Length: 6-12 weeks per timeline.

**Table 7.12 — Load phase summary** (p.238):
- Goal: build strength; maintain/build muscle.
- Main lifts: 2-4 singles/lift/wk; progress from 6 to 9 RPE (4 to 1 RIR); 2 back-off sets/single at 5-8 RPE (5-2 RIR); progress from 5 to 3 reps (~80-85% 1RM); use variations if needed.
- Secondary lifts: 5-10 sets/wk/main-lift muscle group, mostly 6-20 reps at 0-3 RIR; autoregulated double progression, non-specific/low-demand exercises.
- Length: 4-8 weeks; gradually reduce secondary volume by 1-2 sets every 1-2 weeks (e.g., 10 sets wks 1-2, 9 sets wks 3-4, 8 sets wks 5-6, 6 sets wks 7-8).

**Table 7.13 — Peak phase summary** (p.239):
- Goal: peak strength, maintain muscle.
- Main lifts: 2-5 singles/each/wk at 7-10 RPE (3-0 RIR); 0-3 back-off sets/single at 5-8 RPE (5-2 RIR); if 3-4 weeks, progress from 4 to 2 reps/set (~85% 1RM); use variations if needed.
- Secondary lifts: 0-4 sets/wk/main-lift muscle group, mostly 6-20 reps at 0-3 RIR; autoregulated double progression, non-specific/low-demand.
- Length: 2-4 weeks — 2 weeks if volume+load phase totaled 10-12 wks, 3 weeks if 13-15 wks, 4 weeks if 16+ wks; decrease back-off-set and secondary volume weekly.

### When progress plateaus (p.239-241)

**Table 7.14 — Training status definitions and progress rates** (p.239-240):
- **Novice:** able to add load/reps every time a lift repeats in the same week or week-to-week; phase can last years, or as little as 6 months with consistent progressive training, sufficient recovery and effort.
- **Intermediate:** progress slows — may add reps in the 10-20 rep range week-to-week, or add load in lower rep ranges month-to-month; most reach this stage by year 1 and may remain through years 4-5; many never progress past it.
- **Advanced:** gains much slower — a rep or two in the 10-20 range month-to-month, or small load/rep increases in lower rep ranges over longer timeframes; progress gets harder near muscular potential; most lifters never reach this phase.

**Plateau flowchart (Figure 7.1, p.240-241):**
1. Ask: are you plateaued (not making training-status-appropriate gym progress)?
   - **No** → don't change anything.
   - **Yes** → ask: is the answer "no" to any of: sleeping 7+ hours? not in an energy deficit? protein ≥0.7g/lb (≥1.6g/kg)? neither over- nor under-estimating RPE? training muscles/lifts min. 2x/wk (strength goals)? exercise selection/ROM/tempo appropriate and consistent?
     - **Yes** (i.e., something's off) → fix that, run another mesocycle, reassess.
     - **No, I've taken care of all of those** → ask: are you recovering? (dreading the gym? worse sleep quality? load/reps actually decreasing? worse life stress? worse aches/pains? — if no to all or all-but-one → likely recovering; if yes to 2+ → likely not recovering.)
       - **Recovering (yes)** → it may be time to increase your training dose.
       - **Not recovering (no)** → temporarily reduce volume / take a light week. If that fixes it, good. If you quickly re-fatigue and stall again, that likely means you need more than a deload/taper — consider less volume or reorganizing it to better manage fatigue.

**Table 7.15 — Weekly self-assessment after training + days off** (p.241), Y/N per question: dreading the gym? sleep worse than normal? loads/reps at same load decreasing from last cycle? stress worse than normal? aches/pain worse than normal?
[CONVENTION] Scoring rule: yes to 0-1 questions → carry on; yes to 2+ questions → consider a deload.

## Step 4: Exercise selection (p.241-243)

**Table 7.16 — Exercise selection recommendations summary** (p.242):
- **Strength:** use main lifts to hit target volume for those lifts unless tolerance for their volume is low — then select high-carryover variations based on biomechanics, injury history, technical needs, and/or sticking points. For hypertrophy (secondary) work, choose less fatiguing, higher-rep exercises that are quicker to set up/perform.
- **Hypertrophy:** following feasibility and long-muscle-length training/challenge principles, choose 1-3 lifts for each of the six core movement patterns, including: an incline press; an elbow-flared row plus a shoulder-extension-dominant row; at least one isolation exercise each for quads, hamstrings, biceps, triceps, middle delts, and calves.

[CONVENTION] Fractional-set counting reminder for exercise selection: hypertrophy sets = 1 for primary muscle groups, 0.5 for secondary; strength sets of the main lift = 1 for that lift, all other lifts sharing muscles = 0.5. This is a tracking tool, not a literal stimulus measure — exercises vary in effectiveness for growing given muscles or transferring to a given lift (p.242).

## Steps 5-6: Rest periods and tempo (p.243-244)
[CONVENTION] Set rest periods appropriate to the goal on each lift, and match tempo to the intended training effect. Consider antagonist-paired sets, peripheral-paired sets, drop sets, or rest-pause techniques to save time — use only with suitable exercise combinations and adequate cardiorespiratory fitness (p.243-244).

## Warming up (p.244-247)

[SCIENCE] Purpose: prepare for training, potentially enhance performance, and possibly reduce injury risk, primarily via raised body temperature — which increases muscle blood flow/oxygen availability and neuromuscular speed/sensitivity [ref 1] (p.244).

### Does stretching have a role? (p.244-246)
- [SCIENCE] Static stretching that acutely increases flexibility just before training can reduce strength and power [ref 2] (p.244).
- [DISPUTED] Some argue this short-term performance cost is worth it if stretching cuts injury risk. Warm-ups in general (including any stretching type) do reduce injury risk [ref 3], but a 2025 meta-analysis (15 trials, >9,000 participants) found no independent effect of stretching alone [ref 4] (p.244).
- [SCIENCE] Dynamic stretching (active bodyweight stretching drills) can slightly increase muscular performance [ref 2] (p.244).
- [CONVENTION] Static stretching remains useful for a targeted flexibility need at a specific joint that's hard to achieve with dynamic work alone (e.g., tight calves limiting squat depth without heel rise/butt wink) (p.244-245).
- [SCIENCE] Combining static + dynamic stretching, with dynamic performed last, also improves performance [ref 2] — useful for high-mobility-demand athletes (weightlifters needing snatch/clean&jerk depth; powerlifters needing pain-free bar position, e.g., tight pecs/shoulders in low-bar squat or tight triceps/forearms in clean-grip front squat). So long as stretching ends with a dynamic approach, no harm and likely small benefit (p.245).

**Practical rules (p.245):**
- Flexibility needed in a muscle *not* being trained (e.g., pecs/delts during low-bar squats) → static stretch freely; reduced contractile force doesn't matter there.
- Flexibility needed in a muscle *being* trained:
  - Minor ROM requirement → brief static stretching (<60s), not to discomfort, is fine but won't meaningfully increase flexibility.
  - Greater need → follow static stretching with dynamic stretching, or use an extended lift-specific warm-up gradually increasing ROM each rep.
  - [SCIENCE] Foam rolling ~60s at moderate pressure can also increase ROM and, like dynamic stretching, may slightly boost performance [ref 5] (p.245).

**Lifting as stretching (p.245-246):** [SCIENCE] Full-ROM lifting is essentially loaded dynamic stretching; a 2023 meta-analysis (55 studies, 2,756 participants) found no significant difference in ROM improvement between resistance training and stretching [ref 6]. Key technique: perform warm-up sets with gradually increasing load until desired ROM is reached, rather than jumping to heavy sets before mobility suffices (which risks compensation at other joints) (p.245-246).
[CONVENTION] Exception: skill-dependent lifts where limited mobility makes the movement literally impossible even lightly loaded (cleans, snatches, overhead squats, clean-grip front squats, squat jerks) — here static/dynamic stretching, dowel-based warm-ups, or foam rolling should precede warm-up sets, since moving out of position ingrains poor technique (p.246). In competition, foam rolling/static/certain dynamic stretches may be preferable to lifting for improving ROM, since this reduces the warm-up sets needed and preserves strength/minimizes fatigue (p.246).

### Optimizing the warm-up for performance (p.246-247)
[SCIENCE] Traditional general (light aerobic) → sport-specific (dynamic stretching + warm-up sets) sequence is only necessary if that's what it takes to get warm. Dynamic stretching alone consistently improves performance at ~7-10 minutes duration [ref 2], likely by raising temperature sufficiently. If warming up slowly or training in a cold environment, add 5-10 minutes of low-intensity aerobic activity beforehand (p.246).
[CONVENTION] If skipping the general aerobic warm-up, the dynamic warm-up should be full-body and take 7-10 minutes (p.246).
[CONVENTION] For strength/physique athletes, warm-up *sets* are the "sport-specific" portion: do more warm-up sets on the first exercise per muscle group; a single warm-up set often suffices for subsequent free-weight exercises for that muscle; machines may need none. A warm-up could consist purely of these sport-specific sets, depending on individual factors (time of day, climate, gym environment, DOMS, preference) (p.246-247).
[SCIENCE] Specific-warmup research is mixed — some studies find no improvement [refs 7,8,9], others find slight gains in velocity, total work, or reps-to-failure, most consistently after 1-2 warm-up sets with a moderate load (60-80% of working weight) [refs 10,11] (p.247).

**Post-activation performance enhancement (PAPE) (p.247):** [SCIENCE] Acute improvement in sprint/jump/resistance performance 5-15 minutes after a heavy set (e.g., squats for several reps ≥80% 1RM). A 2025 systematic review (Botton) on reps-to-failure in squat/bench/leg press found enhancement in 6 of 9 studies, ~1-7 extra reps when an effect was observed, though usually only ~1-2 reps [ref 12]. A broader 2025 meta-analysis (Xu et al., 62 studies, 1,039 participants) found PAPE can produce small athletic-performance improvements, though effects likely overlap with general warm-up effects [ref 13] (p.247).
[DISPUTED] Underlying PAPE mechanism still under investigation — may act independent of warm-up by enhancing neuromuscular/metabolic/energetic conditions or psychological readiness [refs 13,14] (p.247).
[CONVENTION] Protocols most consistently enhancing back-off-set performance: 1-2 sets of 1-3 reps at ~85-90% 1RM [ref 12] — noted to match the singles already used in the book's Strength Progression System, likely giving a slight boost to subsequent sets (p.247).

**Table 7.18 — Warm-up summary guidelines** (p.247):

| Component | Description |
|---|---|
| General | Optional — 5 minutes of light aerobic activity. |
| Dynamic | Optional — 3-5 min (7-10 min if no general warm-up) of active stretching drills, gradually increasing ROM and force. |

## Dual athletes (p.247-251)
Applies to athletes competing in both strength and physique sports (same season or, more often, separate phases), and to non-competitive "powerbuilders."

### Non-competitive "powerbuilders" (p.247-248)
[DISPUTED] Claim: training strength and hypertrophy simultaneously dilutes effort/stimulus-to-fatigue ratio, slowing progress and capping potential in both. [CONVENTION/rebuttal] Not entirely false, but almost never a concern for non-competitors and only relevant near peak potential — most non-competitors never train hard enough or long enough to push recoverable limits, so any theoretical inefficiency stays theoretical; logistics and life, not training-method dilution, are the real bottleneck for most lifters (p.247-248). No objective health/performance/general-fitness benefit exists to being at 80% vs. 99% of peak strength potential (p.248).
[CONVENTION] Recommendation for self-identified "powerbuilders": follow the strength training guidelines, using the higher end of non-specific (secondary) volumes and the lower end of main-lift volumes (p.248).

### Physique athlete who competes in strength sport (p.248-250)
[CONVENTION] Default approach = Hypertrophy Progression System with additions.
- When in a bodybuilding-focused phase (offseason or prep) with no meet in the next 3-4 months: add 1-2 main-lift singles/week; start some sessions by working up to a single at 5-8 RPE to maintain proficiency (p.248).
- If limb proportions/height/injury resilience allow higher main-lift volume without recovery issues, incorporate main lifts or close variations directly into hypertrophy work:
  - Powerlifters: squat, bench, and/or deadlift or close variations (high-bar squat, close-grip bench, RDL) in the 4-8 rep range.
  - Weightlifters: front squats, OHP, clean pulls, snatch-grip deadlifts with controlled eccentrics, 4-8 rep range.
  - Strongmen/women: squat variations, OHP variations, conventional deadlifts, walking lunges, with higher reps as appropriate to target events (p.249).
- If not well-suited to long hypertrophy phases with these demanding lifts, stick to main-lift singles and wait until 3-4 months out from the meet to shift to specific strength work (p.249).
- At 3-4 months out, switch to the Strength Progression System with modifications: keep hypertrophy work for all muscle groups when selecting secondary lifts (e.g., a powerlifting bodybuilder still includes direct biceps/middle-rear delt/calf work); the volume phase can be shortened or skipped if hypertrophy work on main lifts/high-transfer variations was already occurring (start with the load phase instead); if bodybuilding training only included singles, include a shortened volume phase with more secondary exercises providing main-lift transfer (p.250).
- Outside contest prep, run the peak phase as written — any small size loss is quickly regained. If the meet falls during contest prep, maintain strength-phase secondary-lift volume into the peak phase and keep low-volume hypertrophy work for all muscles (greater muscle-loss risk while dieting); main-lift single/back-off-set frequency and volume will likely need reducing to accommodate recovery while dieting (p.250).

### Strength athlete who competes in physique sport (p.250)
[CONVENTION] Default approach = Strength Progression System with minor modifications: include hypertrophy work for all muscle groups (not just competition-lift-relevant ones) during volume and strength phases. When no meet is planned in the next 3-4 months and dieting for a physique contest, shift to the Hypertrophy Progression System with several added main-lift singles to keep technical proficiency sharp while focusing training on hypertrophy. [SCIENCE] Even in a calorie deficit, muscle gains in certain muscle groups are often achievable at lower training status despite dieting (p.250).

### The true dual athlete (p.250-251)
[CONVENTION] For those placing equal importance on both sports: in the strength-sport offseason while actively competing in strength sport, follow the "Strength Athlete Who Competes in Physique Sport" approach. Once 3-6 months out from starting bodybuilding contest prep — especially with a meet scheduled during prep — switch to the "Physique Athlete Who Competes in Strength Sport" approach, to maintain strength while prioritizing physique goals and managing dieting recovery demands (p.251).
