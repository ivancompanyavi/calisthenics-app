# MSP Training — Level 3 (part 2) — Progression Systems & Plateaus

> **Source:** *The Muscle & Strength Pyramid: Training* 3e (Helms/Valdez/Morgan), book pp.115-144. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/msp-training.txt` or PDF pp.115-144.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] research-established · [CONVENTION] the authors' specific evidence-based recommendation · [DISPUTED] contested / authors flag uncertainty.

## Strength Progression System — overview (p.115)
- A three-mesocycle framework integrating progressive overload, specificity, fatigue management, variation, and linearity: **Volume Phase → Load Phase → (optional) Peak Phase**. [CONVENTION]
- Every session in every phase begins by working up to a **single (1-rep set) at 5–10 RPE / 0–5 RIR** on the main lift(s) being prioritized for strength — typically ≤5 main lifts for a general strength enthusiast, or squat/bench/deadlift for a powerlifter. [CONVENTION] (p.115)
  - Purpose of the daily single: (a) specific heavy-load practice, (b) drives strength, (c) lets you continuously estimate 1RM to track progress (p.115).
- Depending on phase, there may also be **back-off sets** of 2–5 reps on the main lift/close variation, and **secondary lifts** to build/maintain muscle mass (p.115).
- Estimating 1RM: use a 1RM calculator (rep/RIR based, e.g. 100 kg × 4 reps if done at 7 RPE/3RIR = counted rep + 3 reserve reps) consistently — absolute accuracy across calculators doesn't matter, only within-calculator consistency for tracking trend (p.116). [CONVENTION]

## Volume Phase (p.116-117)
- **Goal:** build muscle in the musculature used by main lifts (foundation for later strength gains); maintain or slightly increase main-lift strength; heavy main-lift work is minimized to leave recovery capacity for high hypertrophy volume. [CONVENTION] (p.116)
- **Main lifts:** 1–3 singles/lift/week; RPE progresses gradually across the phase from **5 RPE (5 RIR) toward 8 RPE (2 RIR)**, microcycle to microcycle. Rationale: frequent heavy/specific exposure without excess fatigue accumulation (p.116). [CONVENTION]
  - Expected average estimated-1RM trend over the phase: **novice — increase week to week; intermediate — increase over the full phase; advanced — maintain or slightly increase** (p.116-117). [CONVENTION]
- **Secondary lifts:** set volume "reasonably high" based on individual response history; if unknown, default to the **Level 2 hypertrophy guideline of 10–20 sets/week per main-lift muscle group**, mostly **6–20 reps at 0–3 RIR**, using **autoregulated double progression** (defined below), primarily **non-specific, low-technical-demand exercises** (p.117, Table 3.3). [CONVENTION]
  - Rationale for non-specific exercise choice: e.g. leg press/hack squat may drive quad growth similarly to back squat with less joint/CNS/lumbar fatigue at high volumes, and specificity matters less far from a peak (p.117). [SCIENCE]-informed reasoning, [CONVENTION] application.
- **Length:** 6–12 weeks per timeline; can run longer to absorb reactive deloads (p.117, Table 3.3). [CONVENTION]

**Table 3.3 — Volume Phase summary**
| Element | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Goal | Build muscle; maintain or build strength |
| Main lifts | 1–3 singles/lift/week; progress 5→8 RPE (5→2 RIR) |
| Secondary lifts | 10–20 sets/week/main-lift muscle group; mostly 6–20 reps @ 0–3 RIR; autoregulated double progression; non-specific/low-demand exercises |
| Length | 6–12 weeks per timeline |

## Load Phase (p.117-119)
- **Goal:** build main-lift strength while at least maintaining (ideally still building) muscle mass — achieved by raising main-lift intensity/volume and cutting secondary volume to make room (p.117). [CONVENTION]
- **Main lifts:** singles RPE progresses **6→9 RPE (4→1 RIR)** through the phase; single-set volume **increases by one set** vs. volume phase, ideally via **+1 training frequency occurrence/week**; if schedule is capped, double up singles on the same day instead (p.117-118). [CONVENTION]
  - Target average estimated-1RM trend: **novice — week-to-week increase; intermediate — every 2-3 weeks; advanced — some increase over the whole phase** (p.118). [CONVENTION]
  - **Back-off sets:** 2 sets of **3–5 reps at 5–8 RPE (5–2 RIR)** after each single, first set further from failure, second closer (cumulative fatigue). If doubling singles in a day, complete both singles first, then all 4 back-off sets (p.118). [CONVENTION]
  - Back-off reps **linearly decrease from 5 toward 3** across the phase; pacing depends on phase length × sessions/week with back-off work. Worked example, 6-week phase trained 2×/week (12 total back-off occasions): weeks 1–2 sets of 5, weeks 3–4 sets of 4, weeks 5–6 sets of 3 (p.118). [CONVENTION]
  - Load guidance when unsure: **~80–85% of estimated 1RM** for back-off sets — start closer to 80% if submaximal-rep tolerance is low, closer to 85% if high; progress from there to track the RPE/RIR target (p.119). [CONVENTION]
  - If singles/back-offs aren't tolerable (mental fatigue, pain, injury history), substitute **close variations** of the main lift (safety-bar/high-bar squat, low block pull, medium-grip bench) (p.119).
- **Secondary lifts:** cut volume to **half of the volume-phase amount** — e.g. 10–20 sets → **5–10 sets** — and **gradually reduce further** across the phase as main-lift load rises. Worked example, 8-week phase: weeks 1–2: 10 sets; weeks 3–4: 9; weeks 5–6: 8; weeks 7–8: 6 (p.119). [CONVENTION]
  - With lower secondary volume, some non-specific secondary work can be swapped for **main-lift variations** to better support strength — but must respect the added recovery cost of main-lift back-off work (p.119).

**Table 3.4 — Load Phase summary**
| Element | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Goal | Build strength; maintain/build muscle |
| Main lifts | 2–4 singles/lift/week, 6→9 RPE (4→1 RIR); 2 back-off sets/single @ 5–8 RPE (5–2 RIR), progressing 5→3 reps (~80–85% 1RM); variations if needed |
| Secondary lifts | 5–10 sets/week/main-lift muscle group, mostly 6–20 reps @ 0–3 RIR; autoregulated double progression; non-specific/low-demand |
| Length | 4–8 weeks; reduce secondary volume by 1–2 sets every 1–2 weeks (e.g. 10→9→8→6 across 4 blocks of 2 wks) |

## Peak Phase (optional taper) (p.119-123)
- Used only when preparing to compete/test 1RMs; **not required** to confirm strength gains (singles already estimate 1RM continuously) (p.120). [CONVENTION]
- **Length scales with prior volume+load phase duration** (excluding deloads): **2 weeks if 10–12 wks prior; 3 weeks if 13–15 wks; 4 weeks if 16+ wks** (p.120, Table 3.5). [CONVENTION]
- Includes "opener practice" (rehearsing planned first-attempt weights) and a light "primer session" — a low-load/low-volume main-lift session that may **acutely enhance performance for 24–48 hours** (p.120). [SCIENCE] (cites ref [63])
- After a peak or load phase, take an **intro week** before returning to a volume phase (p.120).
- **Final two weeks are fixed regardless of total peak length** (worked with a powerlifting 3/4/2 squat/bench/deadlift frequency example, but concepts generalize):
  - **Two weeks out:**
    1. Main lifts: 2–5 singles/lift at **7–10 RPE (3–0 RIR)**; frequency/singles count one higher than load phase if sustainable.
    2. Session types across the week: 2× SBD day, 1× SB day, 1× bench-only day.
    3. RPE by session order: 1st SBD ~9–10 RPE → SB day ~8–9 RPE → bench-only ~7–8 RPE → final SBD = **Opener Practice** (lift planned 1st attempts, ~90–92% 1RM / ~8 RPE, or estimated 1RM if progressing fast; **no back-off sets**).
    4. Back-off sets: 1 set of 2 reps at **5–8 RPE (5–2 RIR)**, ~85% estimated 1RM if unsure, after every single except opener practice.
    5. Secondary lifts: ~2 sets/muscle group, placed on SB and bench-only days (p.120-121).
  - **One week out:**
    1. Drop the bench-only session.
    2. 1st SBD day: singles at 7–8 RPE (slightly above last pre-opener warm-up).
    3. SB day: singles at 6–7 RPE.
    4. Final SBD day (1-2 days out): **Primer session** — singles at **4–5 RPE only**, no other work; choose 1 vs 2 days out based on fatigue (2 days out if fatigued, for a full rest day before competing).
    5. No back-off sets or secondary lifts this week (p.121).
- **Weeks 1-2 of a 3-4 week peak phase (i.e., the weeks before the fixed final two):** continue load-phase linearity —
  1. Increase singles **0.5–1 RPE above load phase** (reduce RIR by 0.5-1), near maximal, with one added set/frequency occurrence vs. load phase.
  2. 2–3 back-off sets of **3–4 reps at 5–8 RPE (5–2 RIR)**, ~85% of single if unsure.
  3. Secondary lifts cut to **minimum effective dose: ~3–4 sets/muscle group/week**; main-lift variations acceptable if recovery allows (p.121-122).
  - If the peak phase is a full 4 weeks, progress *between* week 1 and week 2: e.g. singles 7-8 RPE (wk1) → 8-9 RPE (wk2); back-off sets 3→2 sets and reps 4→3; secondary volume 4→3 sets (p.122).
  - Same substitution rules apply as load phase: use close variations, or double singles same-day, if frequency/tolerance is limited (p.122).

**Table 3.5 — Peak Phase summary**
| Element | Prescription |
|---|---|
| Goal | Peak strength, maintain muscle |
| Main lifts | 2–5 singles/lift/week @ 7–10 RPE (3–0 RIR); 0–3 back-off sets/single @ 5–8 RPE (5–2 RIR); if 3-4 wks, back-off reps progress 4→2 (~85% 1RM); variations if needed |
| Secondary lifts | 0–4 sets/week/main-lift muscle group, mostly 6–20 reps @ 0–3 RIR; autoregulated double progression; non-specific/low-demand |
| Length | 2-4 weeks per volume+load phase length (2wk/10-12wk, 3wk/13-15wk, 4wk/16+wk); decrease back-off and secondary volume weekly |

## Peaking for competitive bodybuilders (aside) (p.122-123)
- Physique peaking is primarily a **nutrition strategy**, not a fatigue-management taper — goal is appearance, not performance (p.122).
- Final-week training approach to encourage glycogen loading while minimizing muscle damage [SCIENCE] (cites ref [64]):
  1. Keep split, exercise selection, and set count the same as usual.
  2. Use higher reps: **8–20 reps/set** on all training days.
  3. Keep RPE **6–8.5**.
  4. Avoid exercises causing soreness, especially eccentric-emphasis at long muscle lengths (e.g. RDLs).
  5. Finish training **2-3 days before** competition.
  6. In the final 1-2 days, do a brief full-body pump-up routine (like backstage prep), **RPE ≤6, <30 min** (p.123).

## Hypertrophy Progression System — overview (p.123-124)
- Strips periodization down to what matters for pure muscle growth (drops the strength system's phasic-linearity purpose). For advanced lifters it retains a phasic structure, but for **muscle-group specialization**, not linearity (p.123-124). [CONVENTION]
- Two key features: (1) **rep-range variation**, (2) **autoregulated double progression** (p.124).

### Rep range variation — evidence base (p.124-126)
- [SCIENCE] Rep-range variation beyond the "traditional" 8-12 range appears beneficial, though no single structure is proven superior:
  - Schoenfeld et al. [62]: daily-undulating heavy(2-4RM)/moderate(8-12RM)/low-load(20-30RM) trended toward greater hypertrophy vs. constant 8-12RM (non-significant) (p.124-125).
  - Dos Santos et al. [60]: two pyramid structures (12→10→8RM vs. 15→10→5RM) both compared to 8-12RM alone trended favorable, non-significant.
  - Carvalho et al. [61]: a **block structure — 3 wks 8-12RM, 3 wks 1-3RM, 5 wks 8-12RM** — produced **significantly (if slightly) greater** quad hypertrophy vs. constant 8-12RM (p.125).
  - Antretter et al. [66]: no significant hypertrophy difference training 4-25RM within a session vs. a different weekly rep target within that range — implies high flexibility in how variation is delivered (p.125-126).
- [DISPUTED/limit] Bartolomei et al. [65]: a 4-wk hypertrophy block (10×10 @1RIR) → 4-wk strength block (6×3 @2RIR) → 2-wk power block (5RIR) produced **less** hypertrophy over 10 weeks than volume-matched daily-mixed training, because the block approach concentrated volume early, leaving 6 weeks of declining volume/rising RIR before the final measurement — likely causing muscle loss in the tail end (p.125).
- Synthesized principles (p.125): (1) rep-range variation is probably beneficial; (2) **avoid long stretches (>~3 weeks) training entirely outside hypertrophy-relevant rep ranges** (e.g., <6 reps at high RIR). [CONVENTION]
- Ways to structure variation, all viable per current evidence (p.126, Table 3.6):
  - **Within session** — different rep ranges lift-to-lift in the same workout.
  - **Between session** — low-rep day vs. high-rep day within the week.
  - **Alternating by week.**
  - **Alternating by mesocycle** (each block still must include hypertrophy-relevant ranges).
- Caution: multi-joint free-weight technical lifts training many muscles are poorly suited to high-rep work because discomfort degrades RIR accuracy — pick exercises carefully for high-rep days/weeks/blocks (p.126) (carried over from Level 2).

### Autoregulated Double Progression (p.126-128)
- The progression method used for **all lifts when training for hypertrophy**, and for **all secondary lifts when training for strength** (p.126). [CONVENTION] (cites ref [3])
- Setup: each lift has a **rep range + RIR range**, e.g. "10-15 reps at 0-2 RIR." Goal: pick loads that consistently land within both ranges; raise load once the range becomes too easy (p.126).
- **Ordered protocol:**
  1. **Choose a load for set 1** — pick a weight expected to land near the top of the RIR range and the middle/lower end of the rep range (e.g., aim for 12 reps at 2 RIR ≈ your ~14RM), after warming up.
  2. **Stop at the RIR target** — perform the set, stop at the upper RIR boundary (e.g. 2 RIR) even if more reps are possible.
  3. **Repeat with the same load if within range** — if set 1 landed in the rep range, keep that load for all remaining sets, aiming to hold reps; RIR will naturally drop across sets from fatigue, but don't go below the lower RIR boundary; reps falling below target range on later sets is fine/expected.
  4. **Adjust load if outside range** — if set 1 reps were below or above the target range, adjust load up/down for the next set; **rule of thumb: ~4% load change per rep outside the range** if unsure. Keep that adjusted load for subsequent sets.
  5. **Progress load when ready** — once the top of the rep range is hit at the top of the RIR range on set 1 (e.g., 15 reps @ 2 RIR), **increase load next session**.
  6. **Allow for fluctuations** — performance isn't linear; some weeks regress. Judge by trend, not single sessions. Slight RIR misjudgment (e.g., meaning 2 RIR but hitting 1) is fine; a bit of aggression/overreach now and then is not a problem (p.126-128).
- **Worked example — intermediate lifter**, target 3×10-15 @ 0-2 RIR (Table 3.7, p.127): took **5 sessions** to progress load (40lbs→45lbs) while reps climbed 12,12,11 → … → 15,14,14 before the jump.
- **Worked example — novice lifter**, same target (Table 3.8, p.128): progressed load in **3 sessions** — novices progress faster under this system.
- Rate of progression by training age (p.128): **novice fastest; intermediate as in the 5-session example; advanced may take roughly twice as long as intermediate.** [CONVENTION]
- Intermediate/advanced lifters are explicitly **encouraged to break the model's rules**: peak strength and work capacity can recover at different rates session to session [SCIENCE] (cites ref [67]) — so a lifter who feels stronger-but-not-fresher-at-reps may jump ahead in load, or one who feels more stamina-than-strength may drop a load increment and chase extra reps (even above the nominal rep-range ceiling). Staying within the RIR range while progressing over time is what matters, not rigid adherence to the exact steps (p.128).

### Muscle Group Specialization Cycles (advanced lifters) (p.128-132)
- Rationale: advanced lifters face steep diminishing returns (progress from training years 3-4 may match the following 8 years combined — cites ref [16], p.128-129); simply training more to compensate is infeasible because (p.129-130):
  1. Time cost: tripling training dose (e.g., 10→30 sets/muscle group, theoretically ~doubling hypertrophy rate per Level 2 dose-response data) demands roughly another 8h/week for someone currently training 4h/week for 10 sets — usually impractical.
  2. Can't jump straight to that dose; must ramp up.
  3. The dose-response literature behind that theoretical doubling is drawn from 8-12 week studies on several (not all) muscle groups at once — average population response, not necessarily individual response, and not validated for whole-body simultaneous high volume.
- These same limitations are read as **indirect evidence that specialization cycles (raising volume for 1-2 muscle groups at a time, not all) are a more feasible way to capture the dose-response** (p.129-130). [CONVENTION] (interpretive, not a directly tested claim — book flags this as anecdotal/indirect)
- Anecdotal (untested, likely untestable at needed timescales) practice: **also decreasing** volume on non-specialized groups (not just holding it steady) while raising it on specialized ones, to preserve total weekly training time (p.130). [DISPUTED — explicitly "hasn't been tested in research"]
- Recommended structure for advanced hypertrophy lifters: **phasic alternation between "balanced phases" and "specialization phases,"** each long enough for adaptation; volumes represent phase averages (p.130).

**Table 3.9 — Specialization mesocycle pattern** (p.131)
| Balanced | Specialization | Balanced | Specialization | Balanced |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8-12wk, 10-20 sets/wk all muscle groups | 8-12wk, 20-30 sets/wk on 1-2 specialized groups, 5-15 on remaining | 8-12wk, 10-20 sets/wk all | 8-12wk, 20-30 sets/wk specialized, 5-15 remaining | 8-12wk, 10-20 sets/wk all |

- Numbers assume a baseline of 10-20 sets/muscle/week; **shift proportionally** if the individual's baseline differs: **increase specialized-group volume by ~50%; decrease other groups by 1/3 to 1/2** to hold total weekly volume roughly constant (p.131). [CONVENTION]
  - Worked example: baseline 12 sets/week → specialized ~18 sets, others 6-8 sets (p.131).
- For large/lower-body/multi-joint/axially-loaded specialized groups (hamstrings, glutes, quads): **ramp to target volume over the first 2-4 weeks** of the cycle rather than jumping straight in (p.131-132). [CONVENTION]
- Train specialized muscle groups **first in the session** for freshness/focus; do not let the added volume degrade RIR accuracy or execution quality (p.132).
- Distribution: either a couple of weekly "marathon" sessions for the specialized group with easy sessions elsewhere, or (anecdotally often better for training quality) a **high-frequency approach — 3-4 sets of the specialized group at the start of most/all sessions** (p.132).

**Implementation tips (p.132):**
1. Progress gradually — ramp large/fatiguing specialized groups over 2-4 weeks.
2. Train specialized groups first, while freshest.
3. Avoid "going through the motions" — keep RIR targets and execution sharp despite higher volume.
4. Distribute workload — prefer high-frequency (e.g., 3-4 sets/session) over marathon sessions.
5. Manage fatigue — cutting non-specialized volume creates room without overloading recovery.

## Tracking Progress When Training for Hypertrophy (p.132-133)
- At the advanced stage, muscle-mass gain rate is too slow for objective physique measurement to be a useful *short-term* feedback signal — **use strength changes as the primary progress proxy** even for physique-oriented lifters; visual/caliper/tape measures still have a role (especially during fat-loss phases) but should not be the main feedback mechanism (p.133). [CONVENTION]
- Supporting evidence/reasoning:
  - Advanced natural bodybuilders often compete only every other year (or take years off); improvements between shows are frequently too subtle to see without a diet-down, and stage weight sometimes rises only 1-2 lb (if at all) between seasons — biggest gains are often in **muscle retention or leanness**, not added size (p.133).
  - Large stage-weight jumps in advanced natural bodybuilders, when they occur, are typically attributable to: better contest prep (less muscle loss), recovery from an injury that had held back a muscle group, or fixing a genuine training/nutrition flaw (i.e., the lifter wasn't truly advanced yet) (p.133).
  - Even **DXA** ("gold standard" body-composition tool) has measurement error from hydration, food intake, glycogen, gut contents, time of day, training-induced inflammation — **unstandardized DXA can vary ±5%**, e.g. up to a **~9 lb (~4 kg) swing** in estimated lean mass for some athletes — a margin that can exceed years' worth of real advanced-stage muscle gain (p.133). [SCIENCE]
- Conclusion: performance (progressive overload — load, reps, set quality within the hypertrophy framework) is the best available proxy for hypertrophy at the advanced level; if overload keeps occurring and sleep/nutrition/recovery are adequate, muscle growth is likely occurring even if unmeasurable directly (p.133-134).

## How to Identify and Break Plateaus (p.134-137)

### Diagnostic flowchart — "Are you plateaued?" (Figure 3.6, p.134-135; figure with accompanying text)
Plateau = not making gym progress appropriate to training status (per the training-age progress-rate expectations established elsewhere in the book).

Ordered decision logic:
1. **Are you plateaued?** If **NO** → don't change anything.
2. If **YES** → check basics: is the answer "no" to any of: sleeping 7+ hours? not in an energy deficit? protein ≥0.7 g/lb (≥1.6 g/kg)? neither over- nor under-estimating RPE? training muscles/lifts ≥2×/week (for strength goals)? exercise selection/ROM/tempo appropriate and consistent?
   - If **YES** (i.e., at least one of those basics is unmet) → **fix that, run another mesocycle, reassess.**
   - If **NO** (i.e., "no, I've taken care of all of those") → proceed to the recovery check.
3. **Recovery check:** dreading the gym? sleep quality worse than normal? load/reps actually decreasing? life stress worse than normal? aches/pains worse than normal?
   - **No to all, or all-but-one** → likely recovering.
   - **Yes to 2+** → likely **not** recovering.
4. Branch on recovery status:
   - **Not recovering (still yes to 2+):** **temporarily reduce volume — take a light week.** If performance improves, good. If fatigue/stall recurs quickly, the issue is likely deeper than a deload/taper can fix — consider **doing less volume overall or reorganizing it** to better manage fatigue.
   - **Recovering (mostly no):** **it may be time to increase your training dose.**
- Aside — joint/tendon pain only: if that's the *only* "yes," a normal-volume/normal-RPE week is fine, just raise reps to **12–20** on exercises that provoke pain; if still not pain-free, lower the load further and consider **BFR** for limb training (p.135). [CONVENTION]

### Blood Flow Restriction (BFR) — pain workaround (p.135-136)
- Mechanism: a cuff/bandage/tourniquet/knee wrap at the proximal limb restricts venous outflow while maintaining arterial inflow, preventing metabolite clearance → earlier fiber fatigue → recruitment of high-threshold motor units **at loads as low as 20-30% 1RM** (p.135). [SCIENCE]
- Efficacy: **not more effective than traditional training, but can be equally effective for hypertrophy** (not for strength) [SCIENCE] (cites ref [69], p.135-136); useful for knee/elbow pain because it maintains stimulus at much lower joint-stressing loads, possibly aiding joint recovery [SCIENCE] (cites ref [70]).
- Protocol when substituting for elbow/knee pain: wrap to **7/10 tightness** (firm, not restrictive to tingling/discoloration/purplish — loosen if either occurs); perform the **normally programmed set count** at **20-30% of 1RM to 0-3 RIR** (p.136). [CONVENTION]

### Changing Volume (p.136-137)
- If nutrition, sleep, stress, training organization, and RPE/RIR accuracy are all dialed in and the lifter **still isn't recovering even after a deload**, volume is likely too high — an inefficient stress-to-recovery ratio (only 1-2 microcycles pushing into overreach territory). [CONVENTION]
  - Fix: **reduce sets by ~20% across the board** (e.g., 15 sets → 12 sets/muscle group or movement), then reassess consistency of progress (p.136-137).
- Conversely, if there's no technical issue (misjudging RIR; poor form/exercise selection failing to target the intended muscle; inconsistent tempo/ROM — p.136-137) and no other bottleneck (sleep, nutrition, stress, training organization), and the lifter feels recovered with spare time/energy: a **~20% volume increase is a reasonable, research-supported guideline likely to produce further progress** [SCIENCE] (cites ref [71], p.137).
  - Can be applied globally (all muscle groups/lifts, if plateaued everywhere) or targeted (specific stalling movements only).
  - When raising volume, consider also raising **frequency** to spread the added workload and avoid overloading any single session (p.137).

## Chapter Summary (p.137)
- Establishing an appropriate training dose (Level 2) is necessary but not sufficient — long-term progress requires the plan to **adapt and evolve over time** via the systems in this chapter (Strength Progression System, Hypertrophy Progression System, plateau diagnosis/volume adjustment) (p.137).
