# MSP Training — Level 4 — Exercise Selection

> **Source:** *The Muscle & Strength Pyramid: Training* 3e (Helms/Valdez/Morgan), book pp.145-186. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/msp-training.txt` or PDF pp.145-186.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] research-established · [CONVENTION] the authors' specific evidence-based recommendation · [DISPUTED] contested / authors flag uncertainty.

## Framing: where exercise selection sits in The Pyramid (p.146)
- [CONVENTION] The authors place exercise selection in the middle of The Pyramid, but only under two assumptions: (1) if training for strength, the program already includes the specific lifts the lifter wants to improve; (2) if training for hypertrophy, the program uses a "typical" mix of traditional bodybuilding movements. Without those assumptions in place, exercise selection could matter far more (p.145-146).
- [SCIENCE] Anecdote-based exercise selection (copying what elite lifters/bodybuilders do) is limited: elite performers are often anatomically suited to certain lifts and naturally have standout body parts, which doesn't generalize — "Success leaves clues, not answers" (p.146).

## Exercise Selection Principles for Strength

### Movement specificity (p.146-147)
- [SCIENCE] The SAID principle ("Specific Adaptations to Imposed Demands") is a foundational exercise-science tenet: strength is a skill expressed through specific movements, not just a general body quality (p.146).
- [SCIENCE] Specificity is commonly modeled as a spectrum of both **load specificity** (%1RM used) and **movement specificity** (similarity to the target lift), with "less specific" to "more specific" endpoints (Figure 4.1, p.147).
- [SCIENCE] Training at higher %1RM generally produces greater 1RM improvements than training at lower %1RM (p.147, citing [2,3]), and training volume performed with the specific lift itself tends to be more effective than volume with similar movements targeting the same muscles (p.147, citing [4]) — this is the rationale underlying fractional set counting (from Level 2/3).
- [SCIENCE] Rossi et al. compared squat-only, leg-press-only, and mixed squat+leg-press training: both squat-inclusive groups improved jump height more than leg-press-only, because the squat is biomechanically more similar to jumping despite neither being a literal jump (p.147, citing [5]).

### Specificity is more than a spectrum — movement complexity and sensitivity (p.148)
- [SCIENCE] The same Rossi study: the squat-only group improved squat 1RM more than the leg-press-only group, but **all three groups improved leg-press 1RM similarly** — complex movements (squat) transfer well to simpler movements (leg press), but not vice versa. Complex movements are more "sensitive" to specificity and require more practice to master; simpler movements benefit from a broader range of training inputs (p.148, citing [5]).
- [DISPUTED] Even restricting to complex multi-joint lifts, the simple-spectrum model misses that load specificity and movement specificity interact (e.g., a single rep at 80%1RM on the competition lift vs. a 1RM on a close variation — which is "more specific" is not obvious) (p.148).

### Movement variation / varied practice (p.148-150)
- [SCIENCE] The motor-learning principle of **varied practice**: practicing multiple variations of a skill in a period ingrains motor skills more effectively than always practicing under identical conditions (p.148).
- [SCIENCE] Chua et al. showed practicing throwing/putting at multiple distances improved accuracy more than practicing at one fixed distance, partly via enhanced attentional focus (p.149, citing [6]).
- [SCIENCE] Czyż et al. 2024 meta-analysis of 54 studies (1960s–present) on "contextual interference": creating interference in the learning context (changing grip, load, exercise, or interposing unrelated tasks) reduces short-term skill-acquisition efficiency but **boosts long-term skill retention** (p.149, citing [7]).
- [DISPUTED] Only 1 of the 54 studies in that meta-analysis was on lifting weights (p.149, citing [8]). "Open" skills (reactive sports like throwing/cutting) may respond better to varied practice than "closed" skills (swimming, cycling, track, and potentially lifting) (p.149, citing [9]). The Bulgarian method (maxing the specific competition lift essentially every session) has produced champions, showing hyperspecificity is *viable*, though not necessarily *optimal* for all lifters/contexts/timeframes (p.149).
- [SCIENCE] Fonseca et al. 2014: one group trained only Smith-machine squat; another split volume across Smith squat, deadlift, lunge, and leg press. **The varied group improved more on the Smith squat itself** than the squat-only group, despite the squat-only group varying reps/loads (p.149-150, citing [10]).
- Caveats on that finding (p.150): participants were recreationally trained (variation may matter more early in skill acquisition); all groups still improved (specific-only training still works, just perhaps not optimally); variations must be *relevant* — dissimilar tasks (e.g., swimming for deadlift) don't count as movement variants, though they might still provide contextual-interference/retention benefit if interposed between sets.

### Efficient use of specificity (p.150-152)
- [SCIENCE] [CONVENTION] Per the Level 2 meta-regression, only ~5 fractional sets/week of a specific lift was needed to reach the maximal benefit of highly-specific training on average — a modest dose, well within most trained lifters' recovery capacity (p.150-151, citing [2]). Caveat: that meta-analysis was not restricted to competitive strength athletes.
- [SCIENCE] Androulakis-Korakakis et al.: competitive powerlifters on a "2/3/1" weekly frequency (2 heavy squat singles, 3 bench singles, 1 deadlift single, each + 2×3 back-off sets @80%) made substantial strength gains on just 14 squat reps, 21 bench reps, and 7 deadlift reps/week (p.151, citing [11]) — evidence that high specificity is efficient and doesn't require high volume, at least short-term.
- [SCIENCE] Gonzalez-Badillo et al.: 29 junior weightlifters in three volume-equated groups doing 46 (low), 93 (moderate), or 184 (high) heavy reps (90-100%1RM) over 10 weeks on squat/snatch/clean&jerk. **No significant strength-gain differences between groups**; if anything, low/moderate groups did better — high group only improved squat 1RM from baseline, while low/moderate groups improved both squat and clean&jerk (p.151-152, citing [12]).
- [SCIENCE] Reasoning for why more heavy specific volume can be counterproductive (p.152): heavy specific lifts are resource-intensive (multiple warm-ups, long rests, high mental/physical effort — a strong lifter may need 30+ min for 5 heavy squat sets); the additional long-term strength gain from extra specific volume typically comes from **hypertrophy, not additional skill practice**, but heavy sets (2-4RM) are less efficient for hypertrophy than moderate loads (8-12RM) (citing [13]), so equivalent hypertrophy requires more heavy-lift sets than moderate-rep sets (citing [14]); free-weight lower-body exercises produce higher perceived exertion than machines (citing [15]); bodybuilders (lighter, more single-joint/machine work) have lower injury rates than powerlifters/weightlifters (citing [16]).
- [CONVENTION] Practical implication: for volume beyond the ~5-fractional-set specificity threshold, done for hypertrophy purposes, prefer a secondary lift that's easier to recover from over piling more sets onto the main lift (p.152).

### Targeted secondary lifts (p.152-159)
- [CONVENTION] Coaching-experience section (first-person, Dr. Helms) illustrating that "non-specific" training can be highly specific when targeted at the right individual problem:
  - Switching a lifter from conventional to sumo deadlift purely for better recovery (not because sumo was stronger) allowed higher deadlift frequency and broke a plateau (p.152-153).
  - When grip became the limiting factor on heavy sumo pulls, the fix was heavy barbell holds for time (near-max static holds just below lockout height) rather than an additional heavy deadlift session, which would have added counterproductive fatigue (p.153).
  - A powerlifter with long femurs/short torso/back-pain history plateaued on squat; reducing squat to **one single at 9-10 RPE every 1-2 weeks**, with the rest of "squat" development coming from deadlift variations plus leg press/lunges/back extension/leg extension/leg curl/machine hip thrust, produced a new squat PR after a multi-year plateau — attributed to less pain, more motivation, and better lower-body muscular development (p.153-154).
  - Paused deadlifts at mid-shin used as a teaching tool specifically for lifters who lose bar-to-shin contact and drift the bar forward — used to build awareness/correction, not as a general prescription (p.154).
- [CONVENTION] Principle distilled: "non-specific" training can be highly specific when targeted at the right problem (p.154).

#### Movement weak points and "sticking regions" (p.154-158)
- [SCIENCE] Pausing exactly at a visible sticking point is logically flawed: (1) to pause you must reduce force output — the opposite of what's needed to power through a sticking region; (2) the visible "stuck" point is *after* you've already failed to sustain velocity, like skidding past your stopping point after braking (p.154-155).
- [CONVENTION] Pauses still have value for motor control/awareness — e.g., pausing below the knee on deadlift to reinforce keeping the bar close to the shins; pausing in the squat hole to let elastic energy dissipate and build tightness; pause/pin squats to teach eccentric-concentric transition control; longer chest pauses on bench to prepare for unpredictable competition press commands (p.155).
- [SCIENCE] Strength is joint-angle specific (from Level 2); isometric training at the specific weak joint angle may be a way to target a force deficit at a sticking point, but pinpointing the exact weak angle (vs. the visible momentum-loss point) is hard without lab/video analysis (p.155, citing [17]).
- [CONVENTION] Technique breakdowns near maximal effort (e.g., "squat mornings" — hips shoot up, back tightness lost; or thoracic/lumbar flexion in heavy deadlifts) can cause or worsen a sticking point; using lift variations that "punish" the fault and "reward" correct technique (e.g., front squat, which dumps forward if the hips shoot up) is reasonable (p.156).
- [SCIENCE] Accommodating resistance (bands/chains) increases load progressively through the concentric ROM, altering the resistance profile against the classic "hardest point" of each lift (squat: just past the hole; bench: a few inches off the chest; deadlift: below the knee), making load also increase as biomechanical advantage increases (p.156-157). Historically popularized to mimic the strength-curve effect of equipped lifting (suits/wraps/shirts), which is uncomfortable/time-consuming to train in year-round, so raw + accommodating resistance is often used as a substitute specificity tool (p.157).
- [SCIENCE] [DISPUTED] A 2022 meta-analysis (~500 participants) found **no average benefit of accommodating resistance over traditional loading** (p.157, citing [18]). Caveats: those studies didn't test equipped lifters specifically; lack of average benefit doesn't rule out individual usefulness (especially near lockout); generating more force before a sticking point may create carrying momentum through it (citing [17]).
- [SCIENCE] [DISPUTED] Explosive/speed work: light-load speed work causes a "braking" phase near lockout as the lifter decelerates to avoid the bar/body leaving the bench/floor (p.157-158, citing [19]); accommodating resistance can counter this by making the load heavier near lockout, allowing acceleration through full ROM (citing [20]). Whether explosive training improves rate of force development more than heavy lifting alone is **highly individual** (p.158, citing [21]).
- [CONVENTION] Summary of four tools for weak points/sticking regions (p.158, citing [17]):
  1. Pauses in key areas — for motor control/position awareness, not necessarily at the visible sticking point.
  2. Isometric training at the point of force deficit (exact point hard to pinpoint without lab equipment).
  3. Variations that punish common technical faults and reward efficient technique.
  4. Explosive training to improve rate of force development before a sticking point, with or without accommodating resistance (individual response varies).
- [CONVENTION] These tools involve guesswork and individual variability; they are not required — sometimes more practice with competition-form lifting is the best route (p.158).

#### Strength recommendations (p.158-159)
- [CONVENTION] Step 1: assess how much specific training the lifter can tolerate/recover from. Meeting the ~3-5 fractional-sets/week recommendation (from Level 2/3) doesn't have to mean literally 3-5 sets of the competition lift — using a lower dose of the main lift plus close variations the lifter tolerates better can be more effective/sustainable, while still providing varied practice (p.158-159).
- [CONVENTION] Worked example: a lifter who gets hip pain past 3-4 heavy sets of low-bar competition squats could hit ~5 fractional squat sets via 2 sets of competition squat + 6 sets of close variations (safety bar squat, front squat, high-bar squat, banded/chain squat if the weak point is near lockout, or belt squat) — roughly contributing 3 additional fractional sets (p.159). Note: deadlift work also contributes to squat fractional-set volume.
- [CONVENTION] For the 5-10 sets of hypertrophy work (from Level 2/3 volume targets) in that same scenario, low-bar squats would be a poor choice given their poor recovery profile and pain risk; machine-based options (leg press, hack squat, leg extension, isolated glute work) are easier to recover from (p.159).
- [CONVENTION] Integrate exercise-selection choices with the Level 2 volume/frequency/intensity recommendations and the Level 3 Strength Progression System (p.159).

## Exercise Selection Principles for Hypertrophy

### Exercises must train the target muscle (p.159-166)
- [SCIENCE] Muscle-group labels (e.g., "hamstring exercise") oversimplify: the hamstrings are 4 muscles (biceps femoris long head, biceps femoris short head, semitendinosus, semimembranosus). All four flex the knee (leg curl action); only three (excluding biceps femoris short head) also extend the hip (RDL action) (p.160).
- [SCIENCE] EMG data show more biceps-femoris-short-head activity during leg curls than RDLs — RDL alone under-recruits it (p.160-161, citing [22]).
- [SCIENCE] Monoarticular muscles (cross one joint, e.g., biceps femoris short head, vastus lateralis/medialis/intermedius) perform only that joint's action. Biarticular muscles (cross two joints, e.g., rectus femoris, the other 3 hamstrings, triceps long head, biceps brachii) act on both joints when contracting, which can make recruitment in compound lifts counter-intuitive (p.161-163).
- [SCIENCE] Rectus femoris (biarticular: hip flexion + knee extension) opposes hip extension, so it is **minimally recruited** in squats/leg press/hack squat/lunges (simultaneous hip+knee extension), but **is** recruited during isolated knee extension like leg extension (p.162, citing [23,24]). Consequently, squat training produces minimal hamstring and rectus femoris hypertrophy (p.162, citing [25,26]); leg extensions produce superior rectus femoris growth vs. both Smith-machine squats (citing [27]) and leg press (citing [28]).
- [SCIENCE] The triceps long head (biarticular: elbow extension + scapular/shoulder extension) is not recruited during bench/OHP (opposes the row-like scapular action) nor during rows/pulldowns (opposes biceps action) — must be isolated (e.g., pushdown) to train effectively. Bench press alone grows the other 2 triceps heads but minimally grows the long head; combining pushdowns + bench presses grows all three heads (p.163, citing [29]).
- [CONVENTION] Practical conclusion: hypertrophy training requires more exercise variety than strength-focused training because of this biomechanical complexity, particularly to reach biarticular muscles — isolation movements are needed to maximize development of all muscles (p.163).
- [SCIENCE] Biceps brachii is a relatively weak shoulder flexor (front-raise action) (p.164, citing [30]); some data indicate rows may not develop biceps as effectively as biceps curls (citing [31]) — so a hypertrophy-focused lifter (unlike a pure-strength lifter) should generally include limb-isolation work (p.164).
- [SCIENCE] Pec major has clavicular (upper) and sternal (lower) heads with different joint actions and **separate nerve innervation** (p.164, citing [32]); some data show incline pressing produces more upper-pec growth than flat pressing (citing [33]). Various back muscles attach at different spine/scapula sites (citing [34]), making multi-plane upper-body pulling likely necessary to maximize back hypertrophy (p.164).
- [SCIENCE] The target muscle must be sufficiently fatigued near failure (high RPE/low RIR) to be effectively trained — it must be one of the *limiting factors* of the set, not just anatomically involved (p.166).
- [SCIENCE] Plotkin et al. 2023: hip thrust (glutes shortened at lockout — a poor resistance profile per the long-length principle below) vs. squat (glutes maximally stretched at the bottom — a favorable profile) to failure produced **similar glute growth** despite the resistance-profile difference — because in the hip thrust, an isolation exercise, glutes are reliably the limiting factor, whereas in the squat, quads/glutes/lumbar/adductors compete to be the limiting factor, so glutes aren't always maximally fatigued (p.166, citing [35]). Squats also produced greater quad and adductor hypertrophy in the same study — an efficiency argument for combining single- and multi-joint exercises (p.166-167).

### Exercises ideally challenge the muscle at long lengths (p.167-174)
- [SCIENCE] Full-ROM training has traditionally been considered best for hypertrophy (p.167, citing [36]), but this was largely based on comparisons vs. **shortened partials** (e.g., half squats — training at short muscle length) (p.167, citing [36,37]). In rarer comparisons against **lengthened partials** (e.g., deep squats not locked out — training at long muscle length), full-range training did **not** outperform (p.167, citing [36,38]).
- [SCIENCE] When lengthened and shortened partials are matched for equal degrees of joint motion, **lengthened partials produce greater hypertrophy** (p.168, citing [39-41]). Isometric training likewise produces more hypertrophy at longer vs. shorter muscle lengths (citing [42]). Even at the same ROM, positioning a biarticular muscle in a more stretched state (via the second joint) increases growth (citing [43-47]).
- [SCIENCE] Example: seated leg curl (hip flexed, hamstrings more stretched) grows the biarticular hamstrings more than lying leg curl at the same ROM; lying leg curl instead grows the sartorius (a monoarticular-at-knee, biarticular-at-hip flexor) more via greater stretch there (p.168, citing [43]). Leg extension with the seat leaned all the way back stretches the biarticular rectus femoris more and produces more rectus femoris growth than upright leg extension at the same ROM (p.169, citing [47]).
- [SCIENCE] Reaching long muscle length alone is not sufficient — the muscle must be **challenged** (loaded) at that length. Kobayashi et al.: incline curls (biceps more stretched, biarticular via shoulder) grew biceps slightly more; preacher curls (monoarticular brachialis/brachioradialis more loaded at long length near rep start, because peak load occurs where the forearm is parallel to the ground) grew brachialis/brachioradialis more (p.169-170, citing [46]) — replicated by a second study measuring elbow-flexor thickness (citing [48]).
- [SCIENCE] Maeo et al.: Nordic leg curl (most challenging near full knee extension, body near-parallel to ground) grew the monoarticular biceps-femoris short head more; eccentric-biased seated leg curl (hamstrings stretched via hip flexion) grew the biarticular hamstrings more (p.170, citing [44]).
- [SCIENCE] Synthesis: hypertrophy depends on applying significant load at long muscle lengths, not merely reaching them (p.171, citing [44,46,48,49]).

**Lengthened partials vs. full ROM (p.171-174) — [DISPUTED]:**
- [SCIENCE] Studies favoring lengthened partials over full ROM: Kassiano et al. — leg-press calf raises: lengthened partial (bottom half) > shortened partial and > full ROM for calf growth (p.171, citing [50]). Pedrosa et al. — leg extension initial 45°: similar upper-quad growth, greater lower-quad growth vs. full ROM (citing [41]). Maeo et al. — multi-hip machine: lengthened partial > full ROM for glute growth (citing [51]).
- [SCIENCE] [DISPUTED] Four other studies found similar growth between lengthened partials and full ROM (citing [38,52-54]); two are hard to interpret because both groups showed minimal hypertrophy overall (Werkhausen et al. used a shallow, explosive-only, assisted-eccentric protocol with no real hypertrophy in either group [38]; Gschneidner et al. used skinfold/circumference measures, a large ~300-participant sample, but a single-set-per-exercise protocol produced little growth [53]).
- [SCIENCE] The two remaining studies that did show meaningful growth (Wolf [52], Hinson [54]) both used upper-body exercises (overhead DB triceps extension, behind-the-back cable curl) that are **already hardest at the lengthened position** — so a dedicated lengthened-partial protocol added little because the short range was already lightly loaded (p.172-173).
- [CONVENTION] Practical takeaway (p.173):
  - If an exercise is hardest when the muscle is **shorter** (e.g., calf raises at lockout, many rows, pulldowns) → good candidate for lengthened partials.
  - If an exercise is hardest when the muscle is **longer** (e.g., squats, RDLs, many presses) → lengthened partials may offer no benefit, and staying in the lengthened range can severely limit breathing and reduce total reps (illustrated via squat/RDL breath-holding at the bottom position) (p.173-174).
  - Studies favoring lengthened partials used **machines** with more consistent/engineered resistance profiles where the short range still presents meaningful load — with free weights the hardest portion of a lift is usually more obvious (p.173).
- [CONVENTION] Overall conclusion: training at long muscle lengths (not necessarily via lengthened partials specifically) likely produces superior hypertrophy, but lengthened partials are only an improvement on exercises where the shortened end-range creates fatigue that detracts from getting more exposure to the lengthened range (p.174).

### Exercises must be feasible (p.174-176)
- [CONVENTION] The **PLATE** checklist for assessing exercise feasibility (p.174-175):
  1. **P**ainful? — consistent pain (from injury history, biomechanics, etc.) is demotivating and may inhibit force/tension generation.
  2. **L**oadable? — if you can only manage 2 good reps of an exercise (e.g., Nordic curls), progression becomes very difficult.
  3. **A**vailable? — equipment/gym access must fit the time budget (e.g., don't commute an extra hour for "better" equipment if it eats your session time).
  4. **T**ime-efficient? — don't choose a variation that requires elaborate setup (e.g., stacking blocks/plates for a chest-supported row) when a simpler variation achieves the same training effect.
  5. **E**njoyable? — dread of an exercise (even one with superior resistance profile, e.g., RDL vs. conventional deadlift/back extension) impedes full effort; choose exercises you enjoy when the alternative is close in quality.
- [CONVENTION] The "best" exercise is the one performed consistently and progressed over time within the lifter's schedule/preferences — adherence (the base of The Pyramid) outweighs marginal science-backed exercise superiority (p.175).

### Unfamiliar movements are less effective for hypertrophy (p.175-176)
- [SCIENCE] "Muscle confusion" (constant exercise swapping to keep growing) is largely backwards — **unfamiliar movements produce less hypertrophy until mastered** (p.175).
- [SCIENCE] Chilibeck et al. 1998: untrained participants did bench press, leg press, and biceps curl for 20 weeks, assessed at 10 and 20 weeks. Arm hypertrophy (low-complexity biceps curl) was significant by the 10-week midpoint; trunk/leg hypertrophy (higher-complexity bench/leg press) wasn't apparent until 20 weeks (p.175-176, citing [55]). Explanation: low-complexity movements are learned quickly, permitting early overload; complex multi-joint lifts take longer to master, delaying sufficient overload for hypertrophy (p.176).
- [SCIENCE] Early strength gains on new exercises (especially complex ones) are primarily — not exclusively — neuromuscular rather than hypertrophic (p.176, citing [56]); as movement skill develops, the lifter can progressively overload and produce hypertrophy (citing [57]).
- [CONVENTION] Implication: even for pure hypertrophy goals, movements should be mastered before relying on them to drive growth. Some exercise variation is needed (per the biomechanics/long-length principles above), but variety does not require *constant* swapping — occasional exercise changes are fine, but losing familiarity via continual variation is counterproductive (p.176).

### Hypertrophy recommendations (p.176-178)
- [CONVENTION] Base hypertrophy programming around **six core movement categories**, each definable across machine/bodyweight/free-weight and bilateral/unilateral variants (p.176-177):
  1. **Squats** — simultaneous knee+hip extension (squat, leg press, hack squat, front squat, lunge).
  2. **Hinges** — hip-dominant (deadlift, back extension, RDL, good morning, hip thrust).
  3. **Horizontal presses** — any angle incline/decline (bench press, incline DB press, dips, machine press, weighted push-ups).
  4. **Vertical presses** — including high incline for limited mobility (OHP, high incline DB press, machine shoulder press, pike push-ups).
  5. **Horizontal pulls** — any row angle (bench row, chest-supported DB row, inverted row, machine row, cable row).
  6. **Vertical pulls** — including high incline (lat pulldown, machine pulldown, pull-up, single-arm pulldown, high cable row).
- [CONVENTION] Within each category: select **1-3 exercises** based on the feasibility (PLATE) and long-length-challenge principles above, and distribute volume across them (p.177).
- [CONVENTION] Additional guidelines (p.177-178):
  - Include some incline pressing for upper-pec development.
  - For back development, include **at least two horizontal-pull variations plus vertical pulls**: one horizontal pull with elbows flared/higher pull path (targets scapular retractors + posterior deltoid) and another emphasizing shoulder extension.
  - If overhead pressing is skipped due to mobility/neck issues (justified because incline pressing already heavily trains the anterior deltoid), add more isolation work to still hit deltoid volume targets.
  - Include **at least one isolation exercise** for: biceps/elbow flexors, triceps, calves (straight-leg variant only), quadriceps (to reach the biarticular rectus femoris), hamstrings (to reach the biceps femoris short head), and middle deltoid (under-recruited in most multi-joint upper-body lifts).
  - Direct work for upper traps, forearms, or abs can be added if underdeveloped, but in the authors' coaching experience these often grow little from direct work since they already receive substantial indirect training (p.178).

## Exercise Order (p.178-179)
- [SCIENCE] More total reps per set are generally achievable when an exercise is performed earlier in a session, while fresh (p.178, citing [58]). Rationale for putting multi-joint free-weight lifts first: they stimulate the most total muscle mass, are the most technically complex, create the most fatigue, and carry the highest injury risk (p.178).
- [SCIENCE] Nunes et al. 2021 meta-analysis (11 studies, 268 participants): strength gains are **slightly greater** when an exercise is performed first in the session; **no difference in hypertrophy** was found based on exercise order (p.178, citing [59]).
- [CONVENTION] Exception: a bodybuilder with a glaring weak point poorly stimulated by the day's planned multi-joint lifts can train that muscle group first — the meta-analysis suggests this won't hurt hypertrophy, and prioritizing a lagging area while fresh is logical. Caveat: doing so must not meaningfully reduce performance on the subsequent multi-joint lifts (p.178-179). Example: training weak biceps or calves first, before bench/OHP or squat/deadlift respectively, has minimal negative carryover to those big lifts (p.178-179).

## Summary (p.179)
- [CONVENTION] Prioritize movements you want to get stronger at: perform them first in the session, at your target volume, using the specific version you want to improve — unless specific-work tolerance is limited, in which case use well-transferring variations chosen for pain, injury history, technical faults, sticking points, equipment availability, and preference.
- [CONVENTION] Hypertrophy volume meant to *support* strength goals should use less-fatiguing, less time-consuming exercises consistent with the hypertrophy exercise-selection principles above.
- [CONVENTION] For pure hypertrophy goals, multi-joint barbell movements are still usable (they're time-efficient and squats/hinges/presses tend to have favorable resistance profiles), but recovery cost, per-muscle volume targets, and session mental demand must be balanced against machine-based and isolation exercises — some muscles simply cannot be fully trained via a barbell-only, multi-joint-purist approach. A hypertrophy-focused lifter needs to be "a jack of all trades" compared to a 2-3 lift strength specialist, but more variety does not mean *constant* variation — skill with chosen lifts matters, and excessive swapping stalls progress via repeated relearning.
- [CONVENTION] General rule: perform compound free-weight exercises first while fresh; a single weak-point muscle group that (if fatigued first) won't hinder the day's compound-lift performance can also be trained first.
