# MSP Training — Level 2 (part 1) — Volume

> **Source:** *The Muscle & Strength Pyramid: Training* 3e (Helms/Valdez/Morgan), book pp.25-45. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/msp-training.txt` or PDF pp.25-45.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] research-established · [CONVENTION] the authors' specific evidence-based recommendation · [DISPUTED] contested / authors flag uncertainty.

## Framing: volume, intensity, frequency share one pyramid level (p.25-26)

- Volume and intensity are the primary drivers of adaptation; frequency is "closely tied to both" as the primary tool for distributing training dose (p.25). [SCIENCE]
- The three variables are interconnected and share the same Pyramid level because each affects the others: training close to failure increases muscle damage → delays recovery → reduces tolerable volume/frequency; heavy loads are more fatiguing per rep and require more sets for hypertrophy-relevant volume → longer, more fatiguing sessions; concentrating many sets into one session (e.g., 16 sets/muscle in one day vs. split across two) reduces performance over time, forcing lower load/reps as fatigue accumulates → limits effective frequency (p.25). [SCIENCE]
- The best combination of volume/intensity/frequency depends on experience level, goals, schedule, preferences, recovery capacity, and individual response (p.25). [CONVENTION]

## The evidence pyramid (p.26-29)

- Evidence-based practice = combining client needs/abilities/values/preferences/circumstances/individual response with best available scientific evidence, integrated via practitioner experience (p.26). [SCIENCE]
- Hierarchy of evidence quality, strongest at top (p.26-27, figure — see PDF p.27):
  1. Meta-analyses & systematic reviews of controlled trials — statistically combine findings across studies, increasing confidence and detecting real effects despite individual study flaws.
  2. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) — random assignment isolates cause/effect, but small sport-science sample sizes make results prone to noise.
  3. Non-randomized controlled trials / cohort studies — convenience samples risk systematic differences skewing results.
  4. Case-control / cross-sectional studies.
  5. Animal trials & in vitro studies.
  6. Case reports, opinion papers, letters (weakest) — observational; can't isolate cause, only catalogue what happened; useful when higher tiers are lacking but can't tell you if the approach was optimal, only that it didn't prevent success.
- Top three tiers (RCTs, non-randomized CTs, systematic reviews/meta-analyses) allow assessing *causation*, not just correlation (p.27). [SCIENCE]
- Top-tier evidence tells you what works, why, and how confidently to act; lower-tier evidence gives ideas/insight but must be weighed carefully against context — it cannot establish causation, only that an approach did not prevent success (p.28). [SCIENCE]
- The book's guidance is built on highest-quality available research plus the authors' practical experience, organized around: minimum effective dose (ED), maximum ED, and — most crucially — practical ED (the amount of training that produces results without exceeding recovery capacity, which changes over time) (p.28). [CONVENTION]

## Table 2.1 — Summary starting recommendations (p.29-30)

Fractional-set convention (footnote 1): Hypertrophy — sets count as full sets for primary muscles, half sets for secondary muscles. Strength — sets of the tested lift count as full sets; other lifts training the primary/secondary muscles of that lift count as half sets. [CONVENTION]

| | Hypertrophy | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| **Minimum ED** (footnote 2) | 4 sets/muscle/wk, increases w/training status | 1 set/lift/wk, increases w/training status |
| **Maximum ED** (footnote 3) | ~30 sets/muscle/wk | ~5 sets/lift/wk (short-term) |
| **Practical ED** (footnote 4) | 10–20 sets/muscle/wk; higher volume with specialization | 3–5 sets/lift/wk (short-term), 5–10 sets/muscle/wk (long-term) |
| **Load** | ~4–30RM (~30–90% 1RM) | 1–8RM (~80+% 1RM) |
| **RIR** | 4–0 RIR and failure per load/rep combination | ~7–0 RIR per load/rep combination |
| RIR by rep range | 4–6 reps/set: 4–0 RIR; 6–8 reps/set: 3 RIR–failure; 8–12 reps/set: 2 RIR–failure; >12 reps/set: 1 RIR–failure | Load, not RIR, dictates strength. RIR is a function of load — higher loads allow fewer reps and are inherently closer to failure |
| **Frequency** | ≥1x/muscle/wk based on volume; if >11 weekly sets/muscle, increase frequency so no single session exceeds 11 fractional sets/muscle group | 2–6x/lift/wk based on volume; higher frequency gives some benefit; spread sets over as many days as possible with 1–2 direct sets per main lift per session |

Footnote definitions:
- **Minimum ED** = lowest volume producing measurable gains in 2–3 months. Hypertrophy in trained lifters requires low RIR (near/to failure); strength in trained lifters requires high specificity (high load, on the specific lift). Use when ideal training can't occur. Produces progress in novice–intermediate lifters, maintenance in intermediate–advanced. [CONVENTION]
- **Maximum ED** = volume threshold where diminished returns reach a plateau. [SCIENCE]
- **Practical ED** = appropriate range for real-world, long-term training; individual differences/circumstances dictate placement within the range. Higher volumes near max ED advised only for advanced lifters via specialization cycles. Long-term strength gains require hypertrophy, so some hypertrophy-range volume is recommended even for strength goals. [CONVENTION]
- Load footnote: high load is effective for hypertrophy at high RIR, but sets must be ≥4 reps; RIR underestimation is more likely at low loads.
- Frequency footnote: high volume is best distributed with high frequency for hypertrophy; increased frequency benefits strength via more skill practice/movement-quality reps.

## Volume defined: sets, not volume load (p.30-32)

- Volume = work performed; can be expressed as volume load (sets × reps × load) or simply set count — each gives a different perspective (p.30). [SCIENCE]
- Volume load comparisons are frequently misleading:
  - Example: 3×25×100 (50% 1RM) = 7,500 volume load vs. 3×10×140 (70% 1RM) = 4,200 — a 78% higher volume load for the lighter protocol, yet research shows sets of 8–12RM to failure produce similar hypertrophy to sets of 25–35RM despite the volume-load gap (p.30-31, cite [3]); and for strength, three sets of 2–4RM outperform three sets of 8–12RM even with lower total volume load (cite [4]). [SCIENCE]
  - Volume load also breaks down across exercises (10 curls at 20kg vs. 10 leg-press reps at 200kg → 200kg vs 2000kg volume load, not meaningfully comparable) and across individuals (a 47kg female powerlifter's 5×5×70kg moderately-easy bench session ≈ 1750kg vs. a 120kg male's 3×5×125kg ≈ 1875kg — volume load alone says nothing about relative training advancement) (p.31). [SCIENCE]
  - Conclusion: volume load is only meaningfully trackable within the same person, same lift, similar rep range, over time — useful for progress tracking in that narrow context, not informative outside it (p.31). [CONVENTION]
- Because of this, the book defines volume as **set count within a given intensity range** (based on load and proximity to failure), across exercises and individuals — this is the operational definition used throughout (p.31-32). [CONVENTION]
- Note on progression: set volume for an exercise/muscle should increase only based on specific metrics, carefully, and not in large jumps (sometimes decreasing across a career); by contrast, volume load should progress steadily over time — as strength/muscle force increases, volume load rises as a product of sets × reps × load even if sets/reps stay constant (p.31). [CONVENTION]

## Strength vs. hypertrophy relationship (p.32-33)

1. **Strength is a product of skill acquisition, neurological adaptation, and hypertrophy** — strength is a function of muscle mass/structural changes, neurological adaptation, and technical skill with the lift (p.32, cite [5]). [SCIENCE] Strength is specific to a movement (cite [6]) and rep range (cite [3]) — training with loads/rep ranges/exercises similar to the target transfers best. [SCIENCE]
2. **Volume and proximity to failure are important for hypertrophy** — muscle growth is primarily driven by proximity to failure (cite [7]) and number of hard sets performed (cite [8]) (p.32). [SCIENCE] Sets must be close enough to failure to generate sufficient stimulus, and enough sets/week are needed to accumulate that stimulus over time. [SCIENCE]

## When do sets "count" for strength and hypertrophy? (p.32-35)

- A 2024 meta-analysis (cite [8]) found more sets generally increases rate of adaptation — moderately for strength, more so for hypertrophy — but with diminishing returns (p.32). [SCIENCE]
- A 2022 meta-analysis of 747 trained/untrained lifters (cite [9]) on load and hypertrophy/strength (p.33): training to failure, low (<60% 1RM / >15RM), moderate (60-79% 1RM / 9-15RM), and high (≥80% 1RM / ≤8RM) loads produce **similar hypertrophy**. Mechanism: low-load/high-rep sets generate less tension per rep, but accumulated fatigue over more reps recruits more muscle fibers (cite [10]), yielding a similar net hypertrophy stimulus to high-load/low-rep sets. [SCIENCE]
- **Minimum reps-per-set threshold for a set to "count" for hypertrophy: ~4–5 reps.** Below this, the set doesn't last long enough to stimulate growth on equal footing with higher-rep sets (p.33), based on four studies (cites [4, 11-13]):
  - Schoenfeld et al. [4]: greater hypertrophy in some (not all) muscles with 8–12RM vs. 2–4RM.
  - Mangine et al. [11]: relatively similar hypertrophy comparing 10–12RM vs. 3–5RM in most muscles, slight edge to 3–5RM — but confounded: the 10-12RM group rested 1 min vs. 3 min for the 3-5RM group, which may have shortchanged the higher-rep group's gains (p.33-34). [DISPUTED — study-level confound noted by authors]
  - Weiss et al. [12]: quads, 4 sets of 3-5RM vs. 13-15RM vs. 23-25RM, 3x/week for 7 weeks — no significant/meaningful hypertrophy differences between groups.
  - Cumming et al. [13]: within-participant, 3×3-5RM vs. 3×20-25RM in trained individuals — similar quad thickness increases in both legs, no significant difference.
  - Synthesis: ~4-5 reps (or higher) is "probably" the minimum for a heavy set to fully "count" for hypertrophy with appropriate proximity to failure (p.34). [SCIENCE, with the caveat that individual studies conflict and the book explicitly walks through resolving the apparent Mangine/Schoenfeld discrepancy via a rest-period confound]
- For strength, higher loads provide an advantage (cite [9]) and rep count per set matters less than for hypertrophy (p.34): e.g., 80% 1RM (~6-10 reps) and 90% 1RM (~2-4 reps) sets produce similar 1RM increases on average, via complementary mechanisms — 90% is more specific to the 1RM effort (skill/neuromuscular specificity); 80% allows more reps, contributing to movement practice and hypertrophy that supports strength over time (cite [14]) (p.34). [SCIENCE]
- Summary: volume for strength and hypertrophy = counting sets within a given intensity range; the effective range is broader for hypertrophy, narrower for strength, and always modulated by proximity to failure and exercise selection (p.34). [CONVENTION]

## Strength/hypertrophy volume dose-response (p.35-37)

- Meta-analysis (cite [8]) pooling >1000 hypertrophy participants and >2000 strength participants: dose-response relationship exists (more volume → more strength/hypertrophy), but **non-linear** — initial sets give the highest ROI, returns diminish with more sets, eventually no further progress, and past some point more volume becomes detrimental (p.35). [SCIENCE]
- Figure 2.4 (p.35-37, figure — see PDF pp.35-37) — modeled weekly-sets vs. % change curves:
  - **Strength (panel A):** up to 2 sets = very high efficiency, practical issues very unlikely; up to 3–4 sets = high efficiency, practical issues unlikely; up to 5 sets = moderate efficiency, practical issues for some; beyond 5 sets, additional volume unlikely to improve strength in the short term.
  - **Hypertrophy (panel B):** up to ~10 sets = high efficiency, practical issues least likely; up to ~18–19 sets = modest efficiency, practical issues for some; up to ~30 sets = poor efficiency, practical issues for many; beyond 30 sets, data unclear, growth likely plateaus, practical issues very likely.
  - Curve shapes differ: strength rises sharply then plateaus early with low uncertainty; hypertrophy rises quickly then flattens gradually without a clear plateau, with uncertainty increasing steadily as volume rises. [SCIENCE]

### Strength-volume dose-response detail (p.37-39)

- Section header note (p.37-38, "More on Counting Volume"): the meta-analysis [8] validated the **fractional-set method**: for hypertrophy, full set for primary muscles / half set for secondary muscles best fit observed data; for strength, full set for the tested exercise / half set for any other exercise training the same primary or secondary muscles best fit the data (e.g., only back-squat sets count full for back-squat 1RM; deadlifts, leg extensions, leg press, lunges, hack squats count half) (p.38). [SCIENCE]
- **Strength minimum ED = 1 weekly set** (measurable gains) (p.38, cite [8]). [SCIENCE]
- Adding a **2nd set ≈ doubles** the rate of strength gain — the first two sets are the most stimulative/efficient (highest ROI) (p.38). [SCIENCE]
- To repeat that rate of improvement requires roughly **doubling volume to 3–4 sets** — first drop in efficiency (p.38). [SCIENCE]
- **~5th weekly set** = point where the clear beneficial effect of added volume ends on average; strength-gain rate plateaus beyond this (**maximum ED ≈ 5 sets/lift/wk short-term**) (p.38). [SCIENCE]
- Caveats on generalizing this low ceiling (p.38-39):
  - Studies are short (meta-analysis average duration ~10 weeks) (cite [8]). [DISPUTED — book flags this as a real limitation]
  - Strength gains stem from neuromuscular adaptation, skill acquisition, and hypertrophy, which operate on different (and for hypertrophy, slower, further-slowing-with-experience) time courses (cite [9]).
  - ~40% of the meta-analysis's studies were on untrained lifters; higher-volume studies tended to use more indirect (less specific) sets while lower-volume studies were more specific to the tested lift — meaning the diminishing-returns curve for strength may be **exaggerated**, especially for trained lifters. [DISPUTED]
  - Real-world corroboration: trained powerlifters make meaningful 1RM gains for ~6-12 weeks doing just ~3-6 weekly heavy sets (1-5 reps) per lift (cites [15,16]) — demonstrating the power of specificity — but 1RM may plateau after a couple months at the low end (1-3 weekly heavy sets) (cite [15]). A long-term study of ~15,000 recreational lifters doing 1 weekly set to failure per exercise for ~7 years found strength plateaued within 1-2 years (cite [17]) (p.38-39). [SCIENCE]
  - Conclusion: low volumes meaningfully raise strength early-career, short-to-moderate term; higher volumes likely needed to progress past that phase, especially beyond 2-3 months, and long-term strength gains increasingly depend on hypertrophy with training experience (cite [14]) (p.39). [CONVENTION]

### Hypertrophy-volume dose-response detail (p.39-40)

- Plateau for hypertrophy occurs at **much higher volumes** than for strength (p.39). [SCIENCE]
- **Hypertrophy minimum ED ≈ 4 weekly sets** to produce *detectable* hypertrophy in ~10 weeks (vs. strength's 1 set) — this doesn't mean 1-3 sets produce zero growth, just that ~4 sets is needed to reliably measure it in that timeframe (p.39, cite [8]). [SCIENCE]
- Why the higher threshold vs. strength: relative 1RM strength gains are larger and easier to detect than muscle-size gains — e.g., in ~600 untrained lifters, 12-week biceps curl 1RM rose ~54% while cross-sectional area rose ~19% (cite [18]) (p.39). [SCIENCE]
- Dose-response detail (p.39-40, first/second/third "dots" in Figure 2.4B):
  - **4 sets → ~2%** minimal detectable muscle-size change.
  - **~10 sets → ~4%** (first dot — end of the good-ROI range): to double the initial hypertrophy rate requires more than doubling volume (150% volume increase → 100% rate increase) — a loss of efficiency, but practically 4 sets is very low and 10 sets isn't high, so **10 sets/muscle/wk is the top of the efficient range**.
  - **~18-19 sets → ~6%** (second dot): nearly doubling volume again for only a 50% gains increase — practically becoming a time-commitment issue (e.g., needing 3 sets each of squats, lunges, leg press, hack squat, leg extension, split squat in one week just for quads).
  - **~30 sets → ~8%** (third dot — maximum ED): tripling volume (from 10 to 30 sets) to double the average hypertrophy rate — highly inefficient/impractical.
  - Beyond ~30 sets, the relationship didn't technically plateau in the analysis, but uncertainty is so high it's unclear if more sets help — a **functional plateau** (p.40). [SCIENCE, with the "beyond 30" region explicitly flagged DISPUTED/uncertain by the authors]
- Practical takeaway: strength plateaus at low volumes; hypertrophy keeps rising at much higher but far less efficient volumes — but programming shouldn't be based on these figures alone; meta-analyses describe averages over short (~10-week), mixed trained/untrained populations, so the strength curve may be overstated and the hypertrophy curve may be understated for real-world application (p.40). [CONVENTION]

## High-volume training in the real world (p.40-43)

- The referenced meta-analysis [8] used *direct* hypertrophy measures (muscle thickness/CSA via ultrasound or MRI), and studies isolating single/few muscle groups skew training volume toward the measured muscles at the expense of realism (e.g., a study measuring quads/biceps may pile volume onto those while minimizing other muscle groups, sometimes restricting outside training) (p.40-41). [SCIENCE — methodological caveat the book flags explicitly]
- Study lengths in the meta-analysis: ~10 weeks average, 4 weeks shortest, 32 weeks longest, 8 weeks most common (p.41). This means the data show that very high volume focused on ~1-3 muscles for ~2-3 months produces the fastest hypertrophy rates — **not** that everyone should do 30 sets/week for all muscles all the time (p.41). [DISPUTED — explicit authors' caution against overgeneralizing]
- The meta-analysis controlled statistically for proximity-to-failure method (momentary failure vs. perceived failure vs. stopping shy), rest periods, and exercise selection varying between studies — so it captures volume's *theoretical, isolated* effect, not a realistic combined protocol (p.41). [SCIENCE]
- Adherence/selection bias: not everyone volunteers for a study that might assign 40 weekly sets; only completers are analyzed (p.41). [SCIENCE]
- Injury data: a large survey of soldiers weight training in personal time found injury rate **per 1000 training hours** was roughly half for those training ≥166 min/week vs. 60-165 min/week — but because they trained more total hours, annual injury *incidence* was still higher (7.5% vs. 4.4%) (p.41-42, cite [19]). Interpretation: higher volume may improve per-hour resilience but not enough to fully offset greater exposure — a "tortoise-and-hare" dynamic where moderate volumes may yield greater long-term progress via better sustainability (p.41-42). [SCIENCE]
- Individual variation: population averages don't apply uniformly — response depends on sleep, nutrition, stress, prior injury, training status/history, and possibly genetics; what counts as high or low volume shifts across individuals and across time within the same individual (p.42). [SCIENCE]
- **Theoretical dose-response vs. practical inverted-U** (p.42-43, Figure 2.5, figure — see PDF p.43): research shows dose-response with diminishing returns, but in practice the true relationship is an inverted-U — performance improves with volume to a point, plateaus, then *declines* as volume becomes excessive. This inverted-U isn't shown directly in the research but is "inevitable in the real world with sufficiently high training volumes" (p.42). [CONVENTION/DISPUTED — explicitly stated as not empirically demonstrated but inferred as inevitable]
  - Overtraining syndrome / non-functional overreaching are not real concerns for typical lifters, but burnout, injury, and opportunity cost from extremely high volume are — and these factors cannot be captured by training studies like the ones in the meta-analysis (p.42). [CONVENTION]
  - Figure 2.5 stages (figure — see PDF p.43): (1) end of dose-response / beginning of plateau — due to diminishing returns or inability to maintain RIR, exercise selection, needing more frequent deloads; (2) end of plateau / beginning of decreased progress rate — due to actual reduced stimulus, notable RIR reduction, more frequent poor exercise selection, much more frequent deloads, aches/pains; (3) end of decreased progress / beginning of regression — hypothetical injury or burnout leading to training cessation.
  - Conclusion: very high volumes are recommended only in specific cases, typically via specialization cycles (p.42). [CONVENTION]

## Volume recommendations (p.43-45)

- Core directive: **"Do enough to progress, not as much as possible. Only do more when you plateau if you are well recovered, training appropriately close to failure, and you've technically mastered your lifts."** (p.43). [CONVENTION]
- Volume ranges are guidelines, not commandments — volume, frequency, and intensity are interrelated and affect one another (p.43). [CONVENTION]
- Ideal volume depends on recovery capacity, available time, nutrition, energy balance (gaining/dieting), life stress, travel, etc. — start within the recommended ranges, then adjust based on response (p.44). [CONVENTION]
- The recommendations derive from the 2024 meta-analysis on fractional set volume per week per lift/muscle for strength and hypertrophy (cite [8]) (p.44). Because the theoretical plateau points differ vastly between strength and hypertrophy, and because most trainees care about long-term (not just 2-3 month) progress, the book adds a **practical ED** on top of the research-derived min/max ED: for hypertrophy it sits below the maximum ED; for strength it sits around the maximum ED, with added hypertrophy-range volume recommended even for strength goals to support long-term strength gains (p.44). [CONVENTION]
- Approach: start within the recommended range based on goals/timeframe/schedule/experience; add volume only once sleep, recovery, nutrition, intensity, and frequency are all dialed in and progress has still stalled (p.44). [CONVENTION]

### Table 2.2 — Volume recommendations for hypertrophy and strength (p.45)

Fractional-set convention (footnote 1, same as Table 2.1): hypertrophy — full set for primary muscles, half for secondary; strength — full set for the tested lift, half set for other lifts training the same primary/secondary muscles.

| | Hypertrophy | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| **Minimum ED** (footnote 2) | 4 sets/muscle/wk, increases w/training status | 1 set/lift/wk, increases w/training status |
| **Maximum ED** (footnote 3) | ~30 sets/muscle/wk | ~5 sets/lift/wk (short-term) |
| **Practical ED** (footnote 4) | 10–20 sets/muscle/wk; higher volume with specialization | 3–5 sets/lift/wk (short-term), 5–10 sets/muscle/wk (long-term) |

Footnote definitions (identical intent to Table 2.1):
- **Minimum ED** = lowest volume producing measurable gains in 2-3 months; hypertrophy in trained lifters requires low RIR (near/to failure), strength in trained lifters requires high specificity (high load, specific lift); use when ideal training can't occur; produces progress in novice-intermediate, maintenance in intermediate-advanced. [CONVENTION]
- **Maximum ED** = volume threshold where diminished returns reach a plateau. [SCIENCE]
- **Practical ED** = appropriate range for real-world, long-term training; individual differences/circumstances dictate placement; higher volumes near maximum ED advised only for advanced lifters via specialization cycles; long-term strength gains require hypertrophy, so some hypertrophy-range volume is recommended.
