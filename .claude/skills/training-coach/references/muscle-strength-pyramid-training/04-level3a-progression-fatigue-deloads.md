# MSP Training — Level 3 (part 1) — Progression, Fitness-Fatigue, Deloads

> **Source:** *The Muscle & Strength Pyramid: Training* 3e (Helms/Valdez/Morgan), book pp.83-114. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/msp-training.txt` or PDF pp.83-114.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] research-established · [CONVENTION] the authors' specific evidence-based recommendation · [DISPUTED] contested / authors flag uncertainty.

## Progressive Overload (p.84-85)

- [SCIENCE] "Overload" = a stimulus sufficient to induce adaptation — something that challenges homeostasis and signals the body to adapt (p.84). Adaptation continues only as long as training keeps providing overload.
- [CONVENTION] Key reframe: progressive overload is **not** the proactive act of always adding sets/reps/load. "If you are able to progress, it's because overload occurred" — progression should be read in reverse, as a *consequence* of overload, not a checklist to force every session (p.84-85, citing refs [2,3]).
- [SCIENCE] Whether a given stimulus is still overload depends on what you are **currently adapted to**, not what you previously lifted (p.84). Example: 5 reps @60kg is overload for a true novice; after repeated exposure, the same 5RM becomes their 10RM/15RM/17-18RM and stops being overload — not abruptly, but once full adaptation to that stimulus occurs.
- [CONVENTION] Practical translation of progressive overload by goal (p.85):
  - Strength: establish an appropriate volume (may change across a plan) and **increase load** as you get stronger, to maintain intensity (e.g., a load starting at 80%1RM will effectively become 70%, 60%, 50%1RM if never increased).
  - Hypertrophy: establish a given volume (can fluctuate) and **increase load or reps** as you progress, to maintain proximity to failure (otherwise 1 RIR silently becomes 3, 4, 5 RIR).
- [SCIENCE] Eventually plateaus are inevitable as training status advances; the rest of Level 3 addresses diagnosing and resolving them.

## Training Status (p.85-90)

- [CONVENTION] Training status is classified not by competitive level or strength/muscularity relative to others, but by **how quickly you can currently make progress in the gym** (p.85). It exists on a spectrum from first session to unknown genetic potential, and can differ by body region for the same person (e.g., novice legs / advanced upper body).
- Table 3.1 — Training status definitions & estimated average rates of progress (p.86):

| Status | Definition | Typical duration |
|---|---|---|
| Novice | Able to add load and/or reps each time a lift is repeated within the same week or week-to-week | Can last years, but may be as short as 6 months with consistent, progressive training, sufficient recovery, and effort |
| Intermediate | Progress slows: may add reps in the 10-20 rep range week-to-week, or add load in lower rep ranges month-to-month | Most reach this stage by year 1; may remain here through years 4-5; many never progress past it |
| Advanced | Gains much slower: a rep or two in the 10-20 rep range month-to-month, or small load/rep increases over longer timeframes; progress becomes increasingly hard as genetic potential is approached | Most lifters never reach this phase |

- [SCIENCE] Rationale for the load-vs-rep distinction: load increments (especially on single-joint lifts) typically require a larger absolute increase in force capacity than adding reps, and going from 20→21 reps at a given load (+5%) is a smaller relative improvement than 5→6 reps (+20%) (p.86).
- [DISPUTED/CONVENTION caveat] Training status is not the only determinant of progress rate — genetics, starting point, and recovery environment matter too, so the novice/intermediate/advanced boxes are approximations, not hard cutoffs (p.86).

### Does Volume Need to Increase with Training Status? (p.86-90)

- [DISPUTED] Whether a fixed number of weekly sets eventually becomes insufficient as training status advances is explicitly framed as a debated question (p.86). Some argue experienced lifters generate more fatigue/stimulus per set and may even need less volume.
- [SCIENCE] Key distinction drawn: the **minimum effective dose (ED)** to advance training status increases over time, but that does not necessarily mean the **ideal volume** increases too (p.87).
  - [SCIENCE] Steele et al.: ~15,000 recreational lifters doing 1 weekly set/exercise to failure over ~7 years plateaued in strength within 1-2 years, gaining only up to ~50% from baseline (p.87, ref [5]).
  - [SCIENCE] Hubal et al.: ~600 untrained men/women doing 9 sets/muscle (not to failure) twice weekly (18 weekly sets) for biceps saw average 1RM curl gains of 54% in 12 weeks, with the highest responders gaining 250% (p.87, ref [6]). Conclusion: one weekly set to failure is not enough for most novices to ever reach intermediate status.
  - [SCIENCE] Cross-discipline support: elite cyclists training 10-14h/week can maintain performance on 40-70% of that volume if a single weekly high-intensity interval is retained, but untrained/older/sedentary people given that same reduced protocol would still be training at more than double their required time/intensity to maximize gains (p.87, refs [7-9]) — illustrating that minimum ED scales with training status across endurance disciplines too.
  - [SCIENCE] Pelland meta-regression: ~4 weekly sets is enough to produce measurable hypertrophy in 2-3 months across untrained and trained lifters (p.87-88, ref [10]).
  - [SCIENCE] Experienced strength athletes can temporarily "peak" strength on a low volume of heavy competition-lift sets, but strength plateaus after a few months at that volume, and slight muscle-size reductions have been observed in National-level weightlifters during reduced-volume peaking phases even at volumes above the hypertrophy minimum ED (p.88, refs [11-14]).
- [SCIENCE] Latella et al. analyzed >9,000 drug-tested powerlifters (Open Powerlifting database, up to 17 years of competitive history): totals rose ~10% in year 1 of competing, then took another ~9 years to add a further 10% (20% total over a decade) — despite athletes training 6-10h/week across 4-6 sessions (p.88, refs [15-19]).
- (figure — see PDF p.89, Figure 3.1: "Your Genetic Limit as an Asymptote" — progress curve flattening from novice through advanced against a genetic-limit ceiling.)
- [CONVENTION] Because slowed progress can *feel* like a plateau even when it isn't, many lifters respond by adding volume; whether that's wise is context-dependent (e.g. reasonable for a competitive bodybuilder chasing marginal gains, provided it doesn't cause burnout, injury, or unacceptable life sacrifice) (p.89-90).

## The Fitness-Fatigue Model (p.90-93)

- [SCIENCE] Training simultaneously produces (1) fitness — the adaptations sought — and (2) fatigue, which temporarily masks those gains (p.90-91).
- [SCIENCE] Fatigue sources and timescales (p.91):
  - Short-term: energy-store depletion and metabolite accumulation impair contraction strength; central/neuromuscular fatigue from cardiorespiratory and muscular feedback. Resolves within minutes to at most a day or two with adequate nutrition/recovery.
  - Longer-term: muscle damage (mechanical + metabolic stress, inflammation from remodeling/repair — felt as DOMS) is the primary cause of longer fatigue (refs [23,24]). Driven by total volume, proximity to failure, novelty, eccentric forces, and long-muscle-length forces.
  - [SCIENCE] Repeated bout effect: muscle damage substantially decreases with repeated exposure to a given training dose as experience/familiarity accrue — but fatigue never disappears entirely; any overload-inducing training still causes some damage. In trained individuals DOMS/damage typically resolve within days to a week; in completely untrained individuals hit with an inappropriately hard session it can take weeks.
- [SCIENCE] Core equation: **Performance = Fitness − Fatigue** (p.91, ref [25]).
- (figure — see PDF p.91, Figure 3.2: conceptual Fitness-Fatigue-Performance curves across before/post-training/after-recovery.)
- [SCIENCE] Fatigue fades quickly after a session while fitness fades slowly — the basis of the "two-factor model" for distributing training across a week (different lifts/muscle groups on different days, days off) to manage fatigue while still training hard enough for overload (p.92).
- (figure — see PDF p.92, Figure 3.3: fitness/fatigue/performance changes over a typical training week.)
- [SCIENCE] Balancing fitness and fatigue block-to-block is described as "a hallmark of effective programming" (p.92, ref [26]). Sustained high training dose in pursuit of overload can, in extreme cases, cause Overtraining Syndrome (rare in resistance training alone) or, more commonly, non-functional overreaching (performance drops, injury risk rises) — motivating proactive deloads.

## Overreaching and Overtraining Syndrome (p.93-98)

- [SCIENCE] Overtraining Syndrome (OTS) is currently understood as a decline in performance lasting **months or longer**, with or without secondary hormonal, psychological, immune, or sleep symptoms (p.93, ref [27]). Colloquial lifter use of "overtraining" for any performance dip is a misnomer.
- [SCIENCE] True OTS is rare, and functionally non-existent in sufficiently-fed lifters who only lift; it appears more often in endurance/team-sport athletes, in concurrent aerobic+lifting or high-intensity "functional fitness" contexts, and cases that look like OTS in underfed lifters are more accurately caused by low energy availability (p.93-94, refs [28-30]).
- [SCIENCE] The fatigue spectrum, from normal to pathological (p.94):
  - Normal fatigue: short-term performance reduction resolving before the next similar session.
  - Functional Overreaching (FOR): short-term dip/stagnation (days-weeks) followed by a **supra-baseline** rebound after recovery.
  - Non-Functional Overreaching (NFOR): longer decline (weeks-months) **not** followed by a supra-baseline rebound.
  - Overtraining Syndrome: far end of the spectrum, months+ decline, possible secondary symptoms.
- (figure — see PDF p.95, Figure 3.4: FOR — dose spike → fitness/fatigue rise, fatigue masks performance, dose reduced before harm → new higher performance plateau ["PRs are had"].)
- (figure — see PDF p.96, Figure 3.5: NFOR→OTS — dose kept high despite falling performance → fitness itself declines → dose finally cut, but recovery now takes weeks before fitness can rebuild.)
- [SCIENCE] Evidence that true OTS is rare in lifters (p.96-97):
  - Only 15% of endurance athletes reporting fatigue/declining-performance symptoms met formal OTS criteria in one study (ref [31]).
  - Grandou et al. (2020) systematic review of 22 resistance-training overtraining-induction studies: 10 of 22 failed to reduce performance at all (some even improved); of the 12 that did show declines, 4 lacked follow-up and the remaining 8's longest follow-up was 8 weeks — too short to confirm true OTS (needs months). Conclusion: OTS had never been reported in the resistance-training literature (ref [28]).
  - Bell et al. (2020) review interpreted one study (Fry et al. [32]) as showing full OTS in some participants: daily maximal 1RM-squat-machine singles-to-failure testing for **two weeks straight**, requiring 2-8 weeks off afterward — described as a "truly ludicrous protocol," not something that occurs incidentally.
  - Strength is the physiological quality *least* and *last* impaired by excess training load; secondary OTS signs (hormonal disruption, sleep impairment, low motivation/mood, immune suppression) often appeared in these studies even without measurable performance decline. Injured participants also tend to drop out of such studies, likely under-representing true incidence (p.97, ref [33]).
- [CONVENTION] Practical takeaway: excessive training dose does not "sneak up" on you — warning signs (day-to-day performance volatility, hard-to-sustain motivation, aches/pains, elevated DOMS, disrupted sleep, more frequent illness, spillover into life stress) will be apparent; responding early to these signs after a volume/intensity bump is the appropriate cue to manage fatigue (p.97-98).

## The Biopsychosocial Model (p.98-99)

- [SCIENCE] The Fitness-Fatigue model is a useful but isolated/incomplete picture — mathematical attempts to use it to predict real performance changes have failed (p.98, ref [34]).
- [SCIENCE] Social and psychological factors — not just biological training load — shape the stress response and therefore how much fitness/fatigue a given program produces; this is the biopsychosocial model of stress (p.98, ref [35,36]). Non-training factors that modulate the response: age, genetics, prior training experience, sleep, motivation, cumulative life stress, nutrition, arousal (p.98-99).
- [SCIENCE] Negatively perceived life stress can reduce the magnitude of strength development and impair post-training recovery (p.99, refs [37,38]).
- [CONVENTION] Practical implication: response to training is individual and not always predictable — good planning is essential but must be paired with flexibility/reactivity.

## Deloads, Tapers, and Introductory Cycles (p.99-108)

### What Are Deloads? (p.99-100)

- [DISPUTED] Deloads have very little direct research support; they rest on established general principles (fitness-fatigue, biopsychosocial) plus expert opinion and indirect/observational research, not dedicated deload trials (p.99).
- [CONVENTION] Definition, from Bell et al. (2022) interview study of 18 high-level powerlifting/weightlifting/bodybuilding coaches: a deload is "a point within the overall training programme where training demand was intentionally and systematically reduced" (p.99, ref [39]) — typically a short (usually one-week) period of reduced volume and/or intensity between blocks of harder training.
- [CONVENTION] Coaches' two main reasons for deloading: manage fatigue/promote recovery; facilitate long-term progression. Believed benefits: facilitates adaptation, promotes physical/mental regeneration, enables subsequent progressive training, helps prevent injury and burnout (p.99-100).

### Tapers (p.100-101)

- [SCIENCE] Tapers are intentional reductions in training dose implemented close to competition, to let fatigue dissipate and performance peak; among competitive lifters they primarily reduce **volume**, not intensity (p.100, refs [40-43]). Contrast with deloads: deloads sit between training blocks to enable further progress; tapers are timed before testing/competition to maximize acute strength.
- [SCIENCE] Tapers following a period of intentional overreaching consistently produce acute strength gains, averaging ~2-4% improvement in maximal strength, though magnitude varies with taper structure (p.100, ref [44]).
- [DISPUTED] Effect of tapering style (short/sharp vs. gradual volume reduction) on hypertrophy can be positive, neutral, or even negative (p.100, ref [45]); one study found a taper increased power output while simultaneously decreasing muscle size (ref [14]) — acceptable for meet-day strength peaking but undesirable if deloads occur frequently.
- [CONVENTION] **How to taper** — evidence-based guidelines (p.101, ref [46]):
  - Volume: reduce substantially, usually **50-80%**, to let muscle damage/fatigue dissipate.
  - Load (intensity): maintain or even slightly increase, to preserve the specific skill of maximal-strength expression.
  - Duration scales with prior fatigue: a long/hard overreaching block may need a taper spanning several weeks or a whole block; a shorter/less-fatiguing block may need only a one-week taper; minimal accumulated fatigue may need just 2-3 days off before testing/competition.
  - Caveat: too deep or too long a volume cut risks minor detraining (reduced work capacity and muscle size) — this is precisely why tapers ≠ deloads; deloads shouldn't pull volume back as far as a taper.

### Taking Time Off Completely: Desensitization/Resensitization (p.101-104)

- [DISPUTED] "Desensitization"/"anabolic resistance" — the idea that prolonged stimulus exposure shrinks adaptation magnitude, used by some coaches to justify full-rest deloads — has mixed evidence:
  - [SCIENCE] Jacko et al.: leg press/extension 3x/week for 4 weeks, then 10 days off. Both fiber types grew during training; after the 10-day break, muscle size was **maintained** and anabolic signaling (which had progressively decreased during training) **returned to baseline** (p.101, ref [47]). Supports resensitization framing, but the book cautions muscle-damage-repair and hypertrophy-remodeling signaling pathways overlap in ways not yet fully understood, and the repeated bout effect might be structurally necessary for future growth — so it's unclear whether deliberately resensitizing via time off improves long-term outcomes (p.102).
  - [SCIENCE] Ogasawara et al. study 1: untrained young men training continuously for 15 weeks achieved the **same** bench 1RM and pec/triceps hypertrophy gains as a group doing 6 weeks on / 3 weeks off / 6 weeks on (fewer total training weeks, same result) (p.102, ref [48]).
  - [SCIENCE] Ogasawara et al. follow-up (24 weeks): three 6-week training blocks with two 3-week detraining periods produced similar gains to continuous training — no net advantage for periodic full breaks over 24 weeks in initially untrained lifters (p.102, ref [49]).
  - [CONVENTION] Practical takeaway: novice/early-intermediate lifters regain lost ground rapidly after time off — reassuring for anyone worried a deload, vacation, or forced break will meaningfully hurt gains (p.102).

### How to Deload (p.102-108)

- [SCIENCE] Coleman et al. (2024) — first direct deload RCT: trained men/women did either a continuous 9-week hypertrophy program or the same program with **week 5 taken off completely** (p.102-103, ref [50]).
  - Both groups gained similar muscle size, power, and endurance.
  - The deload (full-week-off) group gained **less strength** and had **more soreness**.
  - Context: 4x/week training, 10-20 sets/muscle group, to failure — nearly all participants reported training harder than ever, yet almost none felt they needed a deload, and most planned to resume normal training within days of finishing.
  - Many in the deload group felt **lethargic**, not refreshed, after the week off — plausibly because they didn't need to deload and instead mildly detrained.
  - [SCIENCE] In contrast to tapers, training cessation beyond 3 days in well-trained lifters sometimes actually **decreases** strength (p.103, ref [51]) — supporting reduced-volume deloads over complete rest weeks as the safer default.
- [CONVENTION] What determines the need for a deload: the goal, training status, exercise selection, total training dose, individual recovery (p.103).
- [SCIENCE/DISPUTED] Coleman's findings challenge the belief that hard/challenging-but-typical training automatically requires periodic deloads to prevent hidden fatigue from undermining gains — no evidence that functional overreaching produces faster gains than hard training that never induces overreaching; over typical study durations (6-12 weeks) even very high-volume programs haven't been shown to generate enough fatigue to require a deload for benefit (p.103).
- [CONVENTION] Conclusion: deloads are **not pointless**, but should be **as-needed, not pre-planned** for most lifters (p.103-104). Most fatigue is manageable via days off, sleep, a recovery-supporting diet, and stress management — recovery and deloading are not the same thing; you don't need a full deload just to recover. Typical lifters train 3-12h/week, leaving 156-165h to recover — the average recreational lifter usually has no structural need for a week off every month. Exception: competitive athletes training 5+ times/week at 1.5-3h/session near failure may genuinely need to deload more regularly — but an as-needed approach covers both cases.
- [CONVENTION] **Reactive deload protocol** — weekly self-assessment (Table 3.2, p.105), Y/N checklist covering both training and rest days:
  1. Dreading the gym?
  2. Sleeping worse than normal?
  3. Loads or reps at the same load decreasing from last training cycle?
  4. Stress worse than normal?
  5. Aches/pains worse than normal?
  - **0-1 "yes" answers → carry on.**
  - **2+ "yes" answers → consider a deload.**
  - Note: a couple of "yes" answers doesn't mean you're overreaching/OTS — just less recovered than usual heading into the next week.
- [CONVENTION] **Standard deload protocol** (broad principles borrowed from tapering but more conservative, since the goal is fatigue reduction *without* detraining) (p.105):
  - **Volume:** reduce sets by **~30-50%** across all exercises (note: less aggressive than a taper's 50-80% cut).
  - **Intensity:** maintain normal loading and proximity to failure.
  - **Frequency:** keep the same number of sessions per week.
- [CONVENTION] **Targeted/localized deload variant** (p.106): if recovery status differs by body region (e.g., general "yes" only to aches/pains, localized to lumbar), deload or remove only the exercises stressing that area (e.g., cut deadlifts/back squats, keep hack squats/leg extensions/leg curls/hip thrusts at normal volume). Strategies can be mixed — e.g., generally fatigued + localized lumbar soreness: cut squats/deadlifts entirely, trim a few sets off other lower-body lifts, reduce upper body sets symmetrically by 30-50%.
- [CONVENTION] Frequency/intensity can also be adjusted in a deload when practical: e.g., collapse a 4-day upper/lower split into 2 days (one upper, one lower) if halved-volume sessions feel like a poor use of a gym commute; or shift to higher reps for movements where heavier loads (but not higher reps) provoke aches/pains (p.106). Overall framing: "deloading is about smart fatigue management, not ticking boxes" (p.106).

### Introductory Cycles (p.106-108)

- [CONVENTION] An intro cycle ("intro week") sits at the **start** of a new/harder training block (as opposed to a deload, which sits **after** a hard block) — it acclimates the lifter to a new/higher training dose and its fatigue before the "real" block begins, avoiding crippling DOMS that would otherwise blunt execution of the intended dose (p.106-107).
- [CONVENTION] Intro week protocol (p.107):
  - Perform **~75% of planned volume**.
  - Train slightly further from failure than the main block will call for.
  - Keep intensity and movement patterns consistent with the upcoming program (i.e., bridge, don't diverge).
- [CONVENTION] Deload vs. intro week — same tool family, opposite orientation: deloads recover you from what you already did; intro weeks protect you from what's coming (p.107). Typical placement when transitioning between a high-volume/lower-load ("volume") phase and a low-volume/higher-load ("load") phase:
  - Volume Phase → **Deload** → Load Phase
  - Load Phase → **Intro Week** → Volume Phase

## Periodization (p.108-112)

- [CONVENTION] Definition: periodization is the structured organization of training into distinct time blocks ("periods") sequenced to produce better long-term adaptation while avoiding stagnation and injury; at its core it is about **planned variation** — training with zero variation in volume/intensity/focus over time is "non-periodized" (p.108).
- [CONVENTION] Basic terminology (p.108-109, refs [52,53]):
  - **Microcycle** = a week of training (one cycle of a training split).
  - **Mesocycle** = a block of training lasting **1-2 months**.
  - **Macrocycle** = multiple, successive, planned mesocycles over a longer period (typically a season/year), often building to a competition.
- [DISPUTED] Periodization theory is described as genuinely contested: rationale, efficacy, application, model definitions, and even the definition of "periodization" itself are debated (p.109, refs [36,53]); it's disputed whether true multi-year periodization is even studied, since "periodization research" typically spans only 8-12 weeks while periodization is meant to span years (e.g. an Olympic cycle) (p.109).
- Named periodization models the book lists without endorsing any single one as necessary (p.109): linear ("classic"/"Western"), reverse linear, undulating/non-linear (daily or weekly variants), block, conjugate, mixed.
- [CONVENTION] Framing for practical purposes: for pure strength/hypertrophy goals, most of periodization's debate/complexity is irrelevant — only two interrelated concepts matter, and they more accurately fall under **programming**: **linearity** and **variation** (p.109).

### Periodization Versus Programming (p.109-110)

- [CONVENTION] Periodization = long-term training planning; **programming** = manipulation of training variables over the short-to-mid term. Given the unpredictability of adaptation, detailed plans should arguably be constrained to short/mid-term horizons; long-term plans (competition timing, deficit/surplus windows) are fine at a coarse level, but planning specific sets/reps/exercises a year out has little value (p.109-110).
- [CONVENTION] The book argues most "periodization research" is more accurately the **study of programming**, since it examines manipulation within a microcycle (daily undulation), between microcycles (weekly undulation), or across 2-3 mesocycles (block periodization) (p.110, ref [53]).
- [SCIENCE] Moesgaard et al. (2022) meta-analysis, 35 studies, ~1000 participants: **periodized programs produced greater 1RM increases than non-periodized programs, regardless of the specific periodization model used** (p.110, ref [54]).
  - [CONVENTION] Definition used in that literature: non-periodized = static volume, intensity, and frequency throughout (e.g., 3x4-6RM on the same 3 exercises, same 3 days/week, for the whole program) — overload can still occur (increasing load once reps exceed the target range), but microcycle structure never changes; "the program is the training split."
  - Periodized = contains variation — e.g., splitting an 8-week protocol into two 4-week mesocycles with different rep ranges, or varying rep ranges week-to-week/day-to-day.

### Linearity and Variation: The Core of Periodized Programming (p.110-112)

- [CONVENTION] Linearity typically refers to progressing from high-volume/lower-intensity training → low-volume/higher-intensity training, mirroring the classic hypertrophy-to-strength progression (p.110):
  - Early blocks: more sets, moderate reps, lower loads (hypertrophy focus).
  - Later blocks: fewer sets, lower reps, heavier loads (strength specificity).
- [CONVENTION] Even undulating programs often display an underlying linear trend over the full period, e.g. (p.111 example):
  - Weeks 1-4: Mon 3x8-12, Wed 3x8-10, Fri 3x6-8.
  - Weeks 5-8: Mon 3x6-8, Wed 3x4-6, Fri 3x2-4.
  - This still reflects a gradual shift toward heavier, more specific training (linearity), despite day-to-day undulation.
- [SCIENCE] Why **variation** helps (p.111-112):
  1. Performance/recovery: varied training is associated with better performance and fewer incidences of OTS/illness than monotonous training in sport science broadly (ref [36]).
  2. Motor learning: "varied practice" aids skill retention more than constant practice — most notably for open skills (e.g., basketball), somewhat for closed skills too (e.g., lifting) (ref [56]).
  - [SCIENCE/DISPUTED nuance] Moesgaard's meta-analysis found undulating periodization (frequent rep/load variation) produced **greater strength gains in trained lifters specifically**, but not in untrained lifters — plausibly because untrained lifters don't need extra complexity to drive early-stage motor learning (p.112, ref [54]).
- [SCIENCE/DISPUTED] Why **linearity** may help (p.112):
  1. Bigger muscles produce more force (ref [20]) — hypertrophy work preceding strength work could raise the ceiling for later peak 1RM; but Moesgaard found periodized programs' hypertrophy advantage over non-periodized was **non-significant and trivial** in magnitude, so this may not be the primary driver over shorter timeframes.
  2. Specificity — more likely explanation: periodized protocols have lifters training heavier, more test-like, closer to the 1RM test itself, leveraging the principle of specificity.
  - [DISPUTED] Some researchers argue periodized programs' 1RM benefit can't be confidently separated from simply training close to the test (specificity confound) (ref [57]); others argue variation itself is the active ingredient (ref [58]). The book notes these aren't mutually exclusive — without variation there is no linearity — and treats "periodized programming includes both elements" as the practical takeaway regardless of which mechanism dominates.

## Programming for Strength (intro) (p.112-113)

- [SCIENCE] Zourdos et al. compared two daily-undulating powerlifting programs (squat/bench/deadlift, 3x/week) differing only in day order (p.112-113, ref [59]):
  - Group 1: Mon 3-5x8 @75%1RM → Wed 3xAMRAP @85-95%1RM → Fri 3-5 singles @80-90%1RM.
  - Group 2: identical sessions, Wed and Fri swapped.
  - Group 2 (moderate-intensity singles placed **between** the high-volume and AMRAP days) achieved **greater 1RM increases**. Mechanism: Group 1's most fatiguing session (high volume, Monday) was followed just two days later by the hardest-effort session (AMRAP near-max), likely while still carrying fatigue/damage; Group 2 buffered the AMRAP day with a lighter session on either side, improving recovery into the highest-specificity day (the one most likely to drive 1RM gains).
- [CONVENTION] Takeaway: **session order within the week matters** — programming should consider each session's downstream effect on recovery for subsequent sessions.
- [CONVENTION] For strength, effective programming consists of (p.113):
  1. Establishing an appropriate training dose and frequency.
  2. Ensuring specificity occurs at the appropriate time (linearity and variation).
  3. Ordering and distributing training within/between weeks to facilitate recovery and thus strength.
- The book flags that it will next present a programming system moving from higher-volume/less-specific training toward lower-volume/more-specific training to peak strength while maintaining hypertrophy, adaptable to training status with built-in progression monitoring (p.113) — covered in the following unit, not detailed here.

## Programming for Hypertrophy (intro) (p.113-114)

- [DISPUTED] Moesgaard's meta-analysis found periodized programs did **not** meaningfully outperform non-periodized programs for hypertrophy (p.113-114, ref [54]) — but the book cautions against concluding periodization offers no hypertrophy benefit from this, since all studies in that analysis were strength-focused, not hypertrophy-specific programs; periodization is designed to improve athletic/strength performance, with hypertrophy work as one phase in service of that goal, not an end in itself.
- [DISPUTED] Several studies suggest varying rep ranges beyond the classic 8-12 "hypertrophy range" may yield slightly greater muscle growth, though the book flags this as **not conclusive** (p.114, refs [60-62]); the mechanism (differential emphasis of hypertrophy pathways, type I vs. II fiber stimulus, or another factor) is unresolved.
