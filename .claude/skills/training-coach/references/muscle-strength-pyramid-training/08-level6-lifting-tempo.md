# MSP Training — Level 6 — Lifting Tempo

> **Source:** *The Muscle & Strength Pyramid: Training* 3e (Helms/Valdez/Morgan), book pp.205-222. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/msp-training.txt` or PDF pp.205-222.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] research-established · [CONVENTION] the authors' specific evidence-based recommendation · [DISPUTED] contested / authors flag uncertainty.

## Overview & framing (p.205-206)

- Tempo = the speed of performing a lift. This is the final/least-weighted level of The Pyramid; the authors' stated goal is to "help you avoid pitfalls rather than provide critical advice" — tempo is framed as low-leverage relative to earlier levels (adherence, volume/intensity/frequency, progression, exercise selection, rest periods). [CONVENTION]
- Most lifters will do fine using an "intuitive tempo"; tempo manipulation only matters in specific situations (p.205).
- Three phases of every rep (p.205):
  - **Concentric**: lifting phase, muscle shortens to overcome resistance.
  - **Eccentric**: lowering phase, muscle lengthens to control resistance.
  - **Transition**: brief eccentric→concentric changeover.
- Some lifts start concentric (deadlift from floor, machine lifts, curls); others start eccentric (squat, bench, RDL from rack, good morning). Same muscles act in both phases of a given lift (p.205).
- Light loads can be moved fast or deliberately slowed; heavy loads move slowly regardless of intent because you can't easily overcome their mass on the concentric. Eccentric of a heavy load can be controlled at almost any speed (p.205).

## Eccentric phase duration (p.206-207)

- Mechanistic basis: muscles are **~40% stronger eccentrically than concentrically** on average (p.207) [SCIENCE, cite 5]. Active lengthening "loads" muscle structure like a stretched rubber band, making eccentric contractions stronger and more energy-efficient than concentric (p.206) [SCIENCE, cite 4]. This is why concentric failure (not eccentric failure) is the typical failure mode in max lifts, e.g. powerlifting meet third attempts (p.206).
- Because concentric strength is normally the limiting factor, eccentric speed is a free variable to manipulate — the question is whether to (p.206).
- **Amdi & King 2025 meta-analysis** (9 studies, 166 participants; all 9 assessed 1RM, 5 hypertrophy, 3 jump height) (p.206-207) [SCIENCE, cite 6]:
  - Faster eccentric tempos (1-2s) → greater jump height gains than slower (3-6s).
  - Effect on **strength**: slight lean toward slower eccentrics but "generally uncertain" overall [DISPUTED]. Slower eccentrics showed more clearly greater strength gains specifically in (a) volume-load-matched studies and (b) trained-lifter studies — categories that overlapped substantially, muddying causal attribution (p.207).
    - Plausible mechanism: with matched volume load, slowing the eccentric increases set difficulty/proximity to failure, raising the stimulus (p.207).
    - In the other 5 (non-volume-matched) studies, groups matched sets and trained to failure instead, adjusting load or reps to hold a target rep range/load — so long as eccentric duration isn't excessively slow (>3-6s), mechanical work ends up similar between fast/slow groups (p.207): "a set of 20 reps that each last two seconds is quite similar to a set of 10 reps that each last four seconds."
    - Caveat: results may partly be a study-design artifact — 7 of 8 relevant studies restricted concentric velocity, and faster eccentrics can enhance elastic energy storage that speeds/strengthens the following concentric (p.207-208) [SCIENCE, cite 7].
  - Effect on **hypertrophy**: uncertain and likely not meaningful; more research needed (p.209) [DISPUTED].
- **Recommendation — strength** [CONVENTION]: perform eccentrics as fast as possible while maintaining technical control (naturally slower under heavy load) (p.207-208). Exception: purposefully slow eccentrics (3-6 seconds) can be useful when pain, discomfort, or burnout prevents heavy loading — slowing the eccentric extracts a slightly greater stimulus from a lighter load (p.208, also summary p.215).
- **Recommendation — hypertrophy** [CONVENTION]: 1-4 seconds under muscular control; tempo likely doesn't matter much as long as the eccentric is controlled and it is you (not gravity) doing the lowering — e.g., dropping deadlifts or letting a dumbbell fall between alternating curls cuts effective training volume in half by skipping the eccentric (p.209).

## Supramaximal eccentric training (p.207-208)

- Definition: using specialized equipment/technique to load the eccentric phase above concentric 1RM — e.g., weight releasers (detach at bottom of squat/bench for one supramaximal eccentric per set), motorized systems adding resistance every rep, spotter-assisted concentric with above-1RM load, or bilateral-concentric/unilateral-eccentric on machines like leg press (p.207-208).
- **Strength**: [DISPUTED/SCIENCE-mixed]. Buskard et al. 2018 meta-analysis (5 studies) found a slight, non-significant advantage for supramaximal eccentrics on lower-body 1RM vs. traditional training (p.208) [SCIENCE, cite 8]. Subsequent/non-included studies split: some show no meaningful difference (cites 9-11), others show slightly greater strength gains for supramaximal training (cites 12-15). Overall: "neutral to positive... but the effects are underwhelming" (p.208). Proposed mechanism for the weak effect: since the goal is raising concentric 1RM, eccentric loads above concentric 1RM add little because the trained quality (elastic/eccentric capacity) isn't what's being tested.
- **Hypertrophy**: research even more limited than for strength; authors state you "shouldn't anticipate a beneficial effect since RIR and volume are the key variables that influence muscle growth, not load" (p.208) [CONVENTION, restating Level 2 principle].
- **Practical considerations** [CONVENTION] (p.208): cannot confidently recommend supramaximal eccentric training without more research. If an advanced lifter wants to try it for strength, be careful — it is potentially dangerous (especially free weights), requires a competent spotter and safeties, and produces more muscle damage, soreness, and acute strength suppression than normal training.

## Concentric phase duration (p.208-210)

- Force = mass × acceleration (p.208) [SCIENCE, cite 16]: intentionally slowing a concentric with light/moderate load necessarily means producing less force.
- **Hermes & Fry 2023 meta-analysis** (625 participants, ~24 studies): regardless of age or training experience, purposefully slowing the concentric phase produces poorer strength gains (p.208-209) [SCIENCE, cite 17]. Reasoning: with a sufficiently heavy load, deliberately reduced force means the load literally won't move — so slow-concentric training is, by definition, a form of light-load training; even at moderate loads it worsens the strength stimulus by reducing force output.
- For hypertrophy, "time under tension" is not an independent driver of growth. Jenkins et al. study: biceps curls, 4 sets to failure at 30% vs 80% 1RM; 30% group did 25-40 reps total, 80% group 6-12 — time under tension in the 30% group was ~3x that of the 80% group, yet hypertrophy was similar (p.209-210) [SCIENCE, cite 18]. Conclusion: if TUT independently drove hypertrophy, high reps would out-grow low reps — they don't. Loads under ~30% 1RM tend to produce less hypertrophy than heavier sets (p.210) [SCIENCE, cite 19], plausibly because perceived failure at very light loads is driven by discomfort/high perceived exertion rather than true muscular fatigue (p.210) [SCIENCE, cites 20-21], making overly slow concentrics risk the same problem: a moderate number of very slow reps can extend a set to the length of a very-high-rep set, with the same potential hypertrophy penalty.
- **How slow is too slow** — two syntheses converge on the same ceiling (p.210):
  - Schoenfeld et al. 2015 meta-analysis: 204 participants, 8 studies.
  - Moreno-Villanueva et al. 2022 systematic review: 726 participants, 24 studies.
  - Both: **total repetition duration >8 seconds → poorer hypertrophy** [SCIENCE, cites 22-23]. Given eccentric tempos of 1-4s are fine for hypertrophy, concentric tempo must stay fast enough that concentric + eccentric + transition together don't exceed 8 seconds.
  - Both note that reps ≤8s can produce similar hypertrophy to normal tempo **only when trained to (or near) failure** [SCIENCE]. Rationale: training to failure eventually fatigues all fibers, producing equivalent per-set hypertrophy even if early reps generate less force due to slow tempo — the same logic that equates high-rep/low-load training with heavy training when both go close enough to failure.
- **Key nuance** [CONVENTION] (p.210-211): you do not need to train to literal failure for slow concentrics to work — you just need enough reps that concentric tempo becomes **involuntarily** slow by the end of the set. Even maximal-intent reps inevitably slow near failure (last rep slower than the 1-RIR rep, slower than the 2-RIR rep, etc.); intentionally-slow reps undergo the same transition from voluntary to involuntary slowing as the set progresses. So long as this involuntary slowing occurs appropriately for the prescribed RIR, and reps stay ≤8s, the hypertrophy stimulus should be similar.
- **Recommendation — strength** [CONVENTION] (p.215): lift with maximal intent (fast as possible with control), barring pain/injury; avoid intentionally slow concentrics — they impair strength gains.
- **Recommendation — hypertrophy** [CONVENTION] (p.215): maximal intent or intentionally slow concentrics are both fine, especially if slowing improves control/technique — but tempo must become involuntarily slow near failure for the set to be effective, and total rep duration should stay ≤~8 seconds.

## Concentric–eccentric transition phase duration (pauses) (p.211-213)

- Definition: whether/how long you pause between the eccentric and the following concentric (p.211). Much less research exists on this phase than on eccentric/concentric tempo.
- Mechanism: when a concentric is preceded directly by an eccentric without a pause, elastic force from the eccentric transfers onto active contractile force (stretch-shortening cycle). Faster eccentrics store more elastic energy, speeding (and thus strengthening) the following concentric (p.211) [SCIENCE, cite 7]. Pausing dissipates this elastic force — the longer the pause, the greater the reduction in absolute strength / submaximal-load rep capacity (p.211) [SCIENCE, cite 24].
- **Strength implications** (p.211-212):
  - Some coaches argue deadlifts should be paused/reset each rep (mirroring competition, which has no preceding eccentric) or bench press should be paused every rep (mirroring the "press" command after the bar is motionless). The book counters (per Level 4) that specificity is more nuanced than always training exactly to competition standard.
  - Direct test: pin squats (paused at bottom on rack pins every rep) vs. regular back squats produced **similar** back squat 1RM gains (p.212) [SCIENCE, cite 25] — contrary to the "always train to competition standard" prediction. Explanation: the powerlifts' sticking regions occur in the early concentric where lifters are biomechanically disadvantaged (cites 26-28); pausing removes elastic-energy assistance through the sticking region acutely, but trains active force production specifically in that weak position.
  - Coaching inference [CONVENTION] (p.212): athletes who lose control during the transition, or who rely on a fast eccentric "bounce" out of the bottom, may benefit from pin/pause training — not as a replacement for the bounce (since faster eccentrics do acutely increase concentric force, cite 7) but as a complement, to also build active strength in the sticking region.
- **Hypertrophy implications** (p.212-213):
  - Some coaches recommend pausing in the stretched (long-muscle-length) position, citing Level 4's point that long-length ranges are most stimulative.
  - Supporting analogy [SCIENCE, weak/indirect]: static stretching, if very long (~1 hour) and intense (8/10 discomfort) (cite 29), or moderately long (~15 min) and maximally intense (cite 30), and done frequently (4-7 days/week), can produce hypertrophy comparable to resistance training — but the book notes a 1-2 second rep pause is far shorter and "probably not comparable" to stretch-induced hypertrophy (p.213).
  - Others argue pausing eliminates elastic contributions so force is purely active — but mechanosensors respond to both active and passive tension, and can even generate passive tension themselves, so elastic contribution is "not inherently wasted" for hypertrophy (p.213) [SCIENCE, cites 31-33].
  - Overall verdict [DISPUTED] (p.213): research on this topic is unclear; if total rep duration stays ≤8 seconds and training is reasonably close to failure, no large hypertrophy effect from pausing in the stretch should be expected either way — so choose to pause or not based on personal preference for consistent technical execution.
- **Recommendation — strength** [CONVENTION] (p.215): conditionally beneficial or harmful depending on context; use pauses strategically to build active force or fix technical issues; expect lower force per rep from dissipated elastic energy.
- **Recommendation — hypertrophy** [CONVENTION] (p.215): pausing in the stretched position is optional; short pauses (1-2s) are unlikely to meaningfully boost hypertrophy unless they improve control/ROM consistency, but can be useful to make a lighter load harder.

## Exercise technique (p.213-214)

- Technique isn't dictated by tempo directly but is influenced by it (p.213). When first learning a movement, use a comfortable (often slower) tempo until control at faster speeds is achieved. Strength is a skill expressed through movement, not purely a bodily quality — perform lifts consistently so progress reflects real strength change, not technique drift (e.g., shortened ROM) (p.213).
- Competitive lifting analogy (p.213): technical rules vary by sport/federation (squat depth in powerlifting, snatch depth in weightlifting, sumo allowed in powerlifting but not strongman, hitching/straps allowed in strongman but not powerlifting, press-outs illegal in weightlifting but not strongman, differing bench "start" commands across powerlifting federations). Universal legal-lift standards exist, but **highly specific universal "good technique" standards do not and should not** — a "correct" squat differs by injury history, height, limb-to-torso ratio, and joint mobility (p.213-214) [CONVENTION].
  - Universal technical principles: performing the joint actions that define the lift, and maintaining balance over center of gravity (p.214). Beyond that, many widely shared "technique rules" range from unproven to context-dependent — be wary of narratives claiming one universally best way to lift (p.213-214).
- **Recommendation — strength** [CONVENTION] (p.214, p.216): use technique that meets your sport's standards, maximizes 1RM, and allows pain-free consistent training at the needed volume/load/frequency.
- **Recommendation — hypertrophy** (p.214, p.216): no governing standards; more subjective. Research-based definition of good technique (cite 34): "the controlled execution of bodily movements to ensure an exercise effectively targets specific muscle groups while minimizing injury risk," involving body positioning/alignment, ROM, and repetition tempo. "Cheating"/loose form debates center on how much momentum from non-prime-mover muscles is used.
  - Cheating can help or hurt depending on effect on stimulus: e.g., bent-over row done upright (shorter lat length) vs. parallel to floor (longer lat length); barbell cheat curls that hip-thrust past the hardest part, lean back to finish, then drop the weight without eccentric control — cutting long-length tension and roughly halving total work by skipping the eccentric (p.214).
  - Not all cheating is bad: continuing partial-ROM reps after failing full-ROM reps can increase set stimulativeness, especially for exercises whose resistance profile fails at short muscle lengths, allowing extra partials at long lengths. Larsen et al. study: calf raises to failure vs. calf raises continued into partial reps past full-ROM failure (until unable to move out of full dorsiflexion) — partials-past-failure produced substantially more calf growth (p.214) [SCIENCE, cite 35]. Rule of thumb [CONVENTION]: alter ROM/momentum contribution with purpose (to increase stimulus), not out of ego.

### Mind-muscle connection (p.214-215)

- Focusing attention on a muscle can increase its electrical (EMG) activity (p.214-215) [SCIENCE, cites 36-37], but this effect disappears at heavy loads (~80% 1RM or higher) on compound lifts like bench press (p.215) [SCIENCE, cite 37]. At light-enough loads not all contributing muscles are required, allowing emphasis-shifting between muscles; at high loads on multi-joint lifts, all muscles must maximally contribute regardless of focus, and the same likely applies near failure late in a set.
- Internal focus (on muscles/body position) versus external focus (on movement outcome) — internal focus may **inhibit strength** (p.215) [SCIENCE, cite 38]. Strength-focused lifters should use external over internal cues (e.g., "push the floor away" vs. "hips").
- For hypertrophy, avoid mind-muscle-connection focus on heavy, free-weight, multi-joint lifts (same rationale as above); it's probably not harmful on single-joint lifts (which cannot be performed at all without engaging the target muscle when done correctly) (p.215).
- Individual biomechanics and relative muscle strength vary between lifters (e.g., lats may limit one lifter's back exercise while a different muscle limits another) — isolation work targeting the individually limiting muscle may be the best solution in such cases (p.215).
- **Recommendation — strength** [CONVENTION] (p.216): focus on external cues for maximal performance.
- **Recommendation — hypertrophy** [CONVENTION] (p.216): internal focus may help activate specific regions on single-joint or lighter-load exercises; less useful on heavy compound lifts.

## SUMMARY OF TEMPO RECOMMENDATIONS (p.215-216)

**Eccentric (Lowering Phase)**
- Strength: lower as fast as possible while maintaining technical control; heavier loads are naturally slower. Slow eccentrics (3-6 seconds) can be useful when heavy lifting isn't possible due to pain, injury, or fatigue, making lighter loads more stimulative.
- Hypertrophy: use 1-4 seconds under muscular control; ensure you (not gravity) lower the weight. Fast eccentrics are fine if controlled, but total rep duration (all phases) should not exceed ~8 seconds.

**Concentric (Lifting Phase)**
- Strength: lift with maximal intent (as fast as possible with control), barring pain/injury. Avoid intentionally slow concentrics — they impair strength gains.
- Hypertrophy: maximal intent or intentionally slowed concentrics are both fine (slowing especially useful for control/technique), but tempo must become involuntarily slow by the end of the set as you approach the target RIR, and total rep length should not exceed ~8 seconds.

**Eccentric–Concentric Transition (Pauses)**
- Strength: conditionally beneficial or harmful depending on context; use pauses strategically for active-force focus or technical fixes. Pausing dissipates elastic energy, so expect lower force per rep.
- Hypertrophy: pausing in the stretched position is optional; short pauses (1-2 seconds) are unlikely to meaningfully boost hypertrophy unless they improve control/ROM consistency; useful for making a lighter load harder.

**Tempo Consistency**
- Strength: keep technique and tempo consistent between sessions to track true strength change; adjust tempo only for specific training purposes.
- Hypertrophy: maintain enough consistency to compare progress, but feel free to vary tempo for exercise variation or control.

**Special Methods**
- Strength: supramaximal eccentrics may provide small additional strength gains for advanced lifters but carry higher injury risk; use only with proper equipment and spotting.
- Hypertrophy: no clear hypertrophy advantage from supramaximal eccentrics; prioritize RIR and volume instead.

**Mind–Muscle Connection**
- Strength: focus on external cues for maximal performance.
- Hypertrophy: internal focus may help activation of specific regions on single-joint or lighter-load exercises; less useful on heavy compound lifts.

**Technique Adjustments**
- Strength: technique must meet competitive standards, be pain-free, and allow sustainable progression.
- Hypertrophy: choose technique variations that best target intended muscles at long lengths and allow training close to failure.
