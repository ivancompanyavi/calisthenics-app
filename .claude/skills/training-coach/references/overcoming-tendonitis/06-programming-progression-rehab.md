# Overcoming Tendonitis Ch.6 — Tendinopathy Programming, Progression, and Rehabilitation

> **Source:** *Overcoming Tendonitis* (Steven Low), Ch.6, book pp.41-58. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/overcoming-tendonitis.txt` or PDF pp.41-58.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] established research · [CONVENTION] Low's system · [DISPUTED] contested.
> **⚠️ Not medical advice — this book distinguishes rehab-programming (coachable) from medical treatment (refer to a physician/physio).**

## Ordering of Rehabilitation and Prehabilitation (p.41)
[CONVENTION] Structure a rehab session like a training session, borrowed from *Overcoming Gravity*: (1) Warm-Up and Mobility, (2) Skill/Technique Work, (3) Power/Isometrics/Eccentrics/Regular Strength Work, (4) Endurance/Metabolic Conditioning, (5) Prehabilitation/Flexibility/Cool Down (p.41). Rationale for each slot:
- Warm-up/mobility: prepares tissue for load; more critical for an injured area, which is more susceptible to reinjury (p.41).
- Skill/technique: used to correct movement patterns that may be contributing to the dysfunction; a physical therapist can identify which movements aggravate the injury and flag compensatory patterns (p.41).
- Power/strength before endurance: high-power/strength work is more neurologically fatiguing than endurance work, so sequencing strength first maximizes quality work performed per session (p.42).
- Prehab/flexibility/isolation last: performed when the body is already fatigued, which lets tissue relax and stretch more effectively; isolation work targets weak links or "injury-proofs" the body (p.41-42).

## Sample Rehabilitation Session (p.42-43)
[CONVENTION] Before programming anything, first determine the irritability stage — if highly irritable, some rehab work may need removing, and isometrics may need adding just for pain management so remaining work can be done with less pain (p.42). Six-step session template:
1. Warm-up/mobility — light stretching, heat if tissue is stiff/painful (p.42).
2. Skilled techniques — soft tissue work (massage/manual therapy/mobilization) at clinician discretion; neuromuscular training for movement quality (p.42).
3. Pain management — isometrics to reduce pain ahead of the loading program; heat/analgesics; can be moved before warm-up if needed (p.42).
4. Targeted eccentric-concentric exercises — e.g., wrist curls (golfer's elbow), reverse wrist curls (tennis elbow), biceps curls (biceps tendinopathy), decline board squats (patellar); compound/sport-specific movements added later in rehab (p.42).
5. Peripheral strength/endurance/kinetic-chain work — strengthen antagonists for adequate strength ratios proximal/distal to the joint; address any control/stability deficits above and below the joint (p.42).
6. Flexibility and follow-up mobility — mobility after flexibility can lock in new ROM; some prefer flexibility before strength if it doesn't interfere with training (p.43).
Modalities (e.g., extracorporeal shockwave therapy) can be slotted around steps 2-3, or standalone (p.43).

## Programming and Progression: Intensity and Volume (p.43-44)
[SCIENCE] Intensity (rep range) and volume (total sets) are intertwined: higher intensity generally requires more total sets for a sufficient adaptation (Prilepin Tables analogy from weightlifting) (p.43).
[SCIENCE] Estimated %1RM by rep count (three formulas — Brzycki/Baechle/dos Remedios), e.g. ~85-87% at 5 reps, ~75-78% at 10 reps, ~60-70% at 15 reps (p.43-44, table).

Comparison of established loading protocols, per Visnes and Bahr (p.44):
- **Alfredson**: 2 exercises × 3×15, 2x/day → 6 sets/day, 42 sets/week (p.44).
- **Stanish and Curwin**: 1 exercise × 3×10, 1x/day → 3 sets/day, 21 sets/week (p.44).
- **Silbernagel combined**: phase 1: 4 exercises × 3×10-15; phase 2: 5 exercises × 3×15-20; phase 3: 4 exercises × 3×15-20; phase 4: 3 exercises × 3×15-20, difficulty progressing each phase → ~12 sets/day (phase 1), 84 sets/week (p.44). Note: some variations (seated/two-leg heel raises) are lower intensity than counterparts (standing one-leg/eccentric heel raises), so raw set counts overstate effective volume (p.44).
- **Heavy-slow resistance (HSR)**: starts 3 exercises × 4×15, builds to 6 exercises → 6 sets/day average, 42 sets/week (p.44).

[DISPUTED] Volume prescriptions vary hugely across programs (21-84 sets/week) with no clear winner; intensity (10-15 RM ≈ 60-75% 1RM) is fairly consistent across programs, but the book suspects the disparity in outcomes stems from studies rarely controlling for: (a) tendinopathy stage (reactive/dysrepair vs. degenerative vs. reactive-on-degenerative), (b) overall irritability/reactivity (dictates whether the program must be adapted on the fly), (c) whether pain is acute or chronic (affects pain education needs) (p.44).
[SCIENCE] Visnes and Bahr estimate Alfredson and Stanish/Curwin produced 50-70% patient improvement; Habits et al suggest Alfredson may be best for mid-portion Achilles tendinopathy; Malliares review suggests HSR may be best for patellar tendinopathy (p.44) — i.e., protocol choice may be tendon-site-dependent [DISPUTED].
[CONVENTION] Overall recommendation: **6-12 sets of 10-15 reps every two days** (≈60-180 reps per 2-day session), i.e. **21-42 sets/week total**, not a hard rule. If symptoms are very reactive/irritable, start with an initial ramp-up period of a few weeks doing only a few sets of a couple of exercises (p.44).

## Frequency (p.45)
[SCIENCE] Alfredson prescribes 2x/day; Stanish/Curwin and Silbernagel prescribe daily; HSR prescribes 3x/week (p.45).
[CONVENTION] Recommend **3x/week** rehab frequency (matching HSR), for three reasons: (1) patient compliance is much higher at lower frequency than daily/2x-daily; (2) different total-stress doses can still achieve maximal collagen synthesis rates without needing multiple sessions per day (though therapeutic-exercise results may not depend on structural change at all — see Ch.5 caveat); (3) it's easier to titrate correct stress dose in one session/day vs. multiple sessions on an already-irritable tendon (p.45).
[CONVENTION] This doesn't preclude other work at higher frequency: flexibility work may warrant more frequent sessions; if pain occurs daily, daily isometrics for pain relief are fine if they let the person continue their sport without aggravating it; different session components can run at different frequencies — e.g., concentric-eccentrics 3-4x/week, while mobility/soft-tissue/heat/isometrics run 5-7x/week (p.45).

## Tempo (p.45-46)
[CONVENTION] Tempo has 4 phases: eccentric, bottom pause, concentric, top pause — notated as a 4-digit code (e.g., 10x0), where "X" means accelerate through the movement rather than a fixed time. Example: 3131 = 3s eccentric, 1s pause, 3s controlled concentric, 1s pause at top. For pulling exercises the sequence order is concentric-pause-eccentric-pause (3,4,1,2) (p.45-46).
[SCIENCE] Bohm et al (2014, building on prior experiments): a tendon strain duration of ~3 seconds loading/relaxation is more optimal for adaptation than either 1s or 12s loading/relaxation (p.46).
[SCIENCE] Contraction type (eccentric vs concentric) may not matter for the adaptation (cited from Ch.5), but evidence is not conclusive enough to say definitively (p.46).
[CONVENTION] Recommend **2-3s eccentric, 1-2s controlled concentric** for initial rehab phases — tempo notation 3010, 2010, 3020, or 2020. Modify: shorter/faster tempo if the patient needs inter-rep rest or is advancing stages; faster eccentric/concentric as the athlete progresses toward the explosive/plyometric demands of their sport (p.46).

## Alternative Protocol: High-Repetition (Low's own, Level V evidence) (p.46-47)
[CONVENTION] Steven Low's pre-research "expert opinion" protocol (no formal study — Level V evidence), developed for gymnasts/parkour/climbing athletes returning to sport:
- 1-2 exercises, 3 sets each (p.46).
- **30-50 reps/set**, depending on progression stage (p.46).
- Tempo **2-3s eccentric, 1s concentric** (3010 or 2010), adjusted for tendon presentation/time constraints (p.46).
- Stay **3-5 reps short of failure** initially, to lower reinjury risk (p.46).
- Can be performed with pain **if pain does not increase afterward or by the next session and function is improving** (p.46) — this is the chapter's explicit pain-tolerance rule for this protocol.
Rationale: lower intensity/higher reps is less likely to reirritate an easily-aggravated area, and hitting volume targets doesn't require adding many different exercises — tradeoff is that varying exercises may target areas more specifically than one repeated exercise (p.46).

Progression scheme (most similar to Silbernagel — volume first, then load) (p.46-47):
1. Add 3-5 reps/workout (or more conservatively 1-2 reps) until reaching 50 reps/set (p.46).
2. Then increase load/progression difficulty while dropping back to ~30 reps/set, working back up to 50 (p.47).
3. Once function has improved enough that heavier loading (10-15 RM) doesn't aggravate it, transition into that range, and slowly work toward heavier loads (e.g. 5RM) if the sport demands it (p.47).

[CONVENTION] Frequency: **3-4x/week**, similar to HSR, also ending in a heavier-loading phase — but starts less intense/slower-progressing than most protocols to minimize reinjury risk while still loading the tendon. Anecdotal (not study-verified) success rate: **60-80%** (p.47).
[CONVENTION] If the high-rep protocol shows little improvement after a few weeks, switching to a 10-15 rep protocol may still work — the protocols are not mutually exclusive (p.47).
[DISPUTED/CONVENTION speculation] Different protocols may suit different tendinopathy stages: reactive / reactive-on-degenerative tendons may respond better initially to high-rep/lower-intensity (since it represents a load reduction from the overuse that caused injury), then transition to 10-15RM progressive loading once reactivity has calmed; degenerative tendon areas may benefit from lighter loads that let weaker type III collagen mature without aggravation, preparing for higher loads later (p.47).

## Rehabilitation Stages (p.47-54)
[SCIENCE] Progression models by program: Alfredson progresses load; Stanish/Curwin progresses speed first then load; Silbernagel progresses volume first then load; HSR moves to fewer reps of heavier load (p.47).
[SCIENCE] Malliares et al (2015) four-phase patellar tendinopathy rehab protocol (p.48, figure/table):
1. **Isometric Loading** — indicated when there is more than minimal pain during isotonic exercise; dosage: **5 reps × 45s hold, 2-3x/day, progressing to 70% max contraction as pain allows** (p.48).
2. **Isotonic Loading** — indicated when pain during isotonic exercise is minimal; dosage: **3-4 sets of 15RM progressing to 6RM, every second day, fatiguing load** (p.48).
3. **Energy-Storage Loading** — indicated when (a) strength/load tolerance is consistent with the uninjured side, and (b) load tolerance is adequate with initial-level energy-storage exercise; dosage: progressively develop volume then intensity to replicate sport demands (p.48).
4. **Return to Sport** — indicated by load tolerance to energy-storage exercise nearing training demands; dosage: progressively add training drills, then competition, once tolerant of full training (p.48).

[SCIENCE] Mascaro et al graded return-to-sport approach (similar sequential structure, figure — see PDF p.49): Isometric Work → Strengthening → Functional Strength → Speed → Plyometrics → Return to Activity, with tendon load increasing at each stage (p.49).
[CONVENTION] Skipping stages is a common reinjury cause: the tendon isn't ready for the frequency/intensity/volume being applied — and note the original overuse injury was itself caused by too much intensity/volume/frequency (p.49).

Book's consolidated 5-stage framework (synthesizing both models above) (p.49):
1. Reduce Pain and Irritability
2. Improve Strength
3. Improve Functional Strength
4. Increase Power
5. Develop Stretch-Shorten Cycle and Return to Sport

### Stage 1 — Reduce Pain and Irritability (p.50-51)
- Distinguish acute vs. chronic pain; provide pain neuroscience education (PNE) so the patient doesn't catastrophize chronic pain as ongoing damage (p.50).
- If aggravated by overuse, identify and remove offending daily/work/sport movements, especially those creating high tension or compressive loading on the tendon (p.50).
- [CONVENTION] Isometrics: **3-4 minutes total isometric tension at 70-80% MVIC, spread across 3-24 sets**; sets of ~1 min or 45s are least tedious; can repeat multiple times/day (p.50) — consistent with Ch.5's isometric pain-relief protocol.
- NSAIDs may reduce pain but may also decrease collagen synthesis; not a concern since no rehab loading exercise is yet introduced at this stage; most useful with associated inflammation (paratendonitis) in compressed regions (rotator cuff, infrapatellar, Achilles insertion) (p.50).
- Heat and (per some evidence) dexamethasone can help calm reactive tendons (p.50).
- Light stretching if ROM is reduced; a clinician should screen for joint weaknesses/compensations above/at/below the affected area (p.50-51).
- [CONVENTION] Tendinopathy stage matters for how this phase behaves: reactive/dysrepair tends to respond well to reduced loading and progresses through phases relatively fast; reactive-on-degenerative or degenerative may not respond well to NSAIDs or even isometrics (p.51).
- PNE recommended for both acute and chronic pain; if presumed-acute pain isn't decreasing as aggravating exercises are removed and rehab progresses, suspect it's actually chronic (p.51).

### Stage 2 — Improve Strength (p.51-52)
- Goal: reintroduce tendon loading via isolation eccentric-concentric exercises to raise tissue load tolerance; pain should generally trend down (unless chronic) (p.51).
- Watch for compensations / jerky-choppy movement quality; aim for smooth, controlled movement (p.51).
- Reactive/dysrepair tendinopathy can combine this stage with Stage 3 (functional strength) — substituting aggravating exercises with other lower-volume/intensity/frequency strength movements plus isolated eccentric-concentric work is also effective (p.51).
- [CONVENTION] Reduce sport-specific training volume/intensity to **~50% or less of normal** while introducing pain-management isometrics; since overuse load was often already 30-50%+ above normal, this reduction typically nets out to **65-75% of one's normal volume** in practice (p.51).
- Better option when feasible: maintain the same intensity/volume but swap out only the aggravating exercises for similar non-aggravating, sport-specific drills (p.51).
- Reactive-on-degenerative: highly irritable at this stage — progress slowly, potentially holding volume/intensity/frequency constant for **2-3+ sessions** before advancing, since small load increases or new exercises can reaggravate; expect pain to remain elevated initially (reactive portion subsides but degenerative/chronic pain may persist) — overall pain reduction is still a good sign (p.51).
- Degenerative tendinopathy: less irritable than reactive-on-degenerative, but expect reduced strength/increased atrophy from time off; risk of chronic pain developing, so PNE remains important (p.51-52).
- [CONVENTION] Simple strength-progression tactics: add light weight (start 2lb, then 3, 4lb...) or step up resistance-band difficulty slightly; OR add reps not-to-failure (10→11→12 over 1-3 workouts); OR add a 4th set of 1-3 not-to-failure reps and slowly build that set's volume over subsequent workouts — use whichever the tendon tolerates (p.52).
- Tempo: **2010 or 3010** as a good starting point, slow-to-moderate speed both phases (p.52).
- Progress mainly via load; use reps/sets as an alternative when load increases are hard to apply or reactivity returns from too-high load (p.52).

### Stage 3 — Improve Functional Strength (p.52-53)
- Definition: transition from isolation rehab exercises to fundamental compound movement patterns relevant to the sport (lower body: squats, single-leg squats, deadlifts; upper body: pushups, pull-ups, rows, dips, overhead presses) (p.52).
- No fixed timing rule: chronic-pain cases may add these rapidly (if no actual tissue damage prevents it, given adequate PNE); reactive or reactive-on-degenerative cases should wait **1-2 weeks** after isolation rehab exercises have settled reactivity before introducing functional strength work, to reduce risk of reactivity returning (p.52).
- Continue isometrics for pain if needed; don't progress too quickly; may replace easier prior exercises with more effective ones; watch compensations/kinetic-chain application when compound exercises are added; if volume gets too high, reduce isolation work in favor of compound movements (e.g., drop to 1-2 isolation sets after compounds) (p.52).
- [CONVENTION] "Flare-ups" (increased pain/tenderness/soreness during, after, or the next day) are a normal part of rehab and don't necessarily mean the loading scheme is wrong — a clinician must judge whether to stay the course or modify. Basing the program purely on symptom fluctuation session-to-session is described as "often ineffective and haphazard" — the actual goal is increased load tolerance, strength, and function over time, not zero flare-ups (p.53).

### Stage 4 — Increase Power (p.53-54)
- Transition functional-strength exercises into power work by gradually decreasing eccentric/concentric time until reps become explosive (p.53).
- [CONVENTION] Progress **one exercise's tempo at a time** rather than several simultaneously, tracking pain/function post-session and the next day, to isolate what's driving any reaction (p.53).
- Avoid exercises that begin with tendon-compressing positions (e.g., very deep heel raises); start power work at **mid-range** motion (less compressive loading) before progressing to end-range/full-ROM, which carries more compressive force and is more easily aggravated (p.53). Example: climbers with golfer's/tennis elbow should begin explosive pull-up work in a limited range (arms slightly bent, no full lock-off) and only progress to full-ROM power pull-ups after **1-2 weeks** of good tolerance (p.53-54).
- For sedentary/occupational patients, power and stretch-shorten-cycle stages may be unnecessary — instead reintroduce task-specific activity with good movement patterns and overload-avoidance strategies (p.54).

### Stage 5 — Develop Stretch-Shorten Cycle and Return to Sport (p.54)
- Stretch-shorten cycle = eccentric-concentric rebound (plyometrics), central to sprinting/jumping sports; apply the same mid-range-before-full-compressive-load principle as Stage 4, building volume/progression over time (p.54).
- Recommend specialized care (sports doctor, PT, athletic trainer, CSCS) if the primary practitioner isn't familiar with these exercises (p.54).
- [CONVENTION] Common failure mode: laypeople/athletes new to plyometrics jump in too fast — depth drops/rebounds done from too great a height, with too many reps. Recommendation: **start with a very small amount, don't go to failure**, increase volume slowly over time, and add faster sport-specific movements as volume increases (p.54).

## Continuing Your Sport During Rehabilitation (p.54-55)
[CONVENTION] Answer to "should I keep training through rehab?" is **yes, but properly** — total rest for more than 1-2 weeks is generally a mistake: it provides no additional benefit, causes atrophy/loss of adaptations, and can lead to habit loss (including nutrition habits, causing inadvertent weight gain) (p.54).
- Instead, rehab the affected area (with proper diagnosis/treatment) while maintaining sport habits/benefits as much as possible (p.54).
- Reactive tendinopathy with low irritability: reduce offending sport-specific movements and rehab on the side; rehab can be as short as a few weeks if no setbacks, ramping activity back up as tolerated (p.54). Example: a runner doing 10h/week should cut to roughly half (4-5h/week) rather than stop entirely; increase again if consistent improvement without compensation, decrease if it worsens (p.54).
- [CONVENTION] **Typical volume reduction: 40-50%**, but the acceptable range spans **0-95%** depending on presentation — some athletes only need to remove one specific aggravating movement (e.g., in climbing/gymnastics) and keep everything else; others need to cut volume close to zero for days to a week if the tendon is extremely reactive (p.55).
- Even minimal exercise is described as almost always better than complete rest (p.55).
- [CONVENTION] Progression rule when ramping back up: add volume/intensity gradually, **typically less than 10% per week**; favor non-painful sport activities as a rule of thumb (p.55).
- Always consult appropriate medical professionals (doctors, PTs, trainers, those experienced with athletes) to guide load/pain management, since individual response varies (p.55).
- Practical guidance: make a medical appointment right away for moderate-to-severe injuries (appointment lag can be weeks) — you can cancel later if it resolves on its own, but even minor injuries are worth getting checked. Continue training movements unrelated to the injured area (e.g., continue lower body/core work for golfer's elbow), and continue near-injury exercises if they don't aggravate pain (p.55).

## Preventing Injury and Reinjury (p.55-58)

### Acute:Chronic Workload Ratio (ACWR) (p.55-57)
[SCIENCE] ACWR = ratio of acute training load (as short as one session; one week is a common practical unit) to chronic training load (rolling 3-6 week average) — a proxy for "fatigue" (acute) vs. "fitness" (chronic) (p.56). Gabbett (2016) established the concept in team sports (p.55-56).
- **"Sweet spot" (decreased injury risk): ACWR 0.8-1.3** (figure, p.55-56, "danger zone" above this).
- Hulin and Gabbett found a similar predictive range of **0.85-1.35 ACWR** (p.56).
- Two elite European soccer teams found **1.0-1.25** ACWR helped prevent injury (p.57).
- [SCIENCE] Mechanism/interpretation: acute load low + chronic load high → ratio ≤1 → well-prepared/"fit" state, low injury risk. Acute load high + chronic load low → ratio >1 → fatigued/under-conditioned, higher injury risk (p.56).
- [SCIENCE] Gabbett: high chronic workload with a lower ACWR was generally protective — likely because the athlete is adapted to that volume/intensity. Implication: high absolute volume/intensity itself is not inherently harmful; a high *relative* dose (too much, too soon) is the risk factor (p.56).
- External workload = e.g. high-speed activity or mileage; internal workload = rate of perceived exertion (RPE) per session (that RPE-based methodology has not been replicated) (p.56).
- [SCIENCE] EWMA (exponential weighted moving average) may be more sensitive to injury risk than traditional rolling-average ACWR because it weights recent sessions more heavily, better catching too-fast progression — but is harder to calculate for most people (p.56).
- Findings replicated across English soccer, professional basketball, collegiate American football (citations only, no numbers given) (p.56).
- [DISPUTED] Extrapolation beyond team sports (per Gabbett himself) may not hold; for non-professional athletes/other sports, take the specific ratios with a grain of salt, though the general principle — progressing too quickly raises injury risk — is considered broadly valid (p.57).

### Heart Rate Variability (HRV) (p.57-58)
[SCIENCE] HRV = variability between heartbeat intervals, a measure of autonomic nervous system function; low/decreasing HRV under chronic stress signals the body is not recovering, prompting reduced training intensity/volume/frequency (p.57). Example: an athlete's 60bpm resting HR might show ~0.9-1.1s (1±0.1s) between beats normally; under stress this trends toward 1±0s (lower variability), and resting HR may also trend upward (p.57).
- [SCIENCE] NCAA D1 soccer players: high correlation between ACWR (via session time) and low HRV, suggesting the two metrics track similar readiness/fatigue signal (p.57).
- [SCIENCE] Small CrossFit study: high ACWR + low HRV together predicted increased self-reported overuse injury the following week (p.57-58).
- Other cited but not detailed: HRV/blood pressure/baroreflex sensitivity in track & field athletes; a prospective NeuroPlus-biofeedback study using HRV reduced both injuries and missed training days in elite soccer players; possible HRV link (via sympathetic/parasympathetic drive) to illness prediction in swimmers via supine/orthostatic positional testing (p.58).
[CONVENTION] The authors note they have not personally seen clinicians use ACWR/HRV in rehabilitation practice, but suggest they could be incorporated into rehab progression — ACWR in particular is presented as a good practical indicator to prevent progressing rehab exercises too quickly, provided volume/load/frequency can be tracked (p.58).
