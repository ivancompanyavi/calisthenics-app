# Overcoming Tendonitis Ch.1 — Understanding the Levels and Classification of Evidence in Studies

> **Source:** *Overcoming Tendonitis* (Steven Low), Ch.1, book pp.3-6. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/overcoming-tendonitis.txt` or PDF pp.3-6.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] established research · [CONVENTION] Low's system · [DISPUTED] contested.
> **⚠️ Not medical advice — this book distinguishes rehab-programming (coachable) from medical treatment (refer to a physician/physio).**

## Purpose of the chapter (p.3)

[CONVENTION] Sets up the evidence-grading vocabulary the rest of the book uses when citing research for tendon rehab recommendations — i.e. every subsequent chapter's claims should be read through this lens of "how strong is the evidence behind this specific recommendation." [SCIENCE] Over the preceding 10-15 years (relative to the book's writing), physical therapy and other disciplines began classifying evidence by grade so clinicians can judge how well a piece of scientific evidence generalizes to clinical guidelines/treatment (p.3). The book's own scheme follows the Center for Evidence-Based Medicine's classification for diagnostic, prospective, and therapeutic studies (p.3, citing ref. 1).

## Levels of evidence, I-V (p.3-4)

[SCIENCE] Five ascending levels rank study *design* quality, independent of what the study concluded:

- **Level I** — Systematic reviews, high-quality diagnostic studies, prospective studies/randomized controlled trials (p.3).
- **Level II** — Lesser quality than Level I: weaker diagnostic/reference standards, weaker blinding, weaker randomization, weaker follow-up (p.3).
- **Level III** — Case-control or retrospective studies (p.3).
- **Level IV** — Case series (p.3).
- **Level V** — Expert opinion (p.3).

## Grades of recommendation, A-F (p.4)

[SCIENCE] A second axis converts the *body* of available evidence (which may mix several Level I-V studies) into a grade for a specific clinical recommendation:

- **A — STRONG**: at least one Level I study; can additionally include Level II studies (p.4).
- **B — MODERATE**: a single Level I study, or multiple Level II studies (p.4).
- **C — WEAK**: a single Level II study, or multiple Level III and Level IV studies (p.4).
- **D — CONFLICTING**: multiple higher-quality studies disagree about the same recommendation (p.4).
- **E — THEORETICAL/FOUNDATIONAL**: multiple animal/cadaver studies, conceptual models, basic scientific research (p.4).
- **F — EXPERT OPINION**: best practices based on clinical experience (p.4).

[CONVENTION] Note the two systems interlock but aren't identical: A-D is evidence-strength for an empirically-tested recommendation, while E and F extend the scale to recommendations resting only on theory/mechanism (E) or clinician experience (F) rather than direct outcome studies (p.4). The book flags that grades E and F, while lower confidence, still carry real information: E signals a plausible mechanistic basis, F signals a plausible practical basis — neither should be read as "no evidence," just "not yet outcome-study-validated" (p.4).

[CONVENTION] Not every grade (A-F) is used everywhere in the book's evidence-referencing sections — the letter that appears for a given recommendation reflects only what evidence actually exists for it (p.4).

[SCIENCE] As of the book's writing, there is only one "gold standard" article applying this A-F classification scheme comprehensively to a tendinopathy: for Achilles tendinopathy specifically, titled *Achilles Pain, Stiffness, and Muscle Power Deficits; Midportion Achilles Tendinopathy Revision* (p.4, ref. 2). Because Achilles tendinopathy is comparatively over-studied relative to other tendinopathies, this chapter's worked example (and much of the book's evidence-grading elsewhere) leans on that article as the template even when discussing other tendons.

## Decision-making process overview (p.5)

[CONVENTION] The referenced Achilles article supplies a six-step clinical decision tree that the book adopts as the general shape of tendinopathy rehab decision-making (p.5):

1. Medical screening.
2. Classifying the condition.
3. Determining the irritability stage.
4. Selecting appropriate outcome measures.
5. Selecting appropriate interventional strategies.
6. Re-evaluating improvement and modifying strategies as needed.

### Step 1 — Medical screening (p.5)

[CONVENTION] Three screening paths, each ending in either continued management or **referral out**: (a) physical-therapy evaluation and intervention; (b) physical therapy plus consultation from another healthcare provider; (c) consultation with another healthcare provider only, which routes straight to **REFER OUT** (p.5). This is the chapter's clearest statement of the coachable/medical boundary: physical therapy handling requires ruling out the case being one that needs a different provider first.

### Step 2 — Classify condition (p.5)

[CONVENTION] Differential diagnosis via clinical findings: does the patient exam suggest Achilles tendinopathy (or the relevant tendinopathy), with consideration of other pain sources — e.g. lumbar radiculopathy, other systemic/medical conditions (p.5). If the picture doesn't fit tendinopathy, the path is **REFER OUT** (p.5).

### Step 3 — Irritability staging (p.5)

[CONVENTION] Decide whether the presentation is **acute** or **non-acute**. Staging matters because it guides treatment frequency, duration, intensity, and type (p.5). [DISPUTED/CONVENTION] Use clinical judgment when the apparent irritability stage doesn't match symptom duration/presentation — i.e. the staging isn't purely mechanical from symptom-duration alone (p.5).

### Step 4 — Outcome measures (p.5)

[CONVENTION] Used to assess level of functioning, associated impairments, and response to treatment. Most commonly used for Achilles tendinopathy (p.5):
- VISA-A, FAAM, LEFS (patient-reported/functional-limitation instruments).
- VAS Pain Scale.
- Range of motion, manual muscle testing, gait testing.

### Step 5 — Intervention strategies (p.5)

[CONVENTION] Determine acute vs. non-acute status to select interventions. Common intervention categories named (without dosing detail at this point in the book) (p.5):
- Modalities, taping, other passive treatment.
- Stretching, eccentric loading exercises, neuromuscular education.
- Joint and soft-tissue mobilization.

### Step 6 — Re-evaluate (p.6)

[CONVENTION] Binary branch: **goals met** → discharge to self-management; **not improving** → referral to other health providers (p.6).

## Worked example: Summary of Recommendations for Midportion Achilles Tendinopathy (p.6, continuing to ch.2 boundary)

[SCIENCE] The chapter closes by applying the A-F grading scale to concrete diagnostic/examination/intervention items for midportion Achilles tendinopathy, drawn from the same reference article (p.6).

**Diagnosis/Classification**
- **[C]** Diagnostic signs: arc sign; Royal London Hospital test; gradual-onset pain subjectively reported 2-6cm proximal to the Achilles insertion; pain with palpation of the tendon mid-portion (p.6).

**Examination**
- **[A]** Outcome measures — activity limitations/self-reported: VISA-A for pain and stiffness; FAAM or LEFS for functional limitations (p.6).
- **[B]** Physical impairment measures: ankle dorsiflexion and subtalar range of motion; plantar-flexion strength and endurance; static arch height and forefoot alignment; pain with palpation (p.6).
- **[B]** Activity limitations/physical performance measures: hop test; heel-raise endurance test (p.6).

**Interventions**
- **[A] Gold-standard intervention**: exercise via mechanical loading of the tendon, using eccentric or concentric/eccentric guided programs (p.6) — the single strongest-evidence intervention named in this chapter, grade A meaning at least one Level I study supports it.
- **[B]** Interventions to consider: patient education and activity modification *without complete cessation*, kept within pain tolerance (p.6) — this is the chapter's specific "how much pain is OK" signal: modify, don't fully rest, and stay within a pain-tolerance threshold rather than a zero-pain threshold; iontophoresis for pain reduction, but only in acute cases (p.6).
- **[C]** Stretching (weak evidence) (p.6).
- **[D — conflicting, no recommendation possible]** Heel lifts; orthoses (p.6).
- **[E — theoretical only]** Low-level laser therapy (p.6).
- **[F — expert opinion]** Patient counseling about key factors involved with the condition; neuromuscular re-education; manual therapy; dry needling combined with eccentric exercises for symptoms lasting over three months; rigid taping for reducing tendon strain and altering foot mechanics (p.6).
- **[C — not recommended]** Night splints (p.6).
- **[F — not recommended]** Elastic taping attempting to reduce pain or improve function (p.6).

[CONVENTION] Reading the table as a whole: the only grade-A intervention is progressive mechanical loading (eccentric/concentric-eccentric exercise) — every passive modality (heel lifts, orthoses, laser, taping, splints) sits at grade C or below, or is actively not recommended. This is the chapter's implicit thesis that carries forward into the rest of the book: loading-based exercise is the best-evidenced treatment lever for tendinopathy, and passive/modality-based treatments are comparatively weak or unsupported.
