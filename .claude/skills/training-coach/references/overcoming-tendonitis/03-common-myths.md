# Overcoming Tendonitis Ch.3 — Common Myths of Tendinopathy

> **Source:** *Overcoming Tendonitis* (Steven Low), Ch.3, book pp.17-20. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/overcoming-tendonitis.txt` or PDF pp.17-20.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] established research · [CONVENTION] Low's system · [DISPUTED] contested.
> **⚠️ Not medical advice — this book distinguishes rehab-programming (coachable) from medical treatment (refer to a physician/physio).**

## Diagnosis / staging note (p.17-18)
- [SCIENCE] Tendinopathy diagnosis relies mainly on localized symptoms and functional presentation plus a medical professional's screening/differential diagnosis; symptoms don't necessarily correlate cleanly to the underlying stage (reactive/dysrepair/degenerative), making staging tricky (p.17).
- [SCIENCE] Only large-scale degenerative changes are typically visible on ultrasound or MRI, and imaging is inconvenient, time-consuming, and expensive — so pinpointing an exact stage is generally unnecessary (p.17-18).
- [CONVENTION] Conservative treatment (e.g. physical therapy) is the recommended initial response across all tendinopathy stages regardless of exact staging (p.17-18).
- [CONVENTION] A general understanding of stage is still useful to manage exercise loading appropriately, and distinguishing acute vs. chronic presentation affects treatment approach (detailed in Ch.5) (p.18).

## Myth: Ice helps (p.18-19)
- [SCIENCE] Ice/RICE (Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation) is "not particularly useful" for tendinopathy. Ice is designed to reduce inflammation, pain, and blood flow/swelling in *acute* injuries (p.18).
- [SCIENCE] Reasoning against ice, by stage: in reactive tendinopathy, simple rest/removal of the aggravating exercise resolves the issue — no ice needed. In dysrepair/degenerative stages, there is little-to-no inflammation present at all, because tendinopathy is fundamentally a non-inflammatory proliferative disorder — so anti-inflammatory ice has nothing to act on. Rehab exercise is more useful than ice in every stage (p.18).
- [SCIENCE] Ice (via cold-water immersion studies) has been shown to decrease strength and muscle adaptations — a plausible negative mechanism, though direct evidence of decreased collagen synthesis in tendons specifically is not established, only "a possible mechanism" (p.18, citing refs 45-46).
- [DISPUTED] Athletes' subjective preference for ice baths may reflect systemic-inflammation relief/feeling better rather than a real physiological benefit for tendinopathy specifically, since tendinopathy itself has minimal inflammation at any stage (p.18).
- [SCIENCE] For pain reduction specifically, analgesics outperform ice because ice only dulls surface-level pain (p.18).
- [CONVENTION] Diagnostic caveat: if ice actually resolves the "tendinopathy," the diagnosis may be wrong — likely paratendonitis (inflammation of the tendon *sheath*), which mimics tendinopathy in similar locations (e.g. sub-acromial space, infrapatellar fat pad, Achilles insertion — areas of high compression) but is a different, inflammatory condition. Both can co-occur, e.g. De Quervain's tenosynovitis (p.18-19).
- [SCIENCE] The RICE protocol is generally inferior to the newer **MEAT** protocol (Movement, Exercise, Analgesia, Treatment) — MEAT is essentially physical therapy rehab starting from day one (or even day zero) post-injury, e.g. after a knee replacement or ankle sprain, because earlier PT promotes faster healing (p.19).
- [CONVENTION] Even for acute injuries (e.g. sprained ankle, absent catastrophic damage like torn ligaments/broken bones), the author's practice rarely uses RICE. Early movement within pain tolerance is preferred over rest; slow movement within range of motion decreases pain in many cases; immobilization significantly lengthens recovery because muscles stiffen and motor control quality decreases (p.19).
- [DISPUTED] Use of ice remains "hotly debated." [CONVENTION] Practical rule: if there's no significant swelling, ice is unnecessary; if there is swelling, compression is more effective than ice; ice's main use is dulling pain, and analgesics usually do this better; if compression is used, elevation becomes unnecessary too. Net conclusion: RICE is outdated/inferior to MEAT (p.19).

## Myth: Analgesics (NSAIDs) help (p.19)
- [DISPUTED] Debate exists on whether to use NSAIDs (e.g. ibuprofen/Advil/Motrin) or paracetamol/acetaminophen (e.g. Tylenol) at any tendinopathy stage (p.19).
- [SCIENCE] Mirroring the ice/cold-immersion pattern, both ibuprofen-type analgesics and paracetamol have shown negative impacts on muscle protein synthesis (hypertrophy) and strength gains; a decrease in collagen synthesis rate is a possible parallel mechanism (not confirmed) (p.19, citing refs 47-48).
- [CONVENTION] One legitimate use case: in the *reactive* stage after aggravation, analgesics may reduce pain and swelling in the surrounding (non-tendon) tissue even though the tendon itself isn't very inflamed (p.19).
- [CONVENTION] Same diagnostic caveat as ice: if analgesics resolve the condition, it may not have been tendinopathy but paratendonitis (p.19).
- [SCIENCE] Isometric exercises are presented as an effective alternative for managing tendinopathy pain without the downsides of ice or analgesics (detailed in Ch.5) (p.19).

## Myth: Avoid all pain (p.19-20)
- [CONVENTION] Core reframe: the rule is not "never push through pain," it's "never push through *aggravating* exercises." Pain and aggravation are different constructs (p.19).
- [CONVENTION] Distinguishing test: a **painful** exercise that yields a positive, progressive result over a few sessions (increased load tolerance and function, often with pain reduction over time) is beneficial and should be kept. An **aggravating** exercise — even if it doesn't hurt in the moment or "feels fine" after warming up — is one that trends toward worsening function/tendon dysfunction over subsequent sessions and should be removed, or have intensity/volume reduced (p.19-20).
- [SCIENCE] Cautionary pattern: an athlete whose tendon pain is worsening over time can still warm up during a session to the point of feeling fine, and mistakenly continue training rigorously hoping it resolves — in many such cases it instead continues to worsen, leading to lower load tolerance and greater tendon dysfunction (p.20).
- [CONVENTION] Practical monitoring target: watch load tolerance and function over time, not pain per se. Remove/adjust any exercise or training intensity/volume that is decreasing performance and increasing signs/symptoms of progressive tendinopathy (usually, but not necessarily, alongside rising pain) (p.20).
- [CONVENTION] Painful exercise can be an "applicable, and often necessary," part of rehab when managed well (p.20).
- [SCIENCE] Pain correlates fairly well with the aggravating exercise in an *acute* injury, but that correlation weakens for a *chronic* injury (defined here as lasting three to six months or more) — pain may not clearly track the causative exercise in chronic cases (p.20).
- [CONVENTION] Because of that acute/chronic distinction and the general risk, rehab exercises should usually be performed under supervision/treatment plan of a qualified medical professional; self-rehabilitation carries risk ("do so at your own risk"). Expanded in Ch.5 (p.20).

## Myth: Increasing blood flow aids rehab (p.20)
- [SCIENCE] The book states the authors previously believed increased blood flow helps rehabilitation, but research shows the opposite for tendinopathy (p.20).
- [SCIENCE] Tendons affected by tendinopathy swell slightly (verifiable via ultrasound/MRI), driven by neovascularization — ingrowth of blood vessels and nerves into the tendon — itself caused by cytokine and growth-factor secretion (p.20, citing ref 49-50).
- [SCIENCE] Mechanistic detail: angiogenesis in tendinosis is mediated by cytokines VEGF, IL-1β, and TNF-α — small secreted signaling proteins. VEGF expression is induced by hypoxia (the region around rotator cuff tears is known to be ischemic). Despite cytokines' classic inflammatory associations, in tendinosis they don't appear to be secreted by classic inflammatory cells (neutrophils, macrophages, lymphocytes) — they may instead come from fibroblasts in the subacromial bursa (p.20, figure/box text).
- [SCIENCE] The swelling and neovascularization are *not necessarily related* to pain or clinical symptoms (p.20, citing ref 51).
- [SCIENCE] Even though neurovascular ingrowth may not be the actual cause of pain/symptoms, eccentrics and resistance training seem to destroy (regress) this ingrowth, which helps normalize blood flow — meaning the mechanism of benefit from exercise is not "increasing" blood flow but reducing the pathological neovascularization (p.20, citing ref 52).
- [SCIENCE] Consequently, modalities aimed at increasing blood flow — heat, ice, contrast baths — do not meaningfully affect this neovascularization process. Voodoo flossing is grouped in the same "increases blood flow" myth category; it may still be helpful/neutral for joint mobilization or breaking chronic pain patterns, but not via a blood-flow mechanism (p.20).
- [CONVENTION] The authors do use heat with tendons, but explicitly *not* for a blood-flow or healing rationale — instead because it decreases tendon stiffness, warms tissue for rehab exercises, and has a pain-reducing effect similar to ice without ice's negative side effects (p.20, citing ref 53).
