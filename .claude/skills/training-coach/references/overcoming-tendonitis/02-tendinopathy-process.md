## A brief history of tendinopathy (p.7)
- [SCIENCE] "Tendonitis" (the historically traditional term) implies inflammation (`-itis` suffix), reflecting the original belief that acute tendon dysfunction was an inflammatory reaction (p.7).
- [SCIENCE] 1976: Puddu first used "tendinosis" to describe persistent tendon degeneration in athletes, once histology showed little-to-no inflammation was actually present in the progressive disorder — contradicting the "-itis" framing (p.7).
- [SCIENCE] Late 1990s: Maffuli et al pushed to replace both "tendonitis" and "tendinosis" with "tendinopathy" to end the terminology confusion; that confusion nonetheless persists today, including among clinicians (p.7).
- [CONVENTION] "Tendinopathy" is now the preferred umbrella term in research, covering the whole spectrum of dysfunction/pain in the tendon, paratendon, and surrounding structures — including reactive and degenerative stages (p.7-8).
- [CONVENTION] Related misnomers: "medial/lateral epicondylitis" (golfer's/tennis elbow) wrongly imply inflammation that is absent or minimal; terminology is shifting to "medial/lateral tendinopathy" or "-algia" variants (epicondylalgia) since pain, not inflammation, is the presenting feature (p.8).
- [CONVENTION] The book itself uses "tendinopathy" throughout (not "tendonitis"/"epicondylitis") despite using "tendonitis" in its title only because that's the term most people search/recognize (p.8).

## Tendinopathy models — historical progression (p.8-12)
Several models have been proposed over time, each building on the last; understanding this progression clarifies the current best picture of what happens in a tendon.

- [SCIENCE] **Arnoczky et al, 2007** — first framed tendon problems as generally resulting from *overstimulation* of the tendon (occasionally *understimulation*). Model: repetitive mechanical loading → either (a) adequate cellular/matrix response → adaptation, or (b) inadequate cellular/matrix response + continued loading exceeding the tendon's healing capacity → overuse injury (p.8, figure — see PDF p.8).
  - Overstimulation is the classic overuse-injury path; understimulation can occur via atrophy from excessive rest, or in late-stage degeneration (p.9).
  - [DISPUTED-by-omission] This model was limited — it didn't well explain cause/effect, symptoms, or the rehab process, but it "got the ball rolling" (p.9).
- [SCIENCE] **Iceberg model, Abate et al 2009** — first attempt to stage the process, including the pre-pain phase. Proposed sequence: overload dysfunction → micro-ruptures → tendon disruption → nerve involvement → pain. Also a "very limited model" of the full process (p.9).
- [SCIENCE] **Cook and Purdam, 2009** — proposed tendinopathy as a *continuum* through stages rather than discrete injury events (p.9-10, figure — see PDF p.9):
  - Normal tendon: under optimised load → adaptation + strengthening (stays normal).
  - Normal tendon under excessive load, or unloaded/stress-shielded → can progress toward **Reactive Tendinopathy**.
  - With appropriate modified load, reactive tendinopathy can return toward normal.
  - Otherwise progression continues: Reactive Tendinopathy → **Tendon Dysrepair** → **Degenerative Tendinopathy**.
  - [SCIENCE] Important finding: tendons at different continuum stages respond *differently* to the same rehab methods — stage-matters for treatment selection (p.10).
- [SCIENCE] **Failed healing model, Fu et al, 2010** — integrated histological (microscopic) findings from overuse tendinopathy into the continuum framework (p.10).
- [SCIENCE] **Cook et al, 2016 (updated model)** — the most complete model currently, explaining how *pain*, *pathology*, and *function* interact as a tendon progresses through the continuum (p.11, figure — see PDF p.11). Key structure:
  - States run: pain-free/good function → pain-free/poor function or insufficient load capacity → Reactive Tendinopathy (painful, poor function) → Tendon Dysrepair (limited ability to remodel tendon structure, reduced likelihood of returning to normal) → Degenerative Tendinopathy (macroscopic changes).
  - [CONVENTION] Different intervention types map to different points on the continuum: interventions addressing poor function/load capacity target the healthier end; interventions directed at pain apply through dysrepair/degenerative stages (p.11).
  - **Reactive-on-degenerative tendinopathy**: occurs when part of the tendon is already degenerative while still being overloaded; the overload can push the surrounding normal tendon tissue into a reactive state too (p.12).

## Tendon microstructure and pathological changes (p.12)
- [SCIENCE] Normal tendon ≈ 90% type I collagen in a cross-linked triple-helix structure (like a 3-strand DNA-helix analog), stabilized by hydrogen-bonded water molecules; these assemble into fibrils → fibers → fascicles connected to proteoglycans — structurally analogous to muscle fiber bundling (p.12).
- [SCIENCE] Combining the Cook et al and Fu et al models, the pathological cascade as tendinopathy progresses (layman's terms in parens) (p.12):
  - Limited inflammation → pro-inflammatory cytokines (possible pain/healing response).
  - Neovascularization → hypervascularity (new blood vessel growth).
  - Innervation → increased neuropeptides/nerve in-growth into the tendon.
  - Cell recruitment/apoptosis → hypercellularity, increased apoptosis (cell death).
  - Matrix synthesis → mucoid, lipoid, calcific degeneration (fatty/calcified tendon changes).
  - Tenogenic differentiation/apoptosis → same mucoid/lipoid/calcific degeneration.
  - Matrix remodeling → collagenolysis, tendon adhesion (collagen breakdown, worse function).
- [SCIENCE] Additional degenerative changes: malformed/irregular vasculature; Type I collagen replaced by weaker, more disordered Type III collagen; increased ground substance linked to collagen loss/breakdown; glutamate and other pain-signaling neurotransmitters present in chronically painful tendons; and hypoxic, hyaline, myxoid, fibrinoid changes with fibrocartilaginous or osseous metaplasia in some tendons (p.12).
- [SCIENCE] Pathological presentation varies widely tendon-to-tendon, especially reactive→dysrepair→degenerative — likely explaining variable rehab outcomes. E.g. osseous metaplasia (calcific tendinopathy) may respond differently than other degenerative-change types (p.12).

## Tendinopathy staging — clinical detail (p.12-13)
Joseph et al's summary of when pathological changes occur within the Cook continuum (p.12-13):
- [SCIENCE] **Reactive tendinopathy**: response to acute overload; non-inflammatory, proliferative. In-vitro evidence: hypercellularity, absence of inflammatory infiltrate, shift toward synthesis of large modular prostaglandins (p.13).
- [SCIENCE] **Tendon dysrepair / failed healing** (2nd stage): resembles reactive stage but with greater matrix disorganization; neovascularity and neuronal ingrowth represent attempted-but-failed repair. Evidence supports recovery of both form and function at this stage with appropriate treatment — specifically load modulation + eccentric exercise stimulus (p.13).
- [SCIENCE] **Degenerative tendinopathy (tendinosis)** (final stage): vast hypocellularity, pooling of proteoglycan, severely disorganized collagen; these changes are thought largely irreversible (p.13).
- [CONVENTION] Clinically this collapses to two practical groups: early reactive vs. late dysrepair/degeneration.
  - Local swelling + mildly focal hypoechoic ultrasound lesions in a younger person → suggests early reactive.
  - Thick nodular tendon, large defined hypoechoic areas, vascular ingrowth, typically in older individuals → suggests late degenerative.
  - Symptoms can be variable at any point on the continuum — clinical picture doesn't reliably pin the stage (p.13).
- [SCIENCE] Hypercellularity (reactive) vs hypocellularity (degenerative) aren't contradictory — they're sequential points on one continuum: cells increase trying to adapt early, then die off (apoptosis) under continued overload as the tendon worsens (p.13).
- [SCIENCE] Pain, pathology, and function are **not the same axis** and don't always move together (p.13-14):
  - Usual case: pain + pathology + function loss increase together through the stages.
  - But also possible: poor function alone; degenerative + non-painful + poor function; or degenerative + non-painful + good function.
  - Up to 85-90% of Achilles tendon ruptures may occur *without* prior pain, swelling, or stiffness — though other predisposing factors (e.g. intense intermittent activity, "weekend warrior" pattern) typically raise the risk (p.14).
- [CONVENTION] Practical implication: interventions should be matched to the tendon's actual stage — both for pain and for function — not treated as one-size-fits-all; this is elaborated in later chapters (p.14).

## Intrinsic and extrinsic risk factors (p.14)
From Morgan and Coetzee's study on patellar tendinopathy risk factors (p.14, figure — see PDF p.14):
- **Intrinsic**: male sex, increased age, impaired lower-limb flexibility, impaired quadriceps femoris strength, increased vastus medialis strength, leg-length variance, shank/forefoot alignment, lower patellar pole position, body composition.
- **Extrinsic**: hard physical work combined with jumping sports; heavy physical demands in volleyball/basketball; general physical activity level; jumping-sport type; level of sports participation (elite vs recreational).
- [CONVENTION] Both intrinsic and extrinsic factors are typically present together in any tendinopathy case. Uses:
  - Screening people with higher propensity to develop tendinopathy.
  - Screening current tendinopathy patients to know they may need to modify external factors and progress rehab *more slowly* if they carry more risk factors than others (p.14).
- [SCIENCE] Common risk factors across most lower-body tendinopathies: sex, age, obesity, strength deficits (especially weakness), impaired flexibility/ROM, body structure (intrinsic); profession-related repetitive strain, and overuse combinations of excessive volume/frequency/intensity (extrinsic) (p.14-15).
- [CONVENTION] Technique- or compensation-related factors (from pain or poor form) are also common; a medical professional (e.g. PT) can help evaluate lifestyle/exercise factors contributing to onset or exacerbation (p.15).

## Tendinopathy, rehabilitation, and outcomes (p.15-16)
- [SCIENCE] Per Cook et al, only a *portion* of a tendinopathic tendon is actually reactive/dysrepair/degenerative at any time — confirmed via ultrasound and MRI (p.15).
- [SCIENCE] As rehab progresses, the dysrepair/degenerative portions typically become more asymptomatic and regain partial function, while the surrounding healthy tendon compensates for the rest (p.15).
- [SCIENCE] **Reactive-on-degenerative tendinopathy** is common after months/years of tendinopathy: part reactive, part degenerative. Rest may clear the reactive portion, but progress stalls because the degenerative portion doesn't improve with rest alone (rest doesn't fix degeneration). Focused rehabilitation is needed to improve function of the healthy parts; as load tolerance increases, the degenerative portion typically becomes progressively more asymptomatic (p.16).
- [SCIENCE] Degeneration is common in the general population even without symptoms:
  - Disc degeneration rises from 37% (age 20) to 96% (age 80) of individuals, with similarly high rates of disc bulges/protrusions/annular fissures, all *without pain* (Brinjikji et al) (p.16).
  - Kannus et al autopsy study: 34% showed tendon degeneration signs (p.16).
  - Achilles tendinopathy prevalence: ~6% in sedentary population, up to 50% in elite athletes (p.16).
  - Asymptomatic Achilles tendinopathy on ultrasound: roughly 4-16%+ depending on study (p.16).
  - Diabetics: ~25% asymptomatic Achilles tendinopathy vs ~12% in the general population (p.16).
  - Imaging-visible tendinopathy stage may not correlate with rehab presentation or symptoms like pain (p.16).
- [SCIENCE] 2019 review (Docking and Cook): high rate of asymptomatic pathological tendons; growing evidence that structural remodeling/repair is *not necessary* for a positive clinical outcome (p.16).
- [CONVENTION] Practical/psychological takeaway: even severe stages (degenerative, reactive-on-degenerative) can often be rehabbed to the point of pain-free daily activity and even resumed sport. Patients with long-standing tendinopathy often feel discouraged; education on this point can motivate continued rehab (p.16).
- [CONVENTION] Staging should inform a rehab plan but must **not** be misused by clinician or patient to assert a prognosis inconsistent with the evidence above (p.16).
- [SCIENCE] Blazina was among the first to propose clinical grading/staging systems (p.16), but practice has since shifted toward outcome measures that track pain/function improvement directly rather than relying on staging alone. Example: the "gold standard" Achilles tendinopathy guidance recommends pairing the VISA-A with either the FAAM or LEFS to track intervention effectiveness during treatment — discussed further in the book's final chapter (p.16).
