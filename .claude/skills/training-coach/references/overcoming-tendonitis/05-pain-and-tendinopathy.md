## Pain Science (p.31-33)

- [SCIENCE] In the Cook et al continuum model, some tendinopathy is painless right up to rupture; more commonly, acute overuse cases show pain that increases progressively as the tendon is overused. Athletes/individuals who push through this pain rather than resting are the population at risk of converting an acute overuse injury into **chronic pain** — defined here as symptoms persistently aggravated for **several months** from initial onset (p.31).
- [SCIENCE] In chronic pain syndromes, imaged/palpated "injured tissue" may in fact be structurally healthy while the patient still experiences pain — explaining pain purely from a musculoskeletal/tissue-damage lens "may do more harm than good" because it can reinforce unhelpful fear/avoidance beliefs rather than address the actual driver (p.31).
- [SCIENCE] Historical progression of pain models: gate control theory (now outdated) → replaced primarily by two current models: the **neuromatrix theory of pain** and the **biopsychosocial model** (p.31).
- [SCIENCE] **Biopsychosocial model** — four factor categories shaping pain perception (p.31-32):
  - Biological: repetitive stress, injury/trauma, nerve damage, illness.
  - Psychological: emotions/thoughts, mood, attention, lack of sleep, anxiety, depression, fear, lack of trust — these alter behavior.
  - Social: biological/psychological pain inputs altering social activities, relationships, work, occupation → can drive isolation.
  - Other/external: medications, lack of medical care access, financial issues, etc. — these act by altering the three categories above.
- [SCIENCE] **Neuromatrix theory of pain** — six areas (verbatim from source) (p.32):
  1. Cognitive: memories of past experience, attributed meaning, anxiety.
  2. Sensory: nociceptive inputs from cutaneous, visceral, muscular sources.
  3. Emotional: limbic system + stress mechanisms (limbic system regulates the brain's threat response).
  4. Pain Perception: sensory, affective, and cognitive dimensions — how the brain interprets pain.
  5. Actions: voluntary and involuntary responses (e.g., reflexively rubbing an injury).
  6. Stress: immune system, cortisol, and other stress hormones.
- [SCIENCE] Mechanism implication: stress, anxiety, and lack of sleep are documented to increase pain; avoiding enjoyable activities also increases pain. This is actionable — behaviors like deep breathing, meditation, and other relaxation techniques can decrease pain through these same pathways (p.32).
- [SCIENCE] Drawing on *Explain Pain* (Butler & Mosely): pain is modulated by the brain/nervous system and functions as a **protective alarm system**, not a direct readout of tissue damage. The brain prioritizes incoming "threat" signals and mounts a protective/corrective response; this system can misfire or persist even without ongoing damage, and can be amplified by stress, unhealthy habits, and life events (p.32).
- [SCIENCE] In chronic pain (>3 months), both brain and local tissue "remember" positions/movements perceived as threatening and place them on high alert, intensifying pain even absent bodily damage — and it is difficult for the person in pain to distinguish this from genuine tissue damage (p.32).
- Worked examples given in-text (mechanism illustration, not prescriptive): (1) a desk worker's shoulder pain escalates from mild-after-3-hours to severe-after-20-minutes as the nervous system amplifies a non-damaging postural alarm signal despite a clean MRI (p.32-33); (2) a marathon runner told her Achilles pain reflects "long-term damage" develops an amplified, fear-conditioned pain response to running distances that resume with no actual tissue damage on imaging — illustrating how clinician language itself can up-regulate the threat/pain response (p.33).
- [SCIENCE] Clinical observation: patients will visibly react (wince/jump) to a movement they *believe* should be painful, then show no reaction when the same movement is repeated — brain expectation is described as driving pain severity more than actual tissue damage in these instances (p.33).

## Pain Education (p.33-35)

- [SCIENCE] Pain education — teaching patients the biopsychosocial/neuromatrix models — measurably reduces pain (pre/post) and reduces disability, per multiple cited reviews/studies (p.33, refs 78-80).
- [DISPUTED]/[CONVENTION] Framing matters: "Pain is only in your brain" is "somewhat true" but can backfire, encouraging worse behavior and more pain over time. Preferred mantras: "Hurt doesn't equal harm," "Stay active," "Return to work as soon as possible" — these avoid detrimental psychological/social patterns (p.33, ref 81).
- [SCIENCE] Per Explain Pain: pain is a protective mechanism, not necessarily a symptom of damage; pain pathways can become over-sensitized so normal positions/movements provoke pain — contributing to chronic pain. "How one makes sense of their pain is an important factor for recovery." Desensitizing techniques (deep breathing, meditation, relaxation) plus biopsychosocial understanding are recommended (p.33-34, ref 82-83).
- [SCIENCE] Louw et al systematic review on pain neuroscience education (PNE) (p.34, ref 84), quoted findings:
  - Given central sensitization, neuroplasticity, and altered endogenous mechanisms, using pain ratings alone as an improvement metric is "illogical"; a sudden, total resolution of pain is "biologically questionable."
  - PNE's core mechanism is *reconceptualizing* pain — patients still feel pain but attribute it to nervous-system sensitization rather than tissue damage, which supports the message that continuing to move/exercise/engage is worthwhile "despite the pain."
  - **Education alone is insufficient**: in the reviewed studies, 5 education-only interventions produced *no* reduction in pain ratings, while 5 of 6 studies combining PNE **with a physical intervention** produced a significant pain reduction. → [SCIENCE] Pain education must be paired with movement-based interventions to be effective (p.34, corroborated by additional studies at ref 85-86).
- [CONVENTION] During rehab, for chronic pain (and sometimes acute pain): don't catastrophize, don't avoid activities because of pain (except temporarily if truly aggravating an acute injury), don't restructure your lifestyle around the pain (p.34).
- [CONVENTION] The book's preferred heuristic: distinguish **aggravating** exercises from merely **painful** exercises (p.34-35):
  - Painful-but-not-aggravating: may hurt during the session but function/performance improves by that session's end or by the next session (this is normal and acceptable rehab pain).
  - Non-painful-but-aggravating: doesn't hurt at the time but worsens the injury (pain and function) over time — often traceable to daily habits, weaknesses, poor movement quality/technique, or self-prescribed rehab done with improper volume/frequency/loading.
  - Both painful AND aggravating: causes more pain afterward while also degrading function/performance — avoid.
  - [CONVENTION] Core progression principle: the key to rehab progress (chronic pain especially, sometimes acute) is **actively increasing strength, endurance, and stability/control with the movement, with or without pain present** — pain is not necessarily indicative of injury/harm, and it typically resolves as rehab continues (p.35).

## Tendinopathy Pain — Mechanisms and Thresholds (p.35-39)

- [SCIENCE] Rio et al 2014 review (p.35, ref 88): surveyed candidate pain contributors — tendon matrix changes, neovascularization, tendon cell structure/function changes, cytokines, neuropeptides/neurotransmitters, metabolites, ion channels, and central pain mediators (brain/spinal cord). Conclusion (quoted): nociception could arise from cell-cell ion-channel signaling to afferent neurons, be modulated spinally or centrally via descending mechanisms, or even be evoked via non-nociceptive load-detection-system dysfunction. **No single cause was isolated** — evidence supports both tendon-based nociceptive contributions and broader peripheral/CNS mechanisms (p.35).
- [SCIENCE] Raney et al 2017 (biceps tendinopathy) similarly surveys nociceptive/neuropathic/chronic pain pathways (transduction, transmission, perception, modulation), cell/molecular mediators (tachykinins, Substance P, CGRP, alarmins), and four competing theories (mechanical, vascular, apoptosis, neural) without resolving a single mechanism — concluding more research is needed (p.35-36, ref 89).
- [CONVENTION]/[SCIENCE] Per-protocol pain tolerance rules recapped from the Malliaras review (Ch.4 material, referenced again here) (p.36):
  - Alfredson model: load up to "moderate pain."
  - Stanish and Curwin: load painful "in the third set."
  - Silbernagel: pain "acceptable within defined limits."
  - Heavy-slow resistance (HSR): pain "acceptable if it was not worse after."
- [SCIENCE] Mascaro et al systematic graded approach ground a **0-10 numerical pain scale** (for non-chronic pain) (p.36, ref 90):
  - 0 = no pain, 1-3 = light pain, 4-6 = moderate pain, 7-9 = intense pain (resulting in modified function), 10 = highest pain.
  - **Safe training intensity = 0-3.** 4-6 is "excessive training intensity." 7-10 is generally too much — causes poor technique, compensations, or bad movement patterns (p.36) *(figure — numeric scale diagram, see PDF p.36)*.
- [CONVENTION] The authors' personal preference goes further than the numeric-scale consensus: even though painful exercise isn't necessarily damaging, they prefer to **avoid pain-eliciting exercises during rehab whenever a non-painful alternative achieves the same goal** — rationale: pain can degrade technique via compensation even at low/moderate levels, and can undermine motivation and psychosocial state (p.36).
  - Worked example: for distal biceps tendinopathy, trial multiple curl variations (preacher, seated arm-on-leg, incline, standing, decline) and grip orientations (supinated, hammer, pronated); supinated curls tend to hurt more (more direct biceps brachii loading vs. brachialis/brachioradialis for hammer/pronated), so pronated/hammer curls are used first, transitioning to supinated as load tolerance rises and pain from the former two drops (p.36-37).
- [CONVENTION] **24-hour/next-session rule** (the chapter's core actionable threshold): across protocols, consensus is pain should be "generally minimal to absent" and, critically, **should not be worse after the rehab session, the day after, or by the next session** (p.36-37). This is the load-management checkpoint used to judge whether a loading dose was appropriate.
- [CONVENTION] Because working through pain can sometimes be detrimial, the authors recommend "painful" (but beneficial) exercises be performed **under supervision of a rehab professional** — most patients/athletes lack the expertise to judge correct exercise type/volume, and self-directed use of painful-but-beneficial exercises risks pushing too far and worsening the injury (p.37).

## Managing Tendinopathy Pain with Isometrics (p.37-40)

- [SCIENCE] Rationale for isometrics: when pain is moderate-to-severe enough to limit/prevent performing rehab exercises, isometrics serve as a **pain-management bridge**, not necessarily a structural-adaptation tool (p.37).
- [SCIENCE] Rio et al single-blinded RCT cross-over (patellar tendinopathy) (p.37, ref 91): **70% MVIC isometric × 5 sets × 45s, 2 min rest** vs. isotonic 4×8 at 8RM with 4s eccentric/3s concentric. Four findings:
  1. Both reduced pain on a follow-up single-leg-squat test, but isometric produced significantly greater reduction (7.0±2.04→0.17±0.41) vs isotonic (6.33±2.80→3.75±3.28, p<0.001).
  2. Isometric decreased **cortical inhibition**; isotonic did not — mechanistically this matters because cortical inhibition reduces motor drive/strength output, so lowering it lets patients perform subsequent exercise better.
  3. Isometric analgesia **persisted up to 45 minutes** post-exercise; isotonic's did not extend beyond the session.
  4. MVIC strength increased significantly vs. baseline and vs. the isotonic group.
  - Conclusion (quoted): "Isometric muscle contractions may be used to reduce pain in people with PT without a reduction in muscle strength" — useful **immediately before** a painful rehab exercise (e.g., single-leg squat) to prime pain-free performance (p.37).
- [SCIENCE] Follow-up Rio et al study on in-season athletes (p.37-38, ref 92): **80% MVIC × 5 sets × 45s** vs. 4×8 @ 80% 1RM isotonic. Similar direction of effect — isometric gave greater pain relief over a **4-week period**. Similar studies replicate this (p.38, refs 93-94).
- [SCIENCE] Pearson et al (p.38, ref 95): **24 sets × 10s** gave comparable pain relief to **6 sets × 40s at 85% MVIC** over 4 weeks — meaning shorter-duration/higher-set isometric protocols are a viable alternative for populations that don't tolerate longer holds well.
- [SCIENCE] Lim et al systematic review, grading evidence quality by intervention for patellar tendinopathy (p.38, ref 96):
  - Isometrics: **Level A** (strong evidence).
  - Eccentrics: **Level B** (moderate evidence) "in most clinical situations."
  - HSR: **Level C** (weak evidence) — use "in individual circumstances with care."
  - Isometrics favor **short-term pain relief**, especially useful concurrent with in-season training where volume reduction is undesirable; eccentrics and HSR favor **long-term pain relief and improved function** — i.e., isometrics and eccentric/HSR are complementary on different timescales, not competing choices (p.38).
- [CONVENTION] Three recommended primary use-cases for isometrics (p.38):
  1. In-season athletes, to reduce pain while maintaining sport-specific training volume.
  2. As a pre-exercise pain-relief primer immediately before eccentric-concentric or HSR rehab work, especially when pain is impairing control/quality of those exercises.
  3. Standalone, multiple times per day, including on non-rehab days, purely for pain reduction.
- [CONVENTION] Angle/position guidance: most isometric patellar-tendinopathy studies used **60-degree knee flexion** — mid-range for a decline board squat, or near end-range for a single-leg squat/step-down. Findings are believed to generalize to other body regions by holding the analogous exercise near mid-range of motion, though individual variation in which angle minimizes pain should be respected and used (p.38-39).
- [CONVENTION] **Load: ~70-85% 1RM/MVIC** is the effective range for pain reduction — cross-referenced to the NSCA training-load chart, this corresponds to a load that would allow 6-12 reps if performed dynamically (p.39, ref 97).
- [CONVENTION] **Volume: total isometric hold time of 3-4 minutes per session**, derived from the protocols reviewed: 5×45s (3:45 total), 6×40s (4:00 total), 24×10s (4:00 total). The authors state a preference for **fewer sets with longer individual hold durations**, while noting no scientific literature has established whether lower or higher total volumes would be more/less effective — this should be individualized by clinical judgment/response (p.39-40).
