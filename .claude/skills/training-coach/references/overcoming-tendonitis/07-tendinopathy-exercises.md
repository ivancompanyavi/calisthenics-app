# Overcoming Tendonitis Ch.7 — Tendinopathy Exercises

> **Source:** *Overcoming Tendonitis* (Steven Low), Ch.7, book pp.59-80. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/overcoming-tendonitis.txt` or PDF pp.59-80.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] established research · [CONVENTION] Low's system · [DISPUTED] contested.
> **⚠️ Not medical advice — this book distinguishes rehab-programming (coachable) from medical treatment (refer to a physician/physio).**

## Scope & how this chapter is organized (p.59)

This chapter is an **exercise catalog**, not a dosage table — it gives named rehab exercises per tendon plus
form cues and progression logic, but leaves explicit sets/reps/%-load numbers to the reader's clinical judgment
and to other chapters. Two general programming rules recur across every region below:

- **[CONVENTION] Order of loading:** for compound movement transition (pushups, pull-ups, rows, dips,
  overhead press, squats, deadlifts), scale difficulty via variation (wall pushup → incline pushup → stair
  pushup → full pushup) rather than by prescribing a fixed load; the specific exercise chosen depends on
  what's needed to return to work/sport (p.59).
- **[CONVENTION] Bilateral testing rule:** perform every exercise on **both** limbs, even when only one is
  affected, to compare ROM/stability/endurance/strength and catch compensatory imbalance — training only
  the affected side risk-inverts the asymmetry (it can end up *stronger* than the healthy side) (p.59).
- **[CONVENTION] Order within a session:** do the affected side **first**, unaffected side second — this
  matches volume/intensity to the affected side's true capacity. Starting with the unaffected (stronger) side
  and then trying to match its output on the affected side risks overload/reinjury, especially in competitive
  athletes (p.59-60).
- **[CONVENTION] Single-limb-too-hard fallback:** when a single-limb exercise (e.g., single-leg calf raise)
  is currently too aggravating, assist the concentric with the unaffected limb and let the affected limb do the
  eccentric alone; remove the assist as tolerance improves (p.60). This same "assist-concentric /
  affected-limb-eccentric" pattern is reused for Achilles, posterior tibialis, patellar, and hamstring exercises
  below.
- **[CONVENTION] Progressive overload tooling:** exercises are shown bodyweight/no-load; progress by
  adding small dumbbells, bands, or other implements once ROM/pain allow (p.60).
- **[CONVENTION] "Appropriate eccentric time"** is used throughout as the tempo cue for the lowering
  phase of nearly every exercise in this chapter — the book does not give a fixed second-count here; it treats
  eccentric tempo as a variable to be tuned to the individual's current pain/load tolerance (this chapter,
  passim).

## Rotator cuff — supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor (p.61-63)

[SCIENCE] Both supraspinatus and infraspinatus/teres minor are the most common rotator-cuff tendons to
develop tendinopathy; the book recommends training **both** together during rehab (p.61). [SCIENCE]
Subacromial bursa involvement is common with rotator-cuff tendinopathy, which is why NSAIDs (anti-
inflammatory) tend to work better here than for tendinopathy elsewhere — bursal inflammation responds to
anti-inflammatories even though the underlying tendinopathy may not (p.61). [CONVENTION] Scapular
strengthening/technique meaningfully reduces shoulder pain because scapular mechanics drive overall
shoulder movement quality — true for tendinopathy and other shoulder injuries alike (p.61).

**Supraspinatus:**
- *Sidelying Empty Can* — lie on unaffected side, affected arm on top, thumb rotated toward the side (internal
  rotation bias), raise arm to ~45° away from body, lower with controlled eccentric (p.61).
- *Standing Scaption Raise, thumb down* — raise arm in the scaption plane (midway between front raise and
  side raise, ~45° off each) to 65-70°, staying below shoulder height, pinky leading; lower eccentrically.
  Flagged as an exercise that **"can aggravate the shoulder, so be careful"** — substitute if it does (p.62).

**Infraspinatus:**
- *Seated External Rotation, elbow on knee* — elbow at 90°, hand rotates inward through range, then back out
  (p.62).
- *Cuban Press* — arms abducted 90° with elbows bent 90°, rotate hands from pointed-down to pointed-up
  (external rotation) and back down eccentrically. [CONVENTION] Flagged as a **more advanced** exercise
  because the top position (90° abduction + 90° external rotation) is one of the shoulder's most unstable
  positions; **contraindicated as a starting exercise** if there is any subluxation/dislocation history, since pain
  + instability already impair motor control there (p.62-63).

**Teres minor:**
- *Sidelying External Rotation* — arm pinned to side, elbow bent 90°, rotate hand from stomach up past
  neutral; go to full ROM or stop just short of discomfort; lower eccentrically. Cue: only the hand/forearm
  rotates, arm stays pinned at the side throughout (p.63).
- *Standing External Rotation with band/pulley* — same motion resisted by band/pulley, perpendicular
  stance, arm pinned to side (p.63).
- [CONVENTION] Gymnastics rings are called out as useful when resuming pull-ups/chin-ups because free
  rotation lets wrists/elbows/shoulders avoid getting locked into a movement pattern that aggravates
  golfer's/tennis elbow, biceps, and rotator-cuff tendinopathies (p.63; repeated for elbow/biceps regions below).
- [CONVENTION] If infraspinatus/teres minor exercises aren't hitting the target area, try lying on the side to
  move the arm into greater flexion so gravity assists external rotation (p.63).

## Golfer's elbow (medial epicondyle) & anterior wrist (p.63-66)

[SCIENCE] The common flexor tendon is the shared origin of flexor carpi ulnaris, palmaris longus, flexor carpi
radialis, pronator teres, and flexor digitorum superficialis (FDS) — so the reactive/degenerative portion can sit
anywhere along that tendon, and **muscle-specific exercises outperform generic ones** when generic wrist
curls stall (p.63). [CONVENTION] Location maps to exercise choice: pain superficial/lateral (pronator teres
area) → pronation/supination bias; pain deeper → finger-specific work (finger curls / resisted finger-pushing
into the ground) (p.63-64).

**Three clinical themes (p.64):**
- **Wrist-flexion type** (flexor carpi radialis/ulnaris) → resolved with wrist-curl eccentric-concentrics +
  pronation/supination as secondary.
- **Pronation/supination type** (pronator teres) → pronation/supination as primary, wrist curls secondary.
- **Finger-flexor type** (FDS) → finger-curl-type work or finger-push-into-ground eccentric-concentrics
  (hits FDS + pronation/supination together); wrist curls as supplementary if needed.
- [SCIENCE] Gymnasts, climbers, calisthenics athletes, and high-pull-up-volume athletes commonly present
  with pronator-teres- or FDS-based golfer's elbow (p.64).
- [CONVENTION] Sequencing rule: bias loading toward the affected sub-region if tolerated; if not tolerated,
  start with exercises that **de-emphasize** that region and introduce targeted loading gradually as tolerance
  improves — directly analogous to starting biceps-curl rehab with pronated/hammer curls before introducing
  supinated curls (p.64).

**Exercises:**
- *Wrist Curl* (seated preferred for forearm stabilization; standing as fallback if seated aggravates or plateaus
  after a few weeks) — palm up, curl against gravity through full ROM, eccentric lower (p.64-65). Standing
  variant can load with dumbbell or barbell, palms-forward or palms-backward (reverse grip) (p.65).
- *Pronation/Supination* — forearm on table, dumbbell or hammer held perpendicular, rotate 180° with
  eccentric control on the return. [CONVENTION] Should be paired with wrist curls because pronator teres
  shares the common flexor tendon origin; especially effective when pain is lateral on the common flexor
  tendon, but described as a solid general exercise regardless (p.65).
- *Finger Curls / Finger Rolls* (FDS-targeted) — no photos provided in text; referenced only via external video
  links (p.66).
- [CONVENTION] Gymnastics rings again flagged as helpful during pull-up/chin-up return for the same
  free-rotation reason (p.65).

## Tennis elbow (lateral epicondyle) & posterior wrist (p.66-67)

[SCIENCE] Both tennis and golfer's elbow are easily misdiagnosed — several differential diagnoses in the
lateral epicondyle region mimic tennis elbow, so clinical symptoms should be confirmed before assuming
tendinopathy (p.66).

- *Reverse Wrist Curl* (seated preferred, standing fallback) — palm down, raise against gravity through
  current ROM, eccentric lower. [CONVENTION] Progression cue: once tolerated, extend ROM by lowering
  *past* parallel — "this is where you will eventually want to be" (p.66).
- Standing variant: arms at side, palms facing backward, extend hand forward through ROM, eccentric
  return; barbell loading changes bar position depending on palm orientation (p.66).
- *Pronation/Supination* (same setup as golfer's elbow variant, palm-up start) — [CONVENTION] noted as
  effective specifically because it provides **incremental loading** as the forearm rotates, making it a good
  basic exercise to (re)introduce load to the tendon, even though it doesn't directly train the extensors (p.67).
- Gymnastics rings cue repeated again for pull-up/chin-up return (p.67).

## Triceps (p.67-68)

- *Triceps Pressdowns* — band/pulley with overhead line of pull, arm pinned to side, extend elbow fully, bend
  back with eccentric control (p.67-68).
- *Skullcrushers* — supine, arm at 90° to body, allow gravity to bend elbow (eccentric) then straighten
  concentrically (p.68).
- *Overhead Triceps Extension* — standing, arm fully overhead, allow gravity to bend elbow with forearm
  tracking in line with the head, then straighten concentrically (p.68).

## Biceps (p.68-71)

**Proximal:**
- *Overhead Straight-arm Lower and Raise* — start with arm at ~120-150° in the scaption plane, elbow bent
  90°; lower shoulder to 90°, then extend elbow straight, then lower arm to the side. [CONVENTION] Eccentric
  emphasis can be placed in any/multiple of the three phases depending on which best matches how the
  proximal tendon responds (p.68-69).
- [SCIENCE/CONVENTION] Proximal biceps tendinopathy rehab usually benefits from **adding rotator cuff
  exercises**, because the proximal biceps tendon attaches to the superior labrum and contributes to shoulder
  stability; strengthening the rotator cuff reduces the load the biceps tendon must otherwise absorb (p.69).

**Distal:**
- *Supinated*, *Hammer*, and *Pronated Biceps Curls* — standing, curl against gravity to ROM limit or onset of
  discomfort, eccentric lower (p.69-70).
- [CONVENTION] Sequencing: supinated/hammer curls can aggravate distal biceps tendinopathy even at low
  intensity for some; start with **pronated** curls, progress to **hammer**, then to **supinated** as pain
  decreases and function improves (p.70).
- *Supination/Pronation* (dumbbell/hammer-head rotation, same setup as forearm exercises above) —
  [SCIENCE] biceps' secondary function is forearm supination, so this exercise trains an eccentric distal-biceps
  stimulus directly; usable alongside curls or as a gentler starting exercise when tendinopathy is severe (p.70).
- [CONVENTION] If standard standing curls don't hit the painful ROM (e.g., near full extension or full
  flexion), switch to preacher curls or incline-bench curls to bias those specific ranges (p.70).
- Decline-bench curls / preacher curls noted generically as usable to vary ROM emphasis (p.70).
- Gymnastics-rings cue repeated once more for pull-up/chin-up return (p.71).

## Achilles (p.71-73)

[SCIENCE] Two sub-regions: **insertional** (at the calcaneal insertion) and **mid-portion**. Insertional tends
to respond worse to rehab because compression around the Achilles bursa makes the area more reactive;
mid-portion tends to respond better — clinicians must be careful not to flare up insertional cases (p.71).

**Insertional Achilles:**
- *Flat Ground Calf Raises* — full ROM heel raise on flat ground, eccentric lower. [CONVENTION] Progression
  path: bilateral first if moderate/severe (single-leg alone can over-aggravate) → increase reps/load bilaterally
  → single-leg. If the concentric (raising) is too aggravating, use the unaffected leg to assist the concentric and
  let the affected leg control the eccentric alone; progress to full single-leg as tolerated (p.71-72).
- *Calf Raise Machine* — seated or standing; **do not let the heel dip below the front of the foot** (avoids
  end-range dorsiflexion/insertional compression) (p.72).

**Mid-portion Achilles:**
- *Box/Step Calf Raises or Incline Board Calf Raises* — stand on a step/incline board so the heel can dip
  *below* toe level (adds stretch-load absent from flat-ground insertional version), full ROM raise, eccentric
  lower. Same bilateral→single-leg progression and concentric-assist fallback as insertional version (p.72-73).
- *Calf Raise Machine* — same as above but heel is allowed to dip below the front of the foot (contrast with
  the insertional-Achilles machine cue) (p.73).

## Posterior tibialis (p.74-75)

[SCIENCE] Posterior tibialis' actions are plantarflexion **and** ankle inversion, so rehab must target both
movements specifically (p.74).

- *Calf Raises* — same as mid-portion Achilles version; same bilateral→single-leg progression and
  concentric-assist fallback (p.74-75).
- *Thera-Band Ankle Inversion Eccentrics* — band looped around the affected foot, unaffected leg extended
  and crossed under; invert the affected foot toward the big toe against the band, then allow the band to pull
  it back with eccentric control (p.75).

## Patellar (p.75-77)

- *Decline Board Squats* — feet on a decline board (toes lower, heels higher), squat to parallel (or as far as
  able) with eccentric-emphasized descent, controlled ascent; keep weight balanced through the whole foot,
  toes/heels stay planted. [CONVENTION] Knees are explicitly allowed to travel in front of the toes on the
  decline board (p.75-76). On a level floor (fallback if no decline board / too difficult), the cue flips: **keep
  knees in line with the toes**, avoiding significant forward knee travel, to reduce knee stress (p.76).
  [CONVENTION] Progress toward single-leg squats or single-leg decline-board squats over time (p.76).
- *Knee Extension Eccentrics* (machine) — extend concentrically (assist with the unaffected leg if needed,
  then disengage it once the knee is straight), lower the affected leg alone with eccentric control (p.76).
- *Stair Step-downs* — stand on a stair, step down using the unaffected leg while the affected leg eccentrically
  controls the descent; turn around to step back up (stepping back up with the affected leg can be
  aggravating, hence the turn-around). [CONVENTION] Alternative if turning around each rep is tedious: start
  at the top of the stairs to chain multiple eccentric reps without turning, or push off hard through the
  unaffected leg's railing-assisted ascent (p.76-77).

## Hamstrings (p.77-79)

**Proximal hamstring:**
- *Eccentric Hip Bridges* — supine, knees bent 90°, lift hips to a straight line knee-hip-shoulder, then lower
  with eccentric control. Progress by adding external load (dumbbell/plate/barbell on the hip) or moving to a
  single-leg hip bridge (p.77).
- *Treadmill Backward Walking* — walk backward on the ground first to learn the pattern, then on a slow
  treadmill using handrails; dosed by **time and speed**, not reps, since it isn't a discrete-rep exercise (p.77).
  [SCIENCE] The treadmill (not overground) version is required because the belt's motion actively drags the
  foot away from the body, producing true eccentric hamstring lengthening; overground backward walking
  instead relies on leaning back and pushing off, which does **not** eccentrically load the hamstring the same
  way (p.77-78).
- *Romanian Deadlift* — hip-hinge lower with straight back/chest up and eccentric control down to knee
  height, controlled ascent. [CONVENTION] Positioned as the fallback/alternative when hip bridges and
  backward walking aren't effective, because the hip-hinge pattern specifically targets the proximal hamstring
  origin, which can otherwise be hard to load. Flagged as technically demanding — get coaching before
  self-teaching form. Progression: lower the bar closer to the ground (within an inch or two) over time (p.78).

**Distal hamstring:**
- *Prone Leg Curl Eccentrics* — prone, affected knee bent 90° (add ankle weight for more resistance); lower
  the leg eccentrically (assist concentric with the unaffected leg if needed, disengaging once knee is straight),
  contract to return. Without weights/bands, the unaffected leg can push against the affected leg to supply
  resistance directly (p.78-79).
- *Machine Leg Curl Eccentrics* — same eccentric-focused pattern on a leg-curl machine: concentric curl
  (assist with unaffected leg as needed) to ≥90° bend, disengage assist, then straighten the affected leg alone
  with eccentric control (p.79).
