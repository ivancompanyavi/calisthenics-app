# Overcoming Tendonitis Ch.8 — Outcome Measures and Intervention Charts

> **Source:** *Overcoming Tendonitis* (Steven Low), Ch.8, book pp.81-132. Distilled 2026-07-09.
> **Verify:** `_sources/overcoming-tendonitis.txt` or PDF pp.81-132.
> **Tiers:** [SCIENCE] established research · [CONVENTION] Low's system · [DISPUTED] contested.
> **⚠️ Not medical advice — this book distinguishes rehab-programming (coachable) from medical treatment (refer to a physician/physio).**

## Grade-of-evidence system used in this chapter (p.82-83)

[CONVENTION] Simplified from the Ch.1 five-level (I-V) / six-grade (A-F) evidence scale into four
evidence tiers — **strong, moderate, weak, none** — crossed against five effect categories:
**favorable** (positive effect), **limited** (slight positive, more study warranted), **no effect**,
**mixed/conflicting** (positive in some studies, none in others), **negative** (worsens the
tendinopathy) (p.82). Each major intervention's chart breaks effect out by body region and by
**pain / function / short-term / long-term** outcome axes (p.82-83). Evidence was weighted per the
Ch.1 hierarchy: systematic reviews & meta-analyses > RCTs > lower-quality studies; level I/II evidence
dominates for Achilles and patellar (most-studied), while e.g. hamstring evidence is weak because few
studies exist (p.83). [CONVENTION] The authors explicitly caveat the charts are not "gospel truth" and
recommend reading the primary literature (p.82).

Abbreviations: AT = Achilles Tendinopathy, PT = Patellar Tendinopathy, LET = Lateral Elbow
Tendinopathy (MET = medial elbow), HS = Hamstring Tendinopathy, RCT = Rotator Cuff Tendinopathy
(note: overloaded abbreviation, NOT "randomized controlled trial" in this chapter), Other = remaining
tendinopathies (medial elbow, posterior tibialis, wrist, etc.) (p.82).

## Major interventions — mechanism, dosing, adverse effects (pp.86-99)

### Eccentric loading & exercise therapy (p.86-87)
[SCIENCE] The only major intervention with a **strong** body of evidence for beneficial effect,
particularly for Achilles and patellar tendons; eccentric-concentric combined loading is regarded as
the near-universal gold standard and first-line treatment before any other intervention is considered
(p.87). [SCIENCE] Evidence shows added benefit when eccentrics are combined with other passive
interventions (p.87). [CONVENTION] Recommendation: use an eccentric-concentric loading program as
the first option for any tendon issue for both short- and long-term healing; other interventions may
give short-term pain relief but should not replace loading exercise (p.87). [SCIENCE] No need to fully
withdraw from sport during eccentric training — modify frequency/intensity/volume of sport-specific
work instead of full rest (p.87). [SCIENCE] Tendon-thickness measurements do not correlate well with
symptomatic/clinical outcome (p.87). [CONVENTION] A systematic review favors the **Alfredson protocol**
for Achilles: 3×15 full-range calf raises, 3×/day, at both bent- and straight-knee positions; heavy
slow resistance (HSR) may be preferred for patellar — protocol choice is at clinician discretion
(p.87, cross-ref Ch.6). Adverse effect: DOMS (p.87). Prevalent outcome measures: VAS/MMT (all);
VISA-A/LEFS (Achilles); VISA-P (patellar); DASH (shoulder); TEFS/elbow disability (elbow) (p.87).

### Corticosteroid injections — CST (p.88-89)
[SCIENCE] Substantial evidence CST weakens tendons over time with repeated use, up to rupture risk;
simultaneously significant evidence of a **favorable short-term** pain effect with **no significant
long-term benefit** (and possible negative long-term effects) (p.89). [CONVENTION] Recommendation:
try other short-term pain interventions first; if those fail and there is a significant function
deficit from pain, 1-2 injections may be an option while other tendon-healing interventions are
implemented — more than 1-2 injections risks minor-to-major adverse long-term effects (p.89).
[SCIENCE] Many studies find CST no more effective than NSAIDs for pain relief; one systematic review
suggests longer-lasting benefit specifically for rotator cuff tendinopathy; ultrasound-guided injection
may improve accuracy (p.89). Adverse effects: tendon weakness/rupture risk with long-term use, joint
infection, nerve damage, bone deficiency (p.89). Outcome measures: VAS (all); VISA-A, pain-pressure
threshold, ultrasound tendon diameter (Achilles); pain-pressure threshold (patellar); DASH, NRS,
PRFEQ (elbow); DASH, shoulder disability questionnaire, Oxford shoulder score (shoulder) (p.89).

### Extracorporeal shockwave therapy — ESWT/ECST (p.90-91)
[SCIENCE] Favorable but limited evidence overall; extensive research base relative to other
interventions, but a more recent review found **high risk of bias** in much of the earlier ESWT
research (p.91). [CONVENTION] Recommendation: use with caution, only after more-proven measures
(eccentric loading) have been tried first; preferred over riskier options like surgery (p.91).
[SCIENCE] Adding an eccentric loading program to ESWT is recommended for accelerated benefit;
consensus that ESWT has higher potential effect at **calcific** sites (insertional Achilles, rotator
cuff) (p.91). Note: ESWT is, in many cases, the **most expensive** of the major interventions (p.91).
Adverse effects: skin reddening, pain, small hematomas (p.91). Outcome measures: VAS (all);
VISA-A/Likert/NRS/pain threshold/AOFAS/FIL/AHS (Achilles); VISA-P/Likert/vertical jump/AOFAS/FIL
(patellar); DASH/UEFS/Roles-and-Maudsley (elbow); patient satisfaction/CMS/UCLA scale (shoulder) (p.91).

### Low-level laser therapy — LLLT (p.92-93)
[SCIENCE] Evidence is diverse/weak overall due to variable wavelength, session count, timing, and
technique; weak-to-moderate evidence for short-term pain relief specifically at **insertional
Achilles and elbow** sites, function benefit less convincing (p.93). [CONVENTION] Virtually no
adverse effects, cost-effective — but not recommended as a long-term solution since it lacks
tendon-healing properties; a reasonable adjunct for short-term pain relief alongside
eccentric-concentric work, especially if other pain-relief options have failed (p.93). [SCIENCE]
Best wavelength range cited as 810-904nm, 904nm most preferred (p.93). Adverse effect: eye injury if
looking directly into the beam (p.93). Outcome measures: VAS (all); VISA-A (Achilles); PRTEE/
pain-pressure threshold/pain-free grip strength (elbow); CMS (shoulder) (p.93).

### PRP, autologous blood injection (ABI), prolotherapy (p.94-95)
[SCIENCE][DISPUTED] Increased research has yielded **conflicting** results — some studies show
benefit, overall effect limited, others show no benefit at all; grouped together due to similar
mechanism (p.95). [CONVENTION] Recommendation: not a first-line option; may be considered for
Achilles, patellar, elbow if conservative options are exhausted first, and should be paired with an
eccentric-concentric loading program; insufficient research to recommend for other sites (p.95).
[SCIENCE] Some evidence platelet injections may benefit **ruptured** tendons more than
tendinopathies; a few studies found PRP more effective in females than males (mechanism unclear)
(p.95). Adverse effects: skin itchiness, transient post-needling pain (p.95). Outcome measures: VAS/
patient satisfaction (all); VISA-A (Achilles); PRTEE/pain-pressure threshold (elbow); DASH (shoulder)
(p.95).

### Therapeutic ultrasound (p.96-97)
[SCIENCE] Not a wealth of evidence, and what exists suggests limited effectiveness; Achilles and
patellar show **no response**; elbow/shoulder show conflicting short-term benefit at best (p.97).
[CONVENTION] Not recommended at all for tendon issues — believed no better than placebo, poor
cost-benefit for both provider and patient time/money; only scenario recommended is prior to
considering surgery once all other upper-body options are exhausted (p.97). [SCIENCE] One systematic
review concludes benefits are no better than placebo and it fails to enhance exercise effectiveness;
one study suggested possible benefit for calcific rotator cuff specifically, another for cryoultrasound
in insertional Achilles (p.97). Adverse effect: can burn skin if left in one spot too long (p.97).
Outcome measures: VAS (all); VISA-A (Achilles); VISA-P (patellar); pain-free grip strength (elbow);
DASH (shoulder) (p.97).

### Surgery (p.98-99)
[CONVENTION] Absolute last resort for all tendon areas — inherent tissue-damage risk from
invasiveness; majority of cases succeed but a real percentage see no symptom change or significant
side effects (p.99). [SCIENCE] Many studies conclude surgical outcomes are no better than conservative
interventions, particularly guided exercise, and it's unclear how excising degenerative tissue affects
vascularity/pain resolution (p.99). [CONVENTION] Only consider surgery after a concerted effort of time
and commitment, with all other measures exhausted, and pain still affecting daily function (p.99).
[SCIENCE] One systematic review found surgery superior to no-treatment/placebo but **similar to sham
("fake") surgery**; clinicians in that review recommended patients try loading-exercise treatment for
a **minimum of one year** before seriously considering surgery (p.99). Achilles surgery is the most
researched/performed tendon surgery, ~75% success rate; may be less beneficial for non-athletes since
the non-degenerative tendon portion in athletes is already exercise-conditioned to bear extra load; no
consensus on best surgical technique for many sites, especially insertional Achilles (p.99). Adverse
effects: DVT, nerve/muscle damage, wound breakdown, re-operation, frozen shoulder (shoulder),
potential inability to return to pre-surgery sport level (p.99). Outcome measures: VAS/patient
satisfaction (all); VISA-A/VISA-P/LEFS (lower extremity); DASH (upper extremity) (p.99).

## Minor interventions — condensed notes (pp.102-117)

[CONVENTION] These have less/lacking research so the book gives a simpler pros/cons + tendon-specific
research + final-recommendation format rather than full charts (p.102).

- **Botox** (p.102-103): Mechanism — neurotoxin blocks acetylcholine release, causing local muscle
  paralysis theorized to reduce continuous muscle tension on poorly-load-tolerant tendons and increase
  pain relief (p.102). [SCIENCE] Moderate evidence: not better than placebo for Achilles pain/function.
  Moderate evidence: short-term pain relief for LET vs placebo, but can cause extensor wrist/finger
  weakness; limited evidence botox is less effective than CST for short-term LET pain. Limited evidence:
  short-term RCT (rotator cuff) pain relief, less effective than CST, no long-term effect (p.102).
  [CONVENTION] Option for upper-extremity issues if other approaches fail; caution given possible
  extended muscle weakness if athletic/professional function is a priority (p.103).
- **Ergonomics** (p.103-104): [SCIENCE] Weak evidence overuse/vibration at work can cause shoulder
  (RCT) tendonitis; limited evidence of causal link to De Quervain's tenosynovitis; lack of high-quality
  reviews for LET (p.104). [CONVENTION] Worth pursuing for general health but no tendon-specific
  recommendation can be made given the evidence gap (p.104).
- **Iontophoresis** (p.104-105): Mechanism — mild electrical current drives charged medication (e.g.
  dexamethasone, sodium diclofenac/salicylate) through skin (p.104). [SCIENCE] Weak evidence of
  favorable pain/function effect at 6 months for Achilles tendinosis; limited evidence of very
  short-term-only benefit for LET (gone by 1 month); weak evidence of no effect for calcific rotator
  cuff (p.104). [CONVENTION] Moderate evidence it's useful for **acute** tendon pain (see Neeter
  protocol); weak/limited for chronic pain — recommended only for acute Achilles pain at clinician
  discretion after other treatments fail (p.105).
- **Manual therapy / massage** (p.105-107): [SCIENCE] Moderate-to-strong evidence manual
  therapy+mobilization/manipulation added to concentric/eccentric exercise favorably affects pain and
  function for elbow (LET/MET) — the strongest site-specific evidence in this category (p.105-106).
  Deep transverse friction massage specifically shows **no added benefit** over standard therapy for
  LET pain/function/grip strength, nor for iliotibial band friction syndrome (p.105-106). Conflicting
  evidence for rotator cuff (possible pain-only effect) (p.105). [CONVENTION] Recommend standard manual
  therapy + muscle massage as adjuncts — mainly for symptom reduction/motivation/flexibility, not
  tendon load-tolerance improvement, so must pair with eccentric-concentric work; do **not** recommend
  deep cross-friction massage specifically; ASTYM therapy (not IASTM) shows promise but insufficient
  evidence yet (p.106-107).
- **Needling — acupuncture / dry needling (TDN)** (p.107-108): [SCIENCE] Moderate evidence acupuncture
  + eccentric exercise favorably affects short-term Achilles pain/function; limited evidence
  acupuncture can beat eccentrics for <16 weeks; limited evidence eccentrics+TDN gives short-term
  benefit only. Weak evidence TDN aids long-term patellar pain. Moderate evidence acupuncture beats
  placebo/ultrasound for LET short-term (2-4wk) but not at 3-12 months. Limited evidence for
  short-term-only rotator cuff benefit (p.107-108). [CONVENTION] Consider as an alternative for
  upper-extremity issues when other treatments fail, or as an adjunct to exercise for lower-extremity
  injuries; TDN evidence is newer/promising but needs more research across all sites (p.108).
- **Nitroglycerin/GTN patch** (p.108-109): Mechanism — topical patch, nitric oxide implicated in
  tissue healing/collagen formation in animal studies, mechanism in humans still unclear (p.108).
  [SCIENCE] Limited-to-moderate evidence of analgesic effect (short & long-term) for mid-portion
  Achilles; limited evidence of no effect on patellar tendinopathy up to 6 months; limited-to-moderate
  analgesic evidence for LET; weak-to-limited short-term analgesic evidence for rotator cuff (weaker
  than CST), no long-term benefit (p.108-109). [CONVENTION] Better response in **chronic** vs acute
  cases for Achilles/LET/shoulder; better effect when added to eccentric loading. Headaches are the
  major adverse effect, sometimes strong enough to force stopping treatment — use with caution
  especially with a relevant medical history (p.109).
- **Pain medication / NSAIDs** (p.109-110): Mechanism — block enzymes producing inflammatory pain
  signals (p.109). [SCIENCE] Limited evidence NSAIDs match CST for pain/function in LET at <3 months,
  neither significant long-term; limited evidence NSAIDs effective for RCT pain/function short-term
  only (p.110). [CONVENTION] Since tendinopathy is primarily non-inflammatory/degenerative, NSAIDs
  have limited effect beyond pain relief and possible negative effect on long-run collagen synthesis;
  may help only where surrounding paratendon/compressive-load inflammation coexists (p.110).
  Recommendation: short-term pain management only, alongside other treatment; consult a doctor/PT,
  especially for chronic pain where extended use risks dependency (p.110).
- **Stretching** (p.110-111): Static / dynamic / ballistic types; ballistic **not recommended** for
  any injury (p.110). [SCIENCE] Weak evidence bent/straight-knee calf stretching reduces Achilles pain
  and improves satisfaction with impaired dorsiflexion; stretching alone shows less effect than
  stretching+strengthening for Achilles, patellar, elbow; limited evidence no significant
  stretching-vs-strengthening difference for elbow; dynamic may outperform static for pain/function,
  reasoned as more similar to eccentric loading (p.110-111). [CONVENTION] Small positive effect,
  particularly with limited ROM or combined with eccentric exercise/manual therapy; static stretching
  alone not significant — recommend both static+dynamic as part of a program centered on strengthening
  (p.111).
- **Supplements** (p.111-113): Candidates: glucosamine, antioxidants, vitamin C, collagen, hyaluronic
  acid (p.111). [SCIENCE] Very limited single-trial evidence of mild positive effect (fatty
  acids+antioxidants) for patellar and elbow; limited two-trial evidence for rotator cuff; in-vitro/rat
  studies suggest vitamin C+collagen and hyaluronic acid/glucosamine chondroitin may raise collagen
  synthesis, but effects are greater for lacerated/torn/ruptured tendons than tendinopathy in rats
  (p.111-112). [CONVENTION] Not recommended as a tendinopathy intervention at this time — evidence
  weak/heterogeneous, unknown interaction risk with other supplements/medications (p.112).
  [DISPUTED] Additional commentary: [SCIENCE] Melendez-Hevia et al. estimate glycine — the proposed
  rate-limiting amino acid for collagen synthesis — has a ~10g/day requirement for a 70kg human against
  ~3g endogenous synthesis + ~1.5-3g dietary intake, a 4-5.5g shortfall (p.112). [CONVENTION] Reasoning
  from that gap: collagen/gelatin is ~26% glycine by weight vs ~2% for whey/soy protein, so the book
  suggests ~20-25g/day collagen supplementation (dose split unclear — could be one 20-25g dose, two
  10-12.5g doses, or three smaller doses) with **at least one dose taken before a rehab session**, to
  close the ~5.5g glycine deficit (p.112-113). Explicitly flagged as insufficiently proven long-term and
  possibly placebo; avoid expensive tendon/ligament-specific collagen products (p.113).
- **TENS** (p.113-114): Mechanism — electrical current via skin pads stimulating sensory nerves for
  pain relief; turning intensity high enough to cause muscle contraction becomes NMES (a strengthening
  modality), not TENS (p.113-114). [SCIENCE] Weak evidence of positive effect on Achilles tendinosis
  using burst setting; evidence too conflicting/poor for LET and RCT (p.114). [CONVENTION] Little
  tendon-specific evidence overall; regarded as able to mask pain without promoting healing — recommend
  for short-term pain relief only, favor more established treatments for long-term care (p.114).
- **Wait and see / watchful waiting** (p.114-115): [SCIENCE][DISPUTED] Poorly standardized as an
  intervention across trials (unclear if activity modification/education was included) (p.115). For LET,
  may have similar long-term pain outcome to CST injections; some evidence shows little difference vs
  general non-surgical care (possibly due to activity modification/education), conflicting with other
  research showing added eccentric exercise beats wait-and-see for LET. Limited evidence wait-and-see ≈
  CST for RCT in intermediate/long term (p.115). [CONVENTION] May be reasonable for **acute** LET/MET
  pain specifically, still with medical guidance on activity modification; not recommended over
  supported interventions for other sites given conflicting/weak evidence (p.115).
- **Calcific tendinopathy** (p.115-116): Distinct pathology — calcium deposits in tendon/muscle tissue,
  most often supraspinatus/rotator cuff; pathophysiology unclear, theorized limited vascularity +
  metabolic change + overuse (p.115). [CONVENTION] Sequence: short-term non-invasive pain relief first,
  then progress to functional exercise/loading (p.115). [SCIENCE] High-energy ESWT more favorable than
  other passive modalities and can be considered early; radial ESWT and ultrasound-guided needling are
  other options; 1-2 CST injections is a well-supported short-term option; ultrasound-guided lavage is
  newer/promising but based on low-quality evidence (p.115-116). [CONVENTION] Per the Elshewy review,
  the disease has formative and resorptive phases — tolerating pain through the resorptive phase
  usually resolves it spontaneously. If conservative treatment + alternatives fail after **6+ months
  (preferably 12+)** with no significant improvement, surgical removal (arthroscopic, for the shoulder)
  is the last resort with attendant complication/rehab-time risk (p.116).
- **Bracing** (p.116-117): [SCIENCE] Per the "gold standard" Achilles article cited in Ch.1: heel lifts
  (Evidence Level D, conflicting, no recommendation), orthoses (Level D, conflicting, no
  recommendation), night splints (Level C, weak evidence **against** use) (p.116). Expert opinion
  (Level F): elastic taping should **not** be used; rigid taping **can** be used to reduce tendon
  strain (p.116). Elbow/patellar compression bands (1-2 in. wide, tightened at the tendon) have little
  research but anecdotal climber reports of pain relief while continuing sport + doing rehab on the
  side; rated Evidence Level F, clinician/patient discretion (p.116). Full-length compression sleeves
  (6-12 in.) are **not effective** for tendinopathy, though may help keep the area warm (p.116).

## Pain management framework (pp.117-120)

[SCIENCE] Pain Neuroscience Education (PNE) core points (p.117): pain is a bidirectional threat-signal
system between body and brain that can misfire or be amplified by stress/habits/life events (Butler &
Mosely, *Explain Pain*); imaging often shows "degeneration" in asymptomatic people, so structural
findings don't determine pain or function; the farther out from an acute injury pain persists, the more
likely it involves chronic-pain mechanisms — **~3+ months** is cited as the common threshold; per Louw,
PNE's goal is reconceptualizing pain (as nervous-system sensitization, not tissue damage) rather than
expecting sudden total resolution (p.117-118).

[CONVENTION] **Aggravating vs. painful exercises** distinction (p.118): some exercises cause pain during
a session but improve function by the next session (acceptable — typical of rehab exercise); some cause
no pain in-session but worsen pain/function hours later or by the next session via overload (unacceptable
— the classic "warmed up and felt fine, worse the next day" trap); some are both — most common in acute
injury. The actionable rule is judged by the **next-session (≈24h) response**, not in-session pain alone
(p.118).

**Acute pain protocol** (p.118-119):
- [SCIENCE] Isometrics used three ways: (1) reduce pain while maintaining sport-specific training volume
  in-season; (2) pre-exercise pain relief before eccentric-concentric or heavy-slow-resistance work,
  especially if pain impairs control; (3) repeatable multiple times per day, even on non-rehab days
  (p.118).
- [SCIENCE] Isometric dosing: **total volume 3-4 minutes**, drawn from studies using 5×45s (3:45 total),
  6×40s (4:00 total), or 24×10s (4:00 total), at **70-85% 1RM (≈6-12RM)**; the authors prefer fewer,
  longer sets, adjustable to what works (p.118). [note: cf. Ch.7's cited protocol of ~70% MVC×45s×5 with
  ~5min rest for cortical/descending-inhibition analgesia lasting ~45min — this chapter gives the same
  family of dosing without restating the mechanism].
- [CONVENTION] Pain-threshold rule for acute cases: keep training pain in the **0-3/10** (no-pain to
  light-pain) range rather than 4-10, per Mascaro's systematic graded approach; cross-check against
  functional/pain improvement across sessions (p.118).
- [CONVENTION] Swap to a less-painful exercise variant if pain is too severe (e.g., pronated/hammer
  curls instead of supinated curls for biceps tendinopathy), reintroducing the original variant as load
  tolerance improves (p.119).
- [CONVENTION] Expected pain trajectory: gradual resolution through isometric → strength → functional
  strength → speed → plyometric → full return-to-sport stages. If pain isn't resolving but function keeps
  improving past **3+ months**, treat it as a chronic case (p.119).

**Chronic pain protocol** (p.119-120):
- [CONVENTION] Ensure PNE education is in place; chronic pain rarely resolves suddenly but can lessen/
  resolve over time (p.119).
- [CONVENTION] Most conservative/non-conservative adjuncts (below) will **not** help chronic pain
  specifically — defer to physician/PT (p.119).
- [CONVENTION] Use systematic graded exposure to raise exercise tolerance — e.g. if walking triggers
  Achilles/knee pain, start at 5 minutes and increase by no more than 1-2 minutes per session (or every
  other session), prioritizing slow, gradual gains (p.119).
- [SCIENCE] Encourage enjoyable non-aggravating activity with others — pain is not indicative of tissue
  damage, and movement/normal routine aids healing (p.119-120). Novel, pain-free movement signals safety
  to the nervous system and can reduce sensitization (p.120). Relaxation activities (meditation, massage,
  deep breathing) may lower nervous-system sensitization and thereby chronic pain; CBT and sensory
  discrimination training are other avenues (p.120).

**Both acute & chronic** (p.120):
- [CONVENTION] Occasional pain flare-ups are a normal part of rehab; if the loading program is gradual/
  progressive, usually just repeat a session once or twice rather than change the plan. If the flare-up
  follows an abrupt program change (new exercises, big jump in intensity/volume, sport return), re-examine
  the program (p.120).
- [CONVENTION] Rehab should not be rushed — prior injury is the single greatest predictor of future injury,
  and pushing through acute painful exercise/activity is a common route into chronic pain (p.120).

## Gold-standard decision chart (pp.120-121)

[CONVENTION] **Gold standard = eccentric-concentric loading program + isometrics for pain.** Clinicians
should tailor it by: tendon reactivity (low vs high), acute vs chronic pain status (with matched
education), tendon stage (reactive/dysrepair vs degenerative), appropriate pain interventions, correcting
technique compensations from pain, correcting weakness/instability/ROM deficits at the joint and at
proximal/distal joints, and addressing intrinsic/extrinsic injury-risk factors (p.120).

**Adjuncts to the gold standard**, tiered by the book (p.121):
- *Likely to help*: manual therapy (small effect?), stretching (if ROM-limited), pain medication (short-term).
- *May help*: iontophoresis (acute/reactive only), TENS (short-term pain), ergonomics (clinician discretion).
- *Unlikely/don't use*: wait and see, supplements (research sparse), ultrasound.
[CONVENTION] Most adjuncts only help under narrow conditions (limited ROM, acute/reactive tendon, or
short-term pain) — hence they must be paired with, never substituted for, eccentric-concentric loading
(p.121).

**If conservative treatment fails → non-conservative adjuncts** (p.121): mostly provide pain relief only,
without promoting tendon healing/load tolerance; conflicting/sparse research, mild benefit at most —
approach with caution.
- *Likely to help*: ESWT (small positive effect, possibly biased research, possibly better for calcific);
  nitroglycerin (short-term pain + possible function gain, but risk of severe headache side effects).
- *May help*: needling (short-term pain); LLLT (short-term pain at certain wavelengths); CST injections
  (short-term pain, no more than 1-2 injections); botox (upper extremity).
- *Unlikely/don't use*: PRP/ABI/prolotherapy (very conflicting; small-positive-to-no results; more
  promising for tendon tears than tendinopathy).
[CONVENTION] Given the sparse/conflicting evidence, some PTs recommend skipping non-conservative
adjuncts entirely; isometrics remain the gold standard for acute pain relief, and chronic pain is
unlikely to be resolved by these adjuncts — PNE paired with movement is judged more helpful long-term.
Use caution and seek second opinions before pursuing these (p.121).

**If conservative treatment (12+ months) AND non-conservative adjuncts fail → last resort: surgery**
(~75% success for Achilles; other sites more variable, side effects common) (p.121).

## Appendix cross-reference: rehab program parameters (pp.121-124)

[CONVENTION] The chapter's appendix restates Ch.6's rehab-session structure and dosing as a summary —
included here only for completeness since it falls in this page range; the authoritative detail lives in
Ch.6:
- Session order: warm-up/mobility → skilled techniques (manual therapy, neuromuscular training) → pain
  management (isometrics, heat, analgesics) → targeted eccentric-concentric exercise → peripheral
  strength/kinetic-chain work → flexibility/mobility follow-up (p.121-122).
- Eccentric-concentric dosing: **6-12 sets × 10-15 reps every 2 days** (≈60-180 reps per session, ≈21-42
  sets/week); frequency starts at **3×/week**, adjusted per patient tolerance — lower frequency aids
  compliance and may synergize with collagen-synthesis timing and highly irritable tendons (p.122).
- Tempo: initial-phase eccentric **2-3s**, concentric **1-2s** (≈3010/2010/3020/2020 tempo notation),
  progressing toward faster tempos as rehab advances toward sport-specific plyometric demands (p.122).
- Alternative protocol: 1-2 exercises × 3 sets × 30-50 reps/set, 2-3s eccentric/1s concentric, staying
  3-5 reps short of failure to reduce reinjury risk; progress by 1-3 reps/session; on hitting 50 reps,
  increase load and drop back to 30 reps, then work back down through 25/20/15/10-rep ranges as load
  increases (p.122).
- Rehab stages: reduce pain/irritability → improve strength → improve functional strength → increase
  power → develop stretch-shortening-cycle return to sport (p.122).
- Isometric pain-reduction dosing (pain/irritability stage): **3-4 minutes total tension at 70-80% MVIC**,
  across 3-24 sets, with 1-minute or 45s sets flagged as least tedious; repeatable multiple times/day
  (p.122-123).
- [SCIENCE] Acute:Chronic Workload Ratio (ACWR) and Heart Rate Variability are flagged as having
  limited-but-positive evidence for injury/reinjury prevention, applicable to rehab as well as healthy
  training (p.124).
