## Collagen synthesis and tendon loading frequency, intensity, volume (p.21-23)

- [SCIENCE] Magnusson et al's schematic: net collagen synthesis is **negative for roughly the first ~30 hours after exercise** because collagen degradation outpaces synthesis in that window; formation only overtakes breakdown after that (p.21). Mechanistic implication (not stated as a hard rule but the direct inference the book draws): loading the same tendon again inside that ~30-hour window compounds net breakdown rather than adaptation — this is the basis for spacing rehab sessions on a reactive/degenerative tendon (p.21-22).
- [SCIENCE] Magnusson et al's second chart shows a **ceiling effect on collagen synthesis vs. loading volume**: 10x10 knee extensions at 70% 1RM produced a collagen-synthesis response (~0.09%/h) nearly identical to 36 km of running (~0.09%/h), and both were similar to 1 hour of unresisted maximal knee kicking (~0.08-0.09%/h), vs. baseline ~0.05%/h at zero loading reps (p.22). [CONVENTION] Practical inference: a program with "sufficient intensity and volume" can maximize collagen synthesis without extreme time commitment (e.g., without needing an hour of low-load kicking or tens of km of running) (p.22).
- [SCIENCE] Common Achilles loading protocols (Alfredson, Stanish & Curwin, Silbernagel) use **daily or even twice-daily** loading successfully; this is tolerated specifically because their **volume is low enough** to offset the high frequency — frequency and volume trade off against each other rather than each being independently "correct" (p.22).
- [SCIENCE/DISPUTED] Some evidence (cited generally, no specific number given) suggests structural/imaging changes in the tendon do **not** explain therapeutic improvement from exercise, except possibly with heavy-slow resistance (HSR) (p.22-23). [CONVENTION] Low's interpretation: benefit likely comes mainly from **removing the aggravating exercise** (the specific movement/intensity/volume/frequency combination that caused the injury) and reintroducing proper loading, not from actively "fixing" collagen structure — degenerative portions of a tendon can become asymptomatic even if imaging still shows degeneration (p.23, consistent with the Docking & Cook continuum model from earlier chapters). Practical takeaway: optimizing purely for collagen synthesis is not necessary to restore performance/reduce pain, though it's not harmful either — worth a small role in a program, not the central organizing principle (p.23).

## Eccentrics vs. concentric-eccentric training (p.23-24)

- [CONVENTION] Definitions: eccentric = muscle-lengthening phase (lowering in a push-up/pull-up); concentric = muscle-shortening/contraction phase (pushing up, pulling chin to bar) (p.23).
- [SCIENCE] Early Achilles tendinopathy research used **isolated eccentric-only** training (e.g., slow lowering from a calf raise, skipping the concentric) and it proved effective, leading to a common belief that rehab should be eccentric-only (p.23).
- [DISPUTED] Low rejects eccentric-only as the default: if the goal is return to sport/daily activity, concentric strength is functionally necessary (p.23). Couppé et al: isolated eccentric loading is **likely no better** than combined eccentric-concentric loading, though evidence is limited (p.23) — [SCIENCE, directly quoted] "There is no convincing clinical evidence to demonstrate that isolated eccentric loading exercise improves clinical outcomes more than other loading therapies," attributing ambiguity partly to inconsistent reporting of load magnitude, movement speed, and inter-session recovery across studies (p.23).
- [SCIENCE] Stasinopoulos et al (RCTs, lateral elbow tendinopathy): a combination of eccentric + concentric + **isometric** contractions was the most effective treatment (quoted directly), though the authors call for more confirmatory studies (p.24).
- [SCIENCE] Bohm et al: tendon adaptation may depend primarily on **loading intensity**, not contraction type — meaning "eccentric" per se may not be the active ingredient; what matters is whether the tendon receives sufficient load, and a longer eccentric contraction may simply have supplied more time-under-tension/intensity than earlier protocols realized (p.24). Corroborating detail: plyometric loading gave mixed results, plausibly because "short loading duration during jumping constrains the transduction of the mechanical stimulus" — i.e., too brief a loading period for adaptation, a pattern that appears to hold at least for isometric contractions too (p.24).
- [CONVENTION] Low's synthesis: a **controlled eccentric-concentric repetition** is likely the most effective way to maximize control and volume during rehab; as rehab progresses, speed up both the eccentric and concentric phases to build strength, power, and plyometric capacity, particularly when returning an athlete to sport (p.24).

## Programming, activity modifications, and analysis of current research (p.24-29)

### The four benchmark loading programs (2013 Malliaras systematic review) (p.24-25)
Table of the four most-cited tendinopathy loading programs, with the mechanism/tradeoff embedded in each column:

| Program | Type | Sets/reps | Frequency | Progression axis | Pain rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Alfredson** | Eccentric only | 3x15 | Twice daily | Increase load | Load enough to reach **up to moderate pain** |
| **Stanish & Curwin** | Eccentric-concentric, power | 3x10-15 | Daily | Speed first, then load | Load enough to be **painful in the 3rd set** |
| **Silbernagel combined** | Eccentric-concentric, eccentric, faster-eccentric | Various | Daily | Volume, then type of exercise | Acceptable **if within defined limits** (patient-specific pain-monitoring model) |
| **HSR (heavy-slow resistance)** | Eccentric-concentric | 4x15→6 | 3x/week | 15RM→6RM (i.e., load increases as rep max drops) | Acceptable **if pain not worse after** the session |

(p.25) — All four differ markedly in frequency, load, and pain tolerance philosophy, illustrating there is no single validated protocol; each embeds a distinct hypothesis about what drives adaptation (load magnitude vs. speed vs. volume vs. RM-based progressive overload).

### Silbernagel combined loading program — full detail example (p.25-26)
[CONVENTION/SCIENCE — worked clinical example] Four phases, each with explicit patient-status gates and daily-or-near-daily frequency, illustrating how a real program encodes progression + pain criteria together rather than as a fixed formula:

- **Phase 1 (weeks 1-2).** Goal: begin exercising, learn the pain-monitoring model. Entry status: pain/difficulty with all activities, cannot complete ten one-legged heel raises. Daily: circulation drills (ankle pumps) + 3x10-15 two-legged floor heel raises + 3x10 one-legged floor heel raises + 3x10 seated heel raises + 3x10 eccentric floor heel raises (p.25).
- **Phase 2 (weeks 2-5).** Goal: start strengthening. Entry status: pain with exercise, morning stiffness, pain on heel raises. Daily, same lifts moved to a **stair edge** (greater ROM/load) at 3x15, plus 3x20 quick-rebounding heel raises added (p.25).
- **Phase 3 (weeks 3-12+).** Goal: heavier strength work, resume running/jumping. Entry status: tolerated phase 2, no distal tendon-insertion pain, stiffness may be up or down. Daily bodyweight work **plus heavier load 2-3x/week**: 3x15 one-legged stair heel raises with added weight, 3x10 seated heel raises, 3x15 eccentric stair heel raises with added weight, 3x20 quick-rebounding, plus plyometrics introduced (p.25).
- **Phase 4 (weeks 12-6 months+).** Goal: maintenance, no symptoms. Entry status: minimal symptoms, stiffness not daily, sport participation without difficulty. Frequency **drops to 2-3x/week**: 3x15 weighted one-legged stair heel raises, 3x15 weighted eccentric stair heel raises, 3x20 quick-rebounding (p.25-26).
- Structural pattern worth generalizing: frequency is highest (daily) in early, low-load/high-tolerance phases and only tapers to 2-3x/week once symptoms are near-resolved and load per session is much higher — i.e., frequency and per-session load trade off across the whole timeline, not just within one protocol (p.25-26).

### Five critiques of tendinopathy research, as identified by the book (p.26-29)

1. **Limited, disparate research — can't isolate what variables matter (p.26).** Few training-program studies exist, and the ones that do use incompatible protocols, so it's impossible to attribute results to any single parameter change. Example: only HSR uses a 3x/week frequency, yet [SCIENCE] net collagen synthesis is only positive after roughly 30-36 hours (consistent with the p.21 figure) — so it's unclear whether Alfredson/Stanish-Curwin/Silbernagel would perform as well or better at 3x/week vs. their studied daily/2x-daily frequencies. [SCIENCE] Contrast with hypertrophy literature: Schoenfeld et al's volume-matched meta-analysis found 2x and 3x/week superior to 1x/week (p.26); Dankel et al found similarly but noted higher-frequency benefit for rehab specifically is still uninvestigated and may be harder to implement (p.26-27); McMaster et al's non-volume-matched review found 3x/week superior to both 2x and 4x/week for general strength training (p.27). [SCIENCE/DISPUTED] No tendinopathy study has run a volume-matched or non-volume-matched frequency comparison — the high-frequency regimens common in tendinopathy rehab are explicitly "uncharted territory" versus lower-frequency alternatives (p.27).

2. **Populations are not distinguished (p.27).** Tendinopathy studies rarely separate sedentary/occupational-overuse patients from athletes, unlike strength/hypertrophy literature, which limits generalizability — the loading workarounds appropriate for a sedentary patient differ substantially from those for an athlete (p.27). [SCIENCE] For contrast, the 2009 ACSM progression-model guidelines for healthy adults give population-specific numbers entirely absent from tendinopathy research: novices → 8-12RM; intermediate/advanced → periodized 1-12RM with eventual 1-6RM emphasis, 3-5 min rest, 1-2s concentric/1-2s eccentric tempo, load increased 2-10% once 1-2 reps over target are achievable; training frequency 2-3 d/wk (novice), 3-4 d/wk (intermediate), 4-5 d/wk (advanced); hypertrophy work favors 6-12RM at 1-2 min rest; power training uses either strength work or light loads (0-60% 1RM lower body, 30-60% 1RM upper body) at fast velocity with 3-5 min rest, 3-5 sets, multi-joint/total-body emphasis; local muscular endurance uses 40-60% 1RM for >15 reps with <90s rest (p.27-28). The tendinopathy literature has no comparable granularity (p.28).

3. **Studies don't distinguish irritability stage or offer alternate progressions (p.28).** Most loading programs modify only for pain, not for tendinopathy stage (reactive vs. degenerative etc.), and rarely branch for patients who respond unusually well or poorly — an issue the book compares to generic beginner strength programs (Starting Strength, StrongLifts 5x5) needing individualized coaching adjustments (p.28). Under-addressed factors: risk factors for tendinopathy, technique/form during the causative exercise, and patient compliance (p.28). [SCIENCE] A 2016 Stanisopolous & Malliaras review found hip-extensor weakness prevalent in patellar tendinopathy, and poor lumbopelvic control can alter knee mechanics via the gluteal role in knee rotation control — implying kinetic-chain (not just local) weaknesses need addressing (p.28). [SCIENCE] Beyer et al found eccentric training and HSR clinically similar, but patient satisfaction and compliance were higher with HSR in the short term (long-term difference not statistically significant) — a reminder that program adherence, not just theoretical efficacy, determines outcomes (p.28).

4. **Address deficits beyond the local tendon — proximal and distal kinetic chain (p.28-29).** Four deficit categories to screen: (a) range-of-motion deficits, (b) weakness/endurance deficits especially for sport-specific or repetitive movements, (c) instability or poor movement control, (d) pain-driven compensatory movement patterns (p.28). [CONVENTION] Practical modification example: fixed hand-position pull-ups/chin-ups can aggravate golfer's elbow, distal biceps tendinopathy, or rotator-cuff tendinopathy because the fixed grip locks the elbow/shoulder into one motion path; switching to gymnastics rings (free hand rotation) often reduces or eliminates symptoms while preserving training continuity (p.29). Same logic applies to fixed-path barbell lifts (squat, bench press) — switching to dumbbells or unilateral variations can mitigate aggravation for lifters/RSI patients without stopping training altogether (p.29). Rationale stated explicitly: exercise modification reduces irritability so rehab proceeds more consistently, avoiding setbacks that can cost weeks or months of progress (p.29).

5. **Malliaras review's clinical recommendations, despite the above limitations (p.29).** [SCIENCE, quoted] Clinicians should consider eccentric-concentric loading alongside or instead of isolated eccentric loading in Achilles/patellar tendinopathy, particularly for patients with marked concentric weakness who may not respond to eccentric-only work (contraction-type specificity) — the Silbernagel combined program is called out as well-suited to this subgroup. Heavy-load approaches (HSR or load-maximized eccentric loading) may better achieve tendon adaptation and suit less-irritable/degenerative or high-load-demand (athlete) populations (p.29). The review calls for future research to examine how load intensity, time under tension, speed, and contraction type causally drive outcomes, rather than assuming eccentric-only is a gold standard, and flags that tendons at different points on the reactive-degenerative spectrum may respond very differently to the same loading intervention (p.29).
   [CONVENTION] Low's overall read: Silbernagel combined loading and HSR offer the most nuanced, staged programming logic compared to the simpler Alfredson and Stanish & Curwin protocols (p.29).
   [SCIENCE, quoted, Lim et al 2018 systematic review] "Findings from isometric exercises can be trusted to guide clinical practice (Grade A), whereas eccentric exercises can be trusted to guide clinical practice in most clinical situations (Grade B). It is recommended that HSR exercises be applied carefully to individual clinical circumstances (Grade C)." Isometrics appear more effective for short-term in-season pain relief; HSR/eccentric work is more suitable for long-term pain reduction and functional improvement (p.29).
   [CONVENTION] Bottom line stated by the book: practitioners must educate themselves on which variables matter for program selection and must be willing to modify loading programs based on individual patient presentation and response, since no single protocol is validated as universally superior (p.29).
